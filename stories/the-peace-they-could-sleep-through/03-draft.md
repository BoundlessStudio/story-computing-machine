# The Peace They Could Sleep Through

At nine in the morning, my god decided the terrace farmers owned next year's
seed beyond the reach of debt.

At nine and three minutes, my god decided the lenders could seize it.

My name was Lumen Mott. In the ordinary course of my office, I kept my god
from contradicting itself in public.

Both judgments arrived in the same blue file. Both bore the private seal of the
Sleepless Majesty. Both began with the phrase I had spent eleven years
perfecting: *Having heard every need, We answer without favor.*

I read them twice while the clock above my desk removed seven minutes from the
time remaining before terrace companies with farm tools reached the provincial
storehouses and house guards closed the city depots against their shipments.

Across from me, Ludo Dask gripped a seed sack between both knees. It held one
district's last clean planting grain, brought as evidence and handled as if it
were an infant. Beside him, Gelda Parn had opened an import ledger whose red
columns represented three shiploads purchased during two failed harvests.

"Well?" Ludo asked.

"The Sleepless Majesty will protect lawful obligation," Gelda said. "There is
no uncertainty."

That was the trouble with an infallible monarch. Uncertainty had to hide in the
clerical staff.

Ludo's districts could not surrender their seed or the tenancy land pledged
against royal loans. Gelda's milling and importing houses could not absorb a
total cancellation without ruining the credit that fed the cities. Both needs
were real. Both delegations had turned them into sacred absolutes. The terrace
companies had promised to occupy the storehouses if I ruled against them. The
house guards had promised to close the depots and detain terrace shipments if I
ruled the other way.

Neither petition offered a concession. I had removed those during preparation.
Undignified bargaining positions confused the question presented to the
Majesty.

There was an old alternative at the back of the procedure book. A common plea
allowed parties to end a case by filing their own agreement before royal
judgment. I had considered it for half a breath, then dismissed it. Common
pleas settled fence lines and disputed apprenticeships. This case concerned
famine, national credit, and perhaps civil conflict. It required a perfect
answer.

I closed the blue file.

"I need clarification," I said.

Gelda looked offended on behalf of divine competence. Ludo looked offended on
principle.

"Wait here."

I took both judgments and went to ask my god which one my god meant.

* * *

The private consultation room had one public door, never opened while a
petitioner waited, and a service door for clerks carrying tea and regrettable
facts. I had used the latter for years. That morning, nobody answered my knock.

Inside, twelve identical figures wearing the face of the Sleepless Majesty were
arguing.

They had the same smooth brown face, the same close silver curls, the same
androgynous body in the same loose gray robe. One stood on the table. Three had
filled the window seat. Two were correcting each other's corrections. Each
held an annotated copy of the grain case.

"Seizing seed preserves contract law by eliminating anyone available to honor
contracts," said the figure on the table.

"Cancellation preserves agriculture by ensuring nobody will finance it
again," replied one at the window.

"We might purchase both claims."

"With whose grain?"

"This is why I requested eighty uninterrupted years before we attempted
credit."

I dropped the blue file.

Twelve identical faces turned toward me.

The one nearest the service door said, "Adjudicator Mott."

"Your Majesty," I said automatically.

"Yes," said all twelve.

The nearest winced. "That is going to complicate the explanation."

The change began with breath. Each human outline expanded on an exhalation, as
if the body had been a garment held taut from within. Gray robes slipped from
shoulders becoming scaled, furred, ridged, or plated. Twelve long necks
uncoiled beneath a ceiling built with suspiciously generous vaults. Claws
clicked on the tiled floor. Wings folded with varying degrees of etiquette.

They were twelve distinct dragons.

One had pearl scales and a broad, blunt muzzle. One was lean and violet, with
translucent spines pressed carefully flat beneath the ceiling. Two were shades
of green but differed in every other proportion. A bronze dragon was small
enough to remain on the table and looked pleased about it. The largest was
gray, soft-frilled, and still wearing the Majesty's silver curls around one
horn.

The dragon nearest me was deep blue, with pale markings around the eyes that
made exhaustion look ceremonial.

"My individual name is Varkesh," the dragon said. "I am the current public
wearer."

"Current."

Varkesh nudged a narrow volume from beneath the discarded robes. It was a
rotation ledger: dates, hearings, journeys, illnesses, and the private mark of
the dragon assigned to each appearance. Twelve marks recurred across two
centuries. On the table lay twelve versions of the grain ruling in twelve
hands.

"The guise is a shape each of us makes while awake," Varkesh said. "It has no
mind of its own. Only one of us wears it in public. Today we all assumed it
privately so we could compare interpretations."

"You share the Sleepless Majesty."

"We constitute the Sleepless Majesty," Varkesh said. "Deliberately. We remain
twelve persons."

My knees found a chair.

The evidence did not rest on two contradictory pages. It breathed around me,
crowded the furniture, and complained when the bronze dragon put a claw through
the table.

"No one else knows?" I asked.

"Several people knew," Varkesh said. "They died at ordinary human intervals.
This has been inconvenient for continuity."

"Two hundred years."

"We were hoping for longer."

* * *

They wanted to sleep.

Not for an afternoon, nor even for the indulgent fortnight granted to senior
clerks after a nervous collapse. The twelve required centuries of deep,
uninterrupted sleep in an old refuge far from inhabited roads. War drums,
collapsing trade, mass hunger, and disputed successions carried far enough to
wake them.

"So you became our monarch," I said.

"Successions had woken us four times in a century," Varkesh replied. "We
removed succession."

The dragons had also ended three cycles of conquest, established grain
reserves, made judgments consistent across districts, and built roads that
turned a bad harvest from a death sentence into an expensive year. My
grandmother had been born during the last levy war. She had described soldiers
taking doors for shields and children hiding from both uniforms. My generation
knew such things as archived testimony.

The gratitude was not foolish. Neither was the worship.

People prayed directly to the living Sleepless Majesty. The twelve supplied
the power behind its impossible continuity and shared guise. Together they
accepted worship, exercised final authority, and acted as one sovereign.
Whatever else they were individually, the collective they had made was a god.

"You did save us," I said.

"Yes," said the pearl-scaled dragon.

"You deceived us."

"Also yes," said another.

Varkesh lowered onto the floor until one pale-marked eye met mine. "We meant
to leave you a stable administration and go. Each useful judgment produced
more petitions. Each answered petition became evidence that no human answer
should be trusted until we improved it."

Against the far wall stood two centuries of ordinary case files. Grain
releases, loan schedules, road accounts, and district disputes sat in the same
clerk-tied bundles used in every hearing office. Most pages carried human
initials. Clerks arranged deliveries, audited measures, and performed the daily
work of government without a dragon entering the room. But whenever interested
parties refused a compromise, the file acquired a blue cover and climbed
toward one final royal answer.

"The clerks know the work," Varkesh said. "The difficulty is that no one
accepts their work once interests collide."

The grain case had divided them. Enforce every debt, and the terrace districts
lost the means to plant. Cancel every debt, and importers would refuse the next
risk or charge for it ruinously. Purchase both sides, and the royal reserve
could not cover another failed harvest. Set a permanent repayment share, and
no one could agree which future counted as normal.

Varkesh pushed the two sealed judgments toward me.

"Select one," the dragon said. "Refine it. You know how to make us sound like
one timeless mind."

The compliment had built my career. Courtiers praised how cleanly I removed
hesitation, trade, and human partiality from draft rulings. I had believed I
was translating mortal confusion into divine clarity.

"How much of the Majesty's voice was mine?" I asked.

"Enough that we are asking you to choose our last answer."

"And then?"

"We will enforce it, leave ordinary administration to the people already
performing it, surrender the guise to history, and sleep."

"For how long?"

"Three centuries, if no one begins a war over grain."

Every dragon looked at me as though human disagreement were an error I had
introduced.

* * *

I tried my old method once more.

Ludo and Gelda sat on opposite sides of my hearing room. I took the raised
chair between them, where the carved back suggested that the Sleepless Majesty
listened through its servants.

"What loss could your districts tolerate?" I asked Ludo.

He looked past me toward the empty royal alcove. "None, if the Majesty is just."

"Could repayment come from surplus after the next harvest?"

"To admit repayment is to admit seizure was lawful."

Gelda straightened. "At last, a true statement."

"What could the importing houses defer?" I asked her.

"Nothing the Majesty guaranteed."

"Could principal remain while collection paused?"

"To pause collection announces that contracts hold only when convenient."

They were not speaking to each other. They were performing certainty upward.
I had arranged the chairs, asked the questions, and promised a verdict. Even my
attempt at bargaining sounded like another examination for a hidden ruler.

A clerk entered with two folded messages.

The terrace companies had begun moving toward the provincial storehouses.
House guards were closing city depots and turning back wagons while they
awaited judgment. Each side believed that taking position before noon would
help the Majesty protect the righteous.

Ludo went pale. Gelda pressed a thumb hard into the import ledger's spine.
Neither withdrew a threat.

I imagined reading the seed judgment. The house guards might stand down today,
because our god had spoken. Tomorrow, every closed depot would become a defense
of sacred contract against an inexplicable divine betrayal.

I imagined reading the debt judgment. The terrace companies might leave the
storehouses today. At the next seizure, their only remaining appeal would be
to defy the law and hope history judged them more mercifully than their god
had.

A perfect answer did not remove conflict. It removed every legitimate way to
change the answer.

"The judgment will be given at noon," I said.

Ludo touched the seed sack. Gelda closed her ledger. They left by separate
doors.

I pulled the procedure book from my lowest shelf and opened it to the common
plea.

The rule had six lines.

For the first time that morning, brevity looked honest.

* * *

The Hall of Petitions was full before noon.

Terrace families packed one gallery. Mill workers, import clerks, and household
guards filled the other. At the center waited Ludo's seed sack and Gelda's
ledger. Above my chair, the royal alcove stood open.

The Sleepless Majesty entered in gray robes.

Varkesh wore the shared face perfectly. The crowd knelt as one body. I knelt
with them from habit, love, and fear.

"We ask," the hall recited.

"You answer."

Varkesh placed a sealed judgment before me. "Read, Adjudicator Mott."

It was a third formula. Temporary royal purchase, compulsory quotas, armed
supervision, and a schedule of repayment comprehensive enough to remain wrong
for decades. In a low voice, Varkesh said, "We will stay awake until compliance
is secure."

The offer was kind. It was terrible. It would prevent the noon confrontation
and preserve the exact dependence that had caused it.

I stood.

"I will not read this judgment."

The prayer's final word seemed to remain in the air after every mouth had
closed.

"For eleven years," I said, "I have drafted the Majesty's rulings. I removed
doubt. I removed bargaining. I made twelve different judgments sound as if one
perfect mind had spoken."

Varkesh's human face did not move.

"I ask the Sleepless Majesty to appear in full."

The eleven side doors opened.

Eleven more copies of the same gray-robed monarch entered. The hall recoiled
before anyone understood why. I set the rotation ledger beside the grain
evidence and read three entries aloud: dates, public acts, changing private
marks.

Then twelve living disguises ended.

Bodies lengthened. Wings unfolded. Tile cracked beneath an incautious claw.
The forms were pearl, violet, green, bronze, gray, blue, and other colors for
which my legal training had provided no useful terminology. They crowded the
hall in twelve breathing, unmistakably individual bodies.

Varkesh remained nearest my chair.

"We are twelve persons," the dragon said. "Together, we have been the
Sleepless Majesty. We built the peace so it would outlast our waking. We have
not found an answer to your grain dispute."

Some people fled. More knelt lower.

"Then command them to make peace," someone shouted from the terrace gallery.

"Order both sides to negotiate," came an answering cry.

The revelation had not ended worship. It had merely given our god twelve
visible bodies.

Varkesh looked at me. "We can issue that command."

"No."

A ripple of outrage crossed both galleries.

"If you order them to negotiate," I said, "they will still be negotiating
with you."

I took the sealed formula, broke its blank fastening cord, and laid the empty
back page on the table.

"Ludo Dask. Gelda Parn. There will be no royal judgment. If you want an
agreement, file a common plea."

"Now?" Gelda asked.

"Before the storehouses are occupied and the depots close completely."

Ludo stared at the twelve dragons. "Command us to sign."

Varkesh drew breath.

"Do not," I said.

For two centuries, the Sleepless Majesty had answered. That noon, twelve
dragons chose silence.

* * *

I moved my high chair away from the table.

Ludo put the seed sack between himself and Gelda. Gelda placed the import
ledger beside it. Neither sat.

"State what your people cannot survive," I said.

"Seed seizure," Ludo answered. "Not after two failed harvests. If we surrender
clean seed, we plant poor grain or nothing. If tenancy land passes to the
houses, families become laborers on fields they held before the loans."

Gelda's reply came quickly, but not upward. She spoke to him. "Our contracts
financed that seed and the imports that fed your districts last winter. If
principal vanishes, no house can borrow for the next shipment. City workers
pay first when grain stops moving."

"What loss can you bear?" I asked.

Neither answered.

From beyond the hall came the noise of a frightened crowd learning that its god
had no verdict. Noon passed.

Ludo loosened the seed sack's tie. "We could repay from surplus. Not from seed.
Not from land before harvest."

Gelda opened her ledger. "Future surplus at what share?"

"A share people can live after paying."

"That is not a number."

"Neither is a harvest."

It was the first useful exchange they had ever had.

They argued over a cap. Gelda demanded published district inventories; Ludo
demanded published import costs. Gelda offered to release depot grain at a
margin subject to audit if the terrace companies withdrew from the
storehouses. Ludo would order withdrawal if the houses stopped detaining
wagons. Each accused the other of disguising theft as necessity. Each was
occasionally correct.

I wrote nothing.

When they reached language, Gelda drafted the first clause in her own hand:
seed stores and pledged tenancy land would be protected from seizure until the
next harvest. Ludo drafted the second: principal would not be canceled, but
repayment would be capped and drawn only from future surplus.

They wrote that all grain inventories, import costs, and depot margins would be
opened for inspection. City depots would resume release at the audited margin.
Terrace districts would withdraw from the provincial storehouses. Detained
shipments would proceed.

"How long?" I asked.

"Until the next harvest," Ludo said.

Gelda shook her head. "If the count is wrong, that locks us into shortage."

"Ninety days," she said.

"Too short."

"Long enough for open stock counts in every district. Renew it then, amend it,
or let it expire."

Ludo looked toward his gallery. His supporters had been promised divine
vindication. Gelda's had been promised eternal contract. Signing would make
each merely accountable.

"Ninety days," Ludo said.

They added the expiry in a cramped final paragraph. No dragon supplied a word.
No royal seal touched the page.

Ludo Dask signed for the terrace districts. Gelda Parn signed for the city
houses. I signed only as witness and entered the plea under the old six-line
rule.

That did not stop the armed groups.

Ludo wrote a second message: *Hold the road. Do not enter the storehouses. The
seed and land are protected for ninety days while we count.*

Gelda wrote hers: *Open the depots. Release the wagons. Make no detention under
the disputed claims.*

They chose their own runners from their own galleries. The messages left
through opposite doors.

We waited.

The twelve dragons waited worst. One wore a groove in the floor with a claw.
Another counted ceiling panels. A smaller dragon unfolded and refolded
its wings. Varkesh watched the empty doors.

"The messages may fail," Varkesh said.

"Yes."

"Both groups may continue."

"Then Ludo and Gelda will answer for the orders they gave, and the rest of us
will decide what comes next."

Varkesh glanced toward the doors. "We would have compelled them."

"That is why you are waiting."

The first runner returned from the city. Depot doors were open. The wagons were
moving.

The second returned from the provincial road. The terrace companies had
stopped short of the storehouses. They would remain assembled until they saw
the protected-seed clause copied in full.

Gelda ordered copies made.

It was not trust. It was enough material interest, written concession, and
human authority to make the next step less dangerous than the last.

The hall began the old response without anyone leading it.

"We ask—"

Voices faltered, waiting.

Varkesh and the other eleven did not answer.

At length, Ludo held up the common plea. Gelda raised the import ledger beside
it.

People began reading.

* * *

The ninety-day articles raised the city price of flour, lowered several debt
payments, and satisfied nobody who had promised perfection. On the seventeenth
day, open stock counts showed one district had less grain than both sides had
assumed. Ludo and Gelda amended the release schedule after a meeting that
lasted fourteen hours and included no revelation whatsoever.

The dragons disclosed the rotation ledger and the false continuity of the
Sleepless Majesty. In their final public act, all twelve renounced royal and
religious authority. Human clerks struck the royal-answer line from every open
docket, and district delegates recognized no dragon's word as judgment. Some
people continued to pray privately, but power and worship without authority no
longer made the collective sovereign a god.

They retained no veto. They appointed no hidden successor and required no
human office to watch for their waking. On the morning they left, all twelve
came to the Hall of Petitions in their own bodies. The shared face did not
appear again.

No escort waited. The dragons carried no decree, kept no messenger, and asked
no one to follow. At a movement among them too small for me to identify, twelve
pairs of wings opened. Their first attempt at a formation required several
midair corrections. Then they departed together toward their old refuge
without circling back.

Three days later, travelers from the last inhabited road reported twelve
rumbling voices disputing the local acoustics after dusk. Before dawn, every
voice stopped. The silence continued. We established no office to listen for a
second night.

My royal title ended with their authority. The temporary docket had several
recallable chairs, of which I occupied one when the others tolerated me. Every
petition had to state a proposed concession. After one year, the districts
could keep the procedure, replace it, or discard it.

At my third hearing, two millers and six villages argued over transport fees.
They interrupted one another, miscounted a shipment, revised a proposal, and
asked me three times to decide for them.

"What can each of you bear to lose?" I asked.

The hearing continued untidily into supper.

Far from our voices, twelve dragons slept through the peace we had not finished
making.
