title: A Penny to Boo

On my day off I meant to go into the city and tell Prince Cader exactly what I thought of him. For a penny I could get a seat at the puppet show in Saffron Square, close enough to do it without raising my voice. The prince had fallen off his horse in public that summer. The puppet had fallen off his every week since.

I'd wound my red scarf twice around my throat and got as far as the lower gate when somebody called my name. Prince Cader was coming down the steps two at a time. His copper hair had a flat place above one ear. One of his bootlaces was loose.

“It's my day off, sir,” I said. He could have found five other maids between his room and the head of the stairs. I wondered what he'd broken.

“I know. Where are you going?”

“Into town.”

He stopped beside me and took a moment to get his breath. “Can I come?”

I looked up the steps for whoever generally came out with him. There was just a leaf blowing back and forth across the top one. If the prince wanted a maid to help him with something in town, it was a very roundabout way of asking.

“As far as the square,” I said. I'd find out soon enough what he was after.

The gatekeeper started to open the gate and put a hand out to stop me. “Run and get His Highness's cloak first, Rilla. It'll be cold down there.”

“I've got the day off,” I said.

“It'll only take a minute.”

“We're on a date,” said the prince.

The gatekeeper took his hand away. He looked at me, then at the prince, and muttered that he begged my pardon. It was the first time I'd ever got out of going back for a cloak with both feet still pointing towards the gate. I took advantage of it. Cader caught up to me halfway down the hill.

“Does that work with all the gatekeepers?” I asked.

“I've only tried it with that one.” He looked terribly pleased with himself. I could see why. I'd have been pleased too if I'd found a word that opened a gate.

He followed me down the narrow steps between the dyer's wall and the backs of the houses. I was wearing shoes I could run in for once and went down fast enough that he had to turn sideways to get his feet on the treads. At the bottom there was a smell of hot currants. I stopped, and he walked into my elbow.

“I always stop here,” I said.

“I know now.” He backed up about an inch.

The bun woman had a board out over the street with twelve currant buns on it. There was usually one where the sugar had bubbled up black at the edge. I had just found it when she saw who was standing at my shoulder and reached under her board for the palace ledger.

“How many do they want up there today, love?”

“We're on a date,” said Cader. He'd had the whole hill to think of something else. He'd used it to get better at saying it.

The woman laid the ledger down. “Are you now.” She took the bun I pointed to, reached for a second, and started to fold the paper around the pair of them.

“Two lots,” I said. “He can get his own crumbs on himself.”

“I'd like that one, please.” He pointed to a bun with a dark edge almost as good as mine. The woman grinned at me as if she'd just seen us choose names for our children.

I paid for mine. Cader put down his penny and bit into his bun as soon as she handed it over. He'd got himself very nearly as far as the burnt sugar when a woman at the next stall leaned between two buckets of flowers to see him better.

“Guess who's on a date,” said the bun woman.

The flower woman caught the sleeve of a boy who'd just taken a bunch of carnations off her. She whispered something and he looked straight at us. A moment later he was halfway to the square.

“She'll tell everybody who buys a flower,” I said to Cader as we walked on.

“How many flowers does she sell?”

“It's a very good corner,” I said. I was trying to work out who he wanted to hear about us. The gatekeeper I'd understood, and even the bun woman if he'd got a taste for paying for his own things. There were people from the palace at both ends of the street on any given day. If he was trying to let somebody know he was spoken for, there were easier ways than catching up to me in those shoes.

At the square, a girl in a green apron was carrying two dining chairs out from behind the puppet booth. She set them side by side in front of the benches. From where I stood I could see that the leather on one seat had worn through and she'd put a little cushion over it. There were carnations in a jar at her feet. I thought someone must be getting married after the show.

“There's the pair of them,” a man said.

The girl looked up at us. “Is it true, then?”

“We're on a date,” said Cader. Somebody behind us whooped. The puppeteer came round the edge of his screen, one hand still inside a horse, and waved us towards the chairs. I stayed where I was. The bench I'd come for was right in front of the stage, where I could see up the horse's nose.

“We could give you the lovers' song,” the man said. “How would that suit Your Highness?” He glanced at me and lowered his voice to the one people used when they wanted advice from a maid. “Or you'd know what he'd like, miss.”

“I'd like the riding scene,” I said.

The man looked at Cader to see if I'd got it right. The gatekeeper's head had turned exactly the same way. You could have pulled them both with the same string. Cader opened his mouth. I took hold of his sleeve and walked him around the corner of the booth.

“Who are you trying to tell?” I asked.

He looked down at my hand, and I let go. “You, chiefly.”

“You could have started with me. I thought you wanted to walk into town.”

“I did. With you.” He picked a bit of sugar off his cuff. “Do you remember telling the girl with the long plait what my horse thought of the goose?”

I remembered doing it in the linen room. I'd put my head through a towel as if it were a bridle. It had taken me a month to get the voice where I wanted it, deep enough to come from a chest that size and cross enough for a horse that had carried princes all its life.

“A pillow with a grievance,” Cader said. He did it an octave too high, but it was my line.

“You heard that?”

“I stood in the passage. I wanted to hear what the horse said next, but you were both laughing too hard.” He looked at me properly then. “I saw you coming down the steps today and ran after you. I thought I might ask you on a date when I caught up.”

“How did that part go?”

“I asked if I could come. Then I told the gatekeeper. I liked saying it. And when I said it to him, he let you go.”

“I told him I was off. It was you he listened to.” I nodded towards the chairs. “So did that man out there.”

Cader leaned far enough around the corner to see the chairs. The girl was smoothing down the cushion. People were waiting in a little semicircle with an opening where we were supposed to come through.

“I can go back up the hill,” he said.

Through the canvas I could hear the knock of the horse's wooden feet as the puppeteer put it away. I'd been saving up a speech for it about getting paid the same amount whether it brought its rider back or left him in a ditch. I had half a mind to see what Cader would make of that.

“I'm going to buy a seat and boo you,” I said. “You can get a seat beside me if you like.”

“How much is a seat?”

“A penny. Tie your boot first or you'll be giving them the riding scene for free.”

I put my penny down in front of the girl in the apron. Cader came up beside me a moment later and put down his own. When I went round the good chairs to sit on the bench, he sat so close that I could feel him trying to make room for me and failing. It was the narrowest bit of the bench. It had the best view.

The cloth went up. Out came the puppet prince, in a fine embroidered coat with a crown sewn straight onto his head. A second puppet came up beside him with a sprig of silk roses. The man behind the screen drew a breath to sing.

“Your Highness,” I called in my horse's voice. “Next time you want to ride out with a goose, get it to carry you home.”

A boy laughed, high and sudden, and hid his face in his mother's sleeve. The puppet prince turned his head very slowly until he was looking at Cader. I was about to ask for my wages when a noise like a rusty hinge came from the bench beside me. Cader had put one hand up like a long neck with a beak at the end of it.

“I'd have left him alone,” said the goose, “if he hadn't stuck a currant bun in his pocket.”

I turned to look at him. “You took a bun on parade?”

His hand swiveled to face me. “I took a bun *off* a parade.”

He held the beak open for one more solemn instant, and I lost the rest of my speech. I could see it all. Cader at the head of the parade, sitting up as straight as he did for a portrait, with breakfast sticking out of his coat. The goose must have thought it was its birthday.

“Do you take commissions?” I asked.

“What are you offering?”

“One bun if you get him off my back. Two if he lands in the fountain.”

Cader leaned his goose close to me. “Can he swim?”

“It's a very shallow fountain,” I said.

At that the mother laughed too. The roses went down out of sight. Up came the horse, and the boy came out of his mother's sleeve long enough to shout that His Highness should look behind him. Of course His Highness was too busy waving. The goose came out from under the horse and seized the puppet prince by the pocket. Someone on the back bench yelled, “The fountain!” The prince went sailing into a tin basin that somebody behind the cloth hit with a spoon. The whole square cheered. For a while it was all I could do to stay on the bench. Every time I got hold of myself Cader would lift a finger to his mouth like a man who knew the terrible punishment for laughing at a prince, and then he'd go again.

After the show, he stood by the end of the bench while I got my scarf back round my neck. The girl in the apron stacked the two dining chairs. Cader started to step aside as if he meant to let me go ahead without him.

“I'm going back for a bun,” I said. “Want to split one?”

“I'd like to.” He said it quite softly. We had to go single file out of the square between two boys taking turns falling off an imaginary horse. When we got to the bun stall he was still right behind me.

The woman lifted the corner of the cloth over her board. “Separate again, love?”

“One will do. We're on a date.”

Cader looked up from counting his pennies. The woman had saved me one with a good black edge, the kind you could snap like toffee. I broke it in the middle and gave him half of the burnt part. We ate it standing close enough to hear each other crack the sugar.
