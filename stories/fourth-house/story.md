# The Fourth House

Delka Por lifted the baby from her mother's chest because a true source-reading began with one living body alone.

The child was slick and startlingly light in her hands. She had opened her eyes but made no cry. A breath moved through her, stopped where it should have gathered strength, and came again as a shallow sip.

"Emri," Yune Aster said from the low birth bed. She sounded as if the name had been waiting behind her teeth through all the hours of labor. "Her name is Emri."

"I have her." Delka tucked the warmed linen around the baby's sides and carried her three steps to the assessment table.

Steam clouded the lower panes of the room's only window. The brazier had made the air too hot for Delka's wool sleeves, exactly as it ought to be. On the bed, Yune lay propped against rolled blankets while the birth attendant worked beneath a clean sheet, speaking in the soft, practical voice of someone with no time for fear. Nothing in the room was quiet except the child.

Delka turned Emri's head, wiped her mouth, and rubbed a folded cloth along the narrow back. The baby drew another breath. Her ribs fluttered under the skin. Delka counted the pause, rubbed one heel, and saw the small mouth darken before the next breath came.

The source kit waited open at Delka's elbow: three hollows in a slab of white clay, three instruments laid in wool. She ignored it. A source could wait. Air could not.

She worked through the ordinary measures first, warmth and position and patient stimulation, doing each long enough to know whether it helped. Emri breathed, but thinly. When Delka slid a finger into the baby's palm, the fingers closed without force.

"Bring her back," Yune said.

It was early to abandon the table. It was not early to listen. Delka gathered Emri against her and returned to the bed.

The moment the baby's cheek touched Yune's bare chest, Yune's arm curved around her. Emri's mouth opened. A clean breath filled her from belly to collarbones, followed by another, and a faint complaining sound escaped on the exhale.

The birth attendant laughed once, from relief, then bent back to her work.

Delka kept two fingers against Emri's ankle. The pulse remained quick, as a newborn's should. The color around her lips improved. She waited through ten breaths and found only one short catch among them.

"She needed my heat," Yune whispered.

"Perhaps."

"That means you don't think so."

Delka looked up. Sweat had dried in pale streaks at Yune's temples. Her hair was stuck to one cheek, and one eye had swollen nearly shut from the long strain. She still watched Delka with unwelcome accuracy.

"It means heat is helping," Delka said. "I don't know yet what else is."

She did know that something else had happened. On the table, when Emri's breath had faltered, the three empty hollows of the source slab had misted at once. No child without active magic could disturb them, and no ordinary working should have crossed a room without leaving a trace Delka could feel.

Most babies never met an assessor. They were born, bundled, fed, and carried home with their sources as dormant as seeds in winter soil. Delka was called only when something in the birth room behaved impossibly. The attendant had sent for her because every iron latch in the house had lifted when Yune's labor began. By the time Delka arrived, they had all fallen closed.

Now the hollows shone with three beads of water, one in each. As Delka watched, they dried.

She pulled the assessment table closer to the bed so Yune could see it.

"I need to test what happened," she said. "The first distinction requires no blood and takes less than a minute. I will keep her warm."

Yune's hand tightened over Emri's linen. "Will it help her breathe?"

"An accurate source can change how we care for her. The wrong house could make this worse."

That answer cost Delka something. It should have been enough by itself. Instead she waited.

Yune studied the baby's face, then nodded. "One minute."

Delka lifted Emri back to the table. The good breathing lasted for three counts before it thinned.

From the first hollow in the clay she took a flat loop of copper. It was only metal; all three instruments were dead matter until a living practitioner supplied the working. Delka settled the loop around her left thumb and gave it the little stepped pulse she had learned in her first winter of study: four equal pressures, a rest, then four again. The copper warmed. That repeated shape was the signature of the house of study. Any learned working laid upon the child might answer it, resist it, or distort it, but it could not mistake deliberate recurrence for accident.

She held the loop above Emri's breastbone. Four pressures. Rest. Four.

Nothing answered. The loop cooled.

Emri's fingers opened. The baby did not cry. Her next breath snagged so badly that Delka had her back in Yune's arms before the promised minute ended.

Again the change was immediate. Yune flinched before Emri inhaled, then pressed the child closer as the breath arrived.

"What was that?" Delka asked.

"What?"

"You moved first."

Yune shut her eyes. "I knew it was coming."

"You felt her chest."

"No." Her voice had roughened. "I felt the space where her breath was going to be."

On the corner of the table, two drops from the wash basin trembled in separate rings of spilled water. The smaller drop crept sideways. The larger moved to meet it. They touched, quivered together, and lay still.

Delka moved the basin away. "Have you ever done that?"

Yune opened her good eye. "Made water crawl? I have been occupied."

The answer was sharper than a joke. Delka deserved it.

She took the second instrument from its hollow, a pair of river-glass rods, one marked at the end with thread. Inherited magic answered along family lines. With Yune's permission, Delka pressed the marked rod to the inside of her wrist until it held the impression of her pulse. She fed both rods a small charge of her own, enough to reveal a likeness but not create one.

"If the source passes through your family, these will lean toward the same rhythm," Delka explained. "I need them apart to read it cleanly."

Yune looked down at Emri. "How long?"

"Three breaths."

"Hers, or yours?"

"Hers."

Yune released the linen reluctantly.

Delka kept Emri within the span of Yune's arm, but the moment skin left skin, both glass rods went cold. The impression on Yune's rod remained clear. Emri's did not take an impression at all. It was as if Delka had laid it against an empty blanket.

The child's first separated breath came. The second broke into two small efforts. Delka did not wait for a third.

When she returned Emri, Yune's hand was ready. Her thumb swept once over the baby's shoulder. On the table between them, the two glass rods rolled in matching arcs until the thread snapped taut.

They had not leaned toward a shared family rhythm. They had moved after the test was over.

Delka caught them before they fell. Her mind offered explanations in the order her teachers had given her: an inherited faculty too faint for the rods, a concealed working in Yune, an error in the charge. Each explanation required the effect to survive separation. None did.

Emri breathed against Yune's skin with an uneven but improving rhythm. Yune winced before every shallow breath now. Each time, she shifted her hand a fraction, bracing without squeezing, and the baby's following effort came fuller.

"What do you feel?" Delka asked.

"Not her breath." Yune swallowed. "A sort of pull. When I answer it, she answers me."

The word *answer* lodged where Delka's tidy explanations had failed.

The third instrument was a twist of undyed wool around a silver hook. Gifted magic always carried an aftertone from the giver, however freely the gift had been made. The hook did not identify the giver; it only tugged the aftertone clear from the receiver's own source. A gift made in the womb was rare, but not impossible. A frightened relative might have reached through Yune during labor. Yune herself might have surrendered some faculty without knowing it.

Delka explained less this time. Yune gave a tired nod when she understood the purpose.

"Last one," Delka said.

She lifted Emri only far enough to settle her on the warmed blanket beside Yune's hip. Yune kept one hand beneath the baby's head until Delka asked her to raise it.

Contact broke.

The silver hook took Delka's charge and pulled. The wool should have tightened around any seam between donor and receiver. It hung loose.

Emri's mouth darkened again.

Yune reached past Delka and planted her palm on the baby's belly. Delka almost stopped her. A clean gifted reading required isolation from every possible donor. Then Emri dragged in a breath, and the wool wound itself tight around the hook.

Delka released her charge. The wool went slack.

"Enough," Yune said.

"Yes."

Delka returned the baby to her chest. She warmed another cloth at the brazier and tucked it over them both. The birth attendant had finished her work and now stood at the bed's foot, hands folded into her apron. She said nothing, but there was no mistaking the question in her face.

Delka checked Emri again without taking her away. Pulse, color, warmth. The child's breathing had not settled entirely. Several breaths came easily, then one failed to deepen. Yune anticipated each failure, though the warning was wearing her down. Her arm shook with the effort of holding position.

"I need a clean reading with neither of you touching," Delka said. Even to her own ears, the sentence sounded like something recited in a room where no baby had ever struggled.

"No."

"It would tell me whether you are carrying the source for her."

"You have taken her three times. She worsened three times. You can learn something else."

Delka's first impulse was to explain that repetition protected them from coincidence. Her second was worse: to remind Yune that an assessor carried responsibility for every house she entered in a register. If Delka wrote *inherited* and was wrong, Emri might someday be taught to depend on blood relations when none could help her. If she wrote *gifted*, someone would search for a donor. If she left the line blank, the family would be sent home with no known precautions.

None of that made a fourth separation safer.

"All right," Delka said.

She covered the three hollows with their wool pad. The habit of isolation was so deep that her hands felt dishonest putting the kit away. Keep the subject separate. Keep the assessor's source out of the result. Keep every living influence on its own side of the instrument. Those rules had saved people from fraudulent gifts and false family claims. They had also made the birth room's impossible event vanish every time Delka obeyed them.

Each time Emri lay alone, the effect ceased.

"Yune," she said, "may I put a studied count through your wrist while you hold her? It will be mine, not yours. If anything answers, I can stop it at once."

"Will you move her?"

"No."

"Then tell me where to put my hand."

Delka drew the assessment table flush with the bed. She checked that Emri's neck was supported, raised the baby's shoulders slightly with a folded cloth, and showed Yune how to hold without spending the last strength in her arm. These were ordinary changes. Emri rewarded them with a thin cry that seemed enormous in the warm room.

Only then did Delka set two fingers on Yune's wrist.

Her breath-count was one of the smallest workings in the house of study. Assessors used it to separate a patient's pulse from their own expectation: four measured beats of warmth through the fingertips, a cool rest, then four again. It had taken Delka seven months to perform without speeding when she was frightened.

She began.

Warm, warm, warm, warm. Rest.

Yune's pulse ran quicker beneath it. Emri breathed twice.

Warm, warm, warm, warm. Rest.

"There," Yune said.

Delka had felt it too. In the cool rest, when her working supplied nothing, a third cadence moved between her fingers and Yune's wrist. It was not a beat. It was closer to the gathering before a beat, a pressure that invited rather than pushed.

Yune answered by shifting her palm. Emri answered with a deeper breath.

The next gathering came sooner. Delka adjusted her count instead of forcing it back into measure. Four became three, then lengthened. Yune followed. Between them, Emri made a rhythm no one of them had begun.

On the table, the two blanket pins turned point for point in matching arcs. Delka heard the silver hook tick against the copper loop and swept all three instruments farther apart with her free hand. The movement of the pins stopped when her attention broke, then resumed when Yune caught the next gathering and Delka met it.

"Again," Yune murmured.

They did it again. It was neither a spell cast upon Emri nor Yune lending the baby a hidden inheritance. Delka offered a learned cadence; Yune altered it according to what she felt; Emri's body answered and changed what came next. The working existed in the responses. Remove one living participant, and there was no lesser version left behind.

After several rounds, Emri's breaths began to carry themselves. Delka let the studied count fade. Yune continued to feel the next effort for a little while, then shook her head.

"It's gone."

"The warning?"

"The pull. She is only breathing now."

Only breathing. Delka had never heard a better report.

She checked the child. The improvement was real, but she did not give the working credit for all of it. Emri was warm now, properly positioned, and no longer being lifted back and forth for Delka's instruments. Her color had evened. She made a small rooting motion against Yune's chest.

One uncertainty remained. Perhaps Yune was the necessary source, some maternal faculty the rods had failed to read. Delka could leave that question unanswered tonight. She could not call a fourth house into existence merely because her familiar instruments had embarrassed her.

"I can stop," she said. "Or, with your agreement, I can hold her once more while you keep your hand on her until she is settled. Not for any of the old tests. I want to know whether she can answer someone who isn't her mother."

Yune's face closed.

Delka waited. She did not add the reasons already visible between them.

"No instruments," Yune said at last.

"None."

"And if her breathing changes, she comes straight back."

"Yes."

Yune slid one hand beneath Emri and helped transfer her. This time there was no sudden loss of warmth, no empty span through the air. Delka settled the child against her forearm while Yune's fingers remained at the baby's back. When Emri was secure, Yune withdrew slowly.

Delka did not begin the breath-count. She offered no pulse, no shaped warmth, nothing learned. Emri's eyes had closed. One tiny hand worked free of the linen and caught Delka's little finger.

The grip tightened.

Delka, who had handled hundreds of newborns without treating a reflex as a question, bent her finger in reply.

Emri's grip loosened, then tightened twice.

Delka answered twice.

Across the table, the copper loop tipped up on its edge. The silver hook tipped with it, though they lay a handspan apart. They rocked once in opposite directions, corrected, and came to rest at the same slant.

Delka held her finger still. Emri squeezed, but Delka did not answer. The instruments did not move.

She bent her finger once more. Emri's next squeeze arrived before she finished. The copper and silver rocked together.

Delka did not ask Emri to do it a third time.

Delka returned Emri to Yune, who received her with a look that contained gratitude and accusation in equal measure. Delka accepted both. The baby settled cheek-down and breathed.

At the assessment table, Delka opened the register. The page offered three square marks after the word *house*. Study. Inheritance. Gift.

For years she had understood the marks as an inventory of possibility. Now they looked like an inventory of methods that worked only after the world had been divided into single bodies.

She did not choose one. Beneath them, in the blank space usually reserved for corrections, she drew a fourth square.

"What will you call it?" the birth attendant asked.

Delka looked at Yune. "What did you call what you felt?"

"I didn't." Yune's eyes were closed now. "I was busy answering her."

So Delka wrote only what they knew: *Observed between Emri Aster and a living respondent. Absent in isolation. Form varies with respondent. Do not separate for source testing.* Then, beside the fourth square, she wrote Emri's name.

Other assessors could argue later about whether one newborn constituted a house, or whether a source that belonged to no one alone belonged in the register. Tonight the fourth square had a member. That was all Delka needed it to hold.

Delka turned to a fresh page and wrote the immediate care instructions. Continuous responsive contact when possible. Warmth. Supported position. Feeding when Emri showed readiness. No magical trials without Yune's agreement. Ordinary checks often.

As she sanded the ink, Emri stirred. Yune shifted in response. The two blanket pins began their matching turn toward the edge of the table.

Delka caught them, moved the scissors out of reach, and asked for another clean cloth.
