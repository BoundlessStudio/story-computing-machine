# A Future Without Rescue

The gate inspector lifted the puppet moon by its wooden nose, complained about the weight of painted canvas, and let us through.

I lay folded under the cart's false floor with Dalen's glue pot against my cheek. Every turn of the wheels ground grit into my hip. Above me, the little stage knocked and muttered: a king with a loose crown, three paper fish, the moon. Dalen answered the inspector in the mild, injured voice he used when someone doubted the dignity of puppets.

"Search them if you like," he said. "They've been rehearsing treason all week."

The inspector laughed. The gate chain rose. Beyond it, dawn smelled of wet fields instead of coal smoke, and the odds house's bond around my wrist loosened by one impossible degree.

Two hours later Dalen stopped beside a ditch. He did not offer his hand when I crawled out. That was all right. I had already seen the milepost behind him and the empty road running south. I had a stolen coat, nine silver rounds, and no master close enough to shout my name.

"We'd have done well with that moon," I told him.

"The moon did well without you."

It was not forgiveness. It was freedom.

My lungs gave up the future.

Cold air tore into them. I was back in the alley with my fist raised toward the rear door of Dalen's booth. The gate was still shut three streets away. Rain ticked on the patched awning. Under my glove, the service bond gripped my wrist as tight as ever.

I had not knocked yet.

That was the cruelty of a useful future: afterward, the present looked like a mistake.

I bent over until the cobbles steadied. Three other answers had broken around the bright one too quickly to keep: a guard's blue sleeve, Dalen's cart standing empty, my own hands locked in irons. Four branches was the most I could bear from one question, on a good day. Tonight I needed only the one in which he said yes.

I knocked our old cue: two quick, one slow, two quick. *Enter miracle, carrying hat.*

Nothing moved inside.

"Your timing's late," Dalen said through the door.

"Your door's slow."

The bolt slid. He opened only far enough to look at me, then past me, down both ends of the alley. Seven years had pared him rather than broadened him. Paste streaked one cuff of his shirt. His hair, which had once required a comb, a saucer of lamp oil, and my supervision, had surrendered entirely.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked."

"Then we can both go to sleep pleased with ourselves."

From the street came the tap of a watchman's staff. Dalen caught my sleeve and pulled me in before the next tap. He barred the door without looking at me.

The booth was smaller behind the stage than memory allowed. Half-made faces watched from a shelf. A paste brush stood in cold tea. Strips of blue paper had been laid across the worktable to dry, and a child's blanket made a low tent between two scenery crates. One bare foot stuck out at the end.

I had seen the blanket in none of my futures.

"What followed you?" Dalen whispered.

"Rain."

"Rain doesn't wait in pairs at the top of the lane."

I glanced toward the shutter. He put himself between it and me, which seemed excessive until I understood that he was also putting himself between me and the blanket.

"House collectors," I said. "Possibly two. More after first bell."

"You brought the odds house to my door."

"I brought myself. They're an unfortunate accessory."

"You always did overpack."

The answer came on the old rhythm, and for half a second my body expected the next beat: my turn, his turn, the audience's laugh. I smiled. He did not, though his thumb worried a clot of paste from his knuckle.

I put the nine silver rounds on the table in a shining stack. "Your cart crosses the ward gate at dawn. The inspectors don't search children's shows. Hide me under the scenery, drive through, and those are yours."

Dalen looked at the money, then at me. "They search everything now."

"Not tomorrow. The inspector on the east lane has a split thumbnail and thinks the moon is too heavy. He lifts it by the nose."

His thumb stopped.

I had given away the preview. Not by knowing the inspector; perhaps Dalen could have believed a bribe or a bed. The moon had no nose yet. On the worktable, its blank wooden disc waited beside a triangle of folded paper.

"How many times?" he asked.

"Once tonight."

"At my door?"

"Before it."

He swept the coins into his palm. For an instant I saw them disappearing into his pocket. Then he dropped them back into my hand. The slap of silver woke one soft rustle beneath the blanket.

"Buy a different cart," he said.

"There isn't one that gets out before the bond call."

"If they find you under mine, they take the permit first. Then the cart. Then this booth, because the cart sleeps here." He nodded toward the blanket tent. "My sister has one room above a tannery and four children already. That's where my girl waits while I argue that puppeteering isn't organized crime."

"They don't find us. I saw us through."

"You saw yourself through."

"I was in your cart. You were driving."

"And after the ditch?"

The yellow room was still ahead of me then, unseen. In the branch by the gate, Dalen had been standing in the road when I crawled out. I could testify to his safety for exactly that long.

"You went home," I said.

He raised his eyebrows.

"You could have."

"Nine rounds," he said, "and an optimistic verb."

"You looked."

"For reachable people."

"Ah. Reachable." He picked up the wooden moon and began fixing its paper nose with more paste than the job required. "How flattering to survive the first cut."

"You are the only person who can leave me free after dawn."

"That sounds like something your talent told you."

"It is something I know."

"Not the same trade."

Outside, the staff tapped again, farther away. I let my coins fall back into their purse. Money had failed in every version where Dalen had time to count what it was buying. I should have begun with history. I had meant to begin with history. The future at the gate had smelled so clean that I had mistaken arrival for a reason.

I turned the old request over in my mind: *Carry me out in your cart because once, before everything spoiled, you loved me enough to do it.* One person. One act. I held my breath.

The booth lunged away.

I was in a rented room with yellow plaster, six days free. A washbasin sweated beside the bed. My wrist was purple where I had cut the house bond off, but nothing tightened when I flexed my hand. Through the window came a market quarrel about pears. No collectors. No barred gate. I ate bread too quickly and laughed crumbs onto the blanket.

Dalen was not there.

I looked for evidence of him: a coin missing, paste on my coat, one of his dull jokes waiting in my mouth. The future gave me only what my own senses could gather. On the table lay the king's loose crown, perhaps a gift, perhaps something I had taken. When I tried to remember how I had reached the room, the branch hurried forward. Nine days. Eleven. The same empty washbasin, the same free wrist. Safety repeated itself while everything beyond the walls remained blank.

I came back with my hand braced on Dalen's worktable.

He had pasted the moon's nose crooked.

"Your left sleeve is burning," I said.

Dalen looked down. It wasn't. The lamp flame stood a foot away.

"No," I said. "That was—"

"I know what it was."

Vertigo dragged the booth sideways. I shut one eye. "Do you remember the night at the wool market? The rain came through the stage roof, and we did the drowned-prince act for eleven people and a dog. You said if I ever needed—"

"Nine people."

"There were eleven."

"Two were vendors hiding from the rain. They didn't pay."

"The dog didn't pay either. You bowed to him."

"He stayed for the ending."

This time the smile nearly reached Dalen's mouth. The old show stood between us in its wet shoes: his prince coughing river water, my princess selling his boots while he drowned. We had eaten well for three days on that dog's owner's generosity.

"You said," I tried again, "that if I ever needed a door—"

"A trapdoor. You kept missing the cue."

"You know what you meant."

The smile went. He set the moon down.

"I know what happened eight months later."

There were some pasts I had never learned how to enter without peeking at the exits. "The counting-room job went bad."

"It went exactly as you promised. For you."

We had sold the counting-house clerk a glimpse of his employer's ruin. Dalen played the bereaved nephew; I played the oracle. The clerk paid, the watch came early, and I followed the branch in which an upstairs window had been left unlatched. Three roofs, a laundry line, a hay wagon. By sunrise I had been outside the city.

"You told me you were behind me," I said.

"I was behind you. Then the window shut."

That did not fit. I remembered his breath at my shoulder, his hand shoving my boot upward. I remembered landing in hay and hearing him laugh.

No. I remembered the branch.

Dalen rolled the moon's paper nose between finger and thumb until it collapsed. "Six months in the lockhouse. They broke two fingers because I wouldn't name you. When I came out, you had joined the odds house and were predicting grain shipments for gentlemen who could afford curtains."

"I signed because they found me."

"You didn't look for me."

"I looked." The lie was almost true. I had looked forward: messages returned, visits refused, Dalen asking for money I did not have, Dalen asking for an apology I could not make land correctly. "Every way I tried made it worse."

"So you spared yourself the attempt."

The blanket shifted. A small, sleep-creased face appeared between the crates.

"Papa?"

Dalen turned at once. Anger left his shoulders without leaving the room. "I'm here. Go back under."

"My foot's cold."

"You put it out."

"It came out."

He crouched and tucked the bare foot beneath the blanket. The child watched me over his shoulder. She had Dalen's solemn stare and a stripe of blue paper stuck to one cheek.

"Is she the princess thief?" she asked.

"Retired," I said.

"She's leaving," Dalen said.

The girl considered this, apparently found it acceptable, and held out the corner of the blanket. "Wrong side."

He turned it so the faded red squares faced up. A practice born of many nights, invisible to prophecy because no future of mine had needed to notice it.

I could use her. Not as threat; I was not that lost. As proof. Dalen would not want collectors tearing apart the booth with his daughter inside. The safest choice for her was the swift choice for me: put the fugitive under the cart, remove the danger from the child, let every need point in one direction.

I heard the argument arranging itself in my own voice, tidy as chairs before a show.

The girl rubbed her cheek against the blanket and transferred the blue paper to it. Dalen left the scrap there. He did not notice. Or he did, and it did not matter.

I swallowed my argument.

"Water," she said.

Dalen passed her a stoppered cup from beneath the worktable. While she drank, wheels grated at the far end of the street.

Not a patrol. Too slow. House collectors wanted you to hear the time being used up.

Dalen heard them too. He rose, spilling a little water on his cuff, and his fear made him spare.

"Front or back?"

"The alley is watched."

"Front, then."

"They'll take me."

"I know."

"You could still—"

"No."

I reached for the last reliable breath. A third preview in an hour was supposed to be the edge, not the fall. I had survived worse bleeds. I only needed to see the appeal in which I named what he had once felt. Not a debt, not the child. Something true: *If any part of you still loves me, hide me in the cart.*

I closed my mouth on the air.

The cart jolted under my spine. Yellow plaster flared around me. The inspector's blue sleeve wiped crumbs from the table. My free wrist wore the king's crown. Dalen's broken fingers lifted the gate chain. The child's blanket lay in a ditch, red side up, while pears rolled across the booth floor.

"—mena."

Someone slapped the table.

"Philomena. Breathe."

I did, and vomited cold tea onto Dalen's boots.

The child began to cry. Dalen scooped her up, blanket and all. His face had gone white around the mouth.

"Sorry," I said. It was the wrong word, late by seven years.

"Don't look again."

"I didn't see what happens to you."

He rocked his daughter once, an impatient movement meant for both of them. "You never do."

The wheels stopped outside the booth.

Someone tried the public door. Its iron latch lifted and fell. "Odds property," a man called. "Open for lawful recovery."

Dalen looked toward the rear door, calculating despite himself. The alley. The cart. The child in his arms. For one sick, bright instant I thought the future had caught him and hauled him toward his mark.

"No," I said.

His gaze snapped back to mine.

Without holding my breath, I had no prepared sentence. The truth came out shapeless. "Will you take me through the gate in your cart?"

The child hiccupped against his shoulder. Paste and cold tea soaked one of his boots. He could have delayed. He could have lied and opened the hidden floor after I turned away. He could have made his answer depend on remorse, payment, the particular quality of my fear.

"No," Dalen said.

The future did not open. Nothing in him reached to soften the word.

Outside, the collector struck the door twice with the butt of his staff. "Last courtesy."

I nodded because Dalen's answer had not made him cruel, and that was somehow harder to bear. "They'll search when they find out I was here."

"Then don't be here when they open it."

It was not an offer. He shifted his daughter higher and waited.

I unbarred the public door.

Rain silvered the street. Two house collectors stood beneath the awning, red retrieval cords looped at their belts. Behind them the lane climbed toward the shut ward gate. There was no field smell, no inspector laughing, no safe room with yellow walls. Only coal smoke and the first bell gathering itself in the tower.

I stepped outside and pulled the booth door closed behind me.

"Evening," I said, though dawn had begun to whiten the roofs. "If you've brought a cart, I hope it has springs."

They moved toward me. I ran the other way, not toward the alley, not toward Dalen's cart, and with no breath left to spend on where the street might end.
