The ninth rock of the week arrived during roll call. It punched through the council's awning, struck the bronze speaking gong, and rang out the last syllable of Senn's name.

Senn, who had a tail built for answering rocks, looked up at the new hole. "That's a vote," he said.

"It is not," said Chair Morro. The sauropod had to lower his head between two roof beams to address the table. "It is an interruption. Secretary, please mark Councilor Senn present."

Nobody had to ask where the rock came from. Humans had abandoned Earth for their stations so long ago that the oldest member of the council had learned about them from her grandmother. They had left the grass and trees, the warm mud, every creature that had made a life on the ground since. Apparently, they had taken plenty of stones.

Vetch waited while the secretary retrieved the one that had hit the gong. It was no larger than an egg, black on one side from its fall. A rock that size would be a nuisance in the council chamber. In a nest it could go straight through the shell.

"Nine rocks in seven days," Morro said. "The first matter is whether to send a formal objection."

"We sent one after the third," said Kella, the sky watcher. She turned a screen toward the table. On a recording from the station's last pass, a human in a corridor grinned at a camera while another human placed a pale stone in her outstretched hand. Beyond them, through a round window, Earth filled the dark. "They put the objection under the picture of their next throw."

The grinning human flicked the stone into the mouth of a machine. A line of lights ran along its length. Vetch had watched enough recordings to know the machine would do the hard part, steering the stone toward the ground. The humans still insisted on calling each release a throw. They counted them aloud and argued about who had the better arm.

Senn leaned close to the screen. "Was that today's?"

"Released a few minutes ago," Kella said. "The fall takes about an hour. It should land in the Gathering Bowl near sundown."

Vetch stood. She was a dryosaur, and standing made little difference to what the others could see over the table, but it let her get the words out. "The nest ledge is above the Bowl. Fen is there now. One of our eggs has started to open."

Morro's head came down another span. "Can it be carried?"

"Not now. Fen would have to lift the whole bed of earth, and the path shakes under a careful foot." Vetch pushed a drawing toward him. It showed the Bowl's white festival circle, the nesting ledge on its eastern rim, and the eight places where stones had landed. Five lay inside the circle. Three had missed. All eight were too close. "I want the circle gone before that rock lands."

"The Opening Procession begins there tomorrow," Morro said.

"Then it can begin without a circle."

Kella enlarged the humans' picture. Their station broadcast carried a second image in one corner: the Bowl as seen from the council's camera on the rim, its white ring neat against the dark stone. The apes had taken the camera feed without asking; a number flashed on their broadcast when a rock struck inside the circle.

The council watched the tiny human take a bow.

"They've made a game of it," Senn said. He looked less pleased with the word than he might have on another day.

"A game requires a target," Vetch said. "We gave them one."

Morro looked at the map, then at the hole in the awning. "Will the council permit the festival circle to be removed?"

There was debate. There had to be debate; Morro would have required it of a falling mountain. One member asked whether a rock, once thrown, could be persuaded by the removal of paint. Kella said no, but the next throw could. Another wondered if erasing the ring amounted to surrender. Vetch could hear the station's cheerful music leaking from Kella's screen while they spoke.

Senn settled the matter without a speech. He crossed to the wall, took down the broad broom used after the procession, and set it beside Vetch's map.

"I vote yes," he said. "Who has another broom?"

By sundown, there was no white circle left in the Gathering Bowl. The procession committee had carried away its banners. Morro had come down with the council, his long neck giving him an excellent view of the rim but no good way to scrub near his feet. Senn had proved better at rubbing chalk from stone with his armored side than with a broom. Vetch had scrubbed until her forelimbs ached. Whenever she looked up, the station crossed the darkening sky like a slow silver seed.

Fen met her at the path to the ledge. His claws and chest were powdered white; he had taken a turn at the Bowl while another nest keeper watched the egg.

"Any change?" Vetch asked.

He shook his head. "A little more shell has lifted. It made a sound when I touched the earth beside it."

She wanted to climb up with him. Instead she stayed at the foot of the path and listened for the rock Kella had predicted. It came down at the Bowl's far side with a sharp crack and a plume of dust. No score appeared on the station feed. The humans had aimed before the circle was erased, and the stone had landed where its edge had been.

"One more," Fen said, looking at the sky, "and they might notice."

"They noticed us erasing it. They can watch their own picture."

"I meant they might notice why."

Vetch did not answer. She went up to the ledge. The egg lay in a shallow nest of earth and leaves, a narrow break near its broad end. Through that break came one faint scrape, then stillness. She sat beside it until the station disappeared beyond the horizon.

The station passed overhead twice more without a throw. Vetch let Fen sleep while she watched the egg. It tapped at its shell and rested and tapped again. For those few hours, the Bowl lay quiet.

At noon the next day, a broad pale stone with three rings carved into one face fell into the Bowl.

It landed on the bare floor where the white circle had been, rings upward. The grooves were carved deep enough to survive the descent, and the stone was pale enough to see from the council's camera on the rim. By the time Vetch reached the emergency meeting, the humans had put its image on their broadcast. One of them pointed at it. Another held up a smaller rock and pointed down.

"They brought a new target," Kella said.

"We can roll it under the overhang," offered a councilor.

"Then they'll drop another," said Vetch. She did not know if they would, but she had watched the human point. "They want to hit this one."

Morro had kept the chamber awning rolled back since the gong was struck. He looked through its square of blue sky. "Our first action failed."

"They stopped throwing for two passes," Vetch said. "For one night, it worked."

Kella checked the figures at the bottom of the human broadcast. "Next station pass in two hours. They've picked their stone."

"We can break their target," Senn said. He lifted his tail a little, to show that this proposal required no vote to become practical.

Vetch could picture the grooved stone in pieces, each piece bright against the dark floor of the Bowl. The humans would choose one of them. Or they would bring more. She looked at Kella's screen. The human with the stone was laughing at something out of view; someone else turned the camera back toward Earth so the audience could see the target below.

"They think that ring is easy," Vetch said.

"It is large enough to see from orbit," Kella replied.

"Then give them something smaller. Somewhere it won't matter if they miss."

Senn lowered his tail. "You're asking them to keep throwing."

"They already are."

The council fell quiet. Vetch had come to ask them to stop the apes, and now she was proposing a better game for them. She disliked the sound of it as much as Senn did. Across the bare ridge west of the Bowl lay an empty quarry, with a wide apron of uninhabited stone around it. If the apes aimed at its center and missed by as much as they had at the Bowl, they would still strike bare ground. The apes wanted a boast. They had not asked which part of the ground the dinosaurs could bear to give them.

"A smaller target in the old quarry," Morro said, following her glance to the map. "How would they know to take it?"

"We move their stone there," Vetch said. "Then we stand a smaller one on top. Put the quarry camera on our broadcast."

"That sounds very much like encouraging them."

"Yes."

Morro's tongue worked once behind his closed mouth. He did not put the question to a vote at once. "We cannot promise they'll accept it."

"I know," Vetch said. "Once it hatches, Fen and I can carry it away. Until then I need them looking somewhere else."

The decision passed by a margin of one. Morro voted with Vetch. Senn voted against, then helped her pull the grooved stone onto a sled because it would not move itself and because he knew what lay above the Bowl.

"If they throw while we're carrying it," Senn said, "we'll be the target."

"The next pass is in two hours," Kella said. She kept the microphone on her sky-watch screen live on the council channel. Vetch clipped her own receiver to her shoulder so Fen could reach her from the ledge. "We'll be over the ridge before they can release anything else."

Six councilors dragged the sled west from the Bowl, up over the bare ridge and down to the quarry. Senn braced it at the crest while Morro steadied the stone with one immense foot. It took less than an hour, though Vetch's forelimbs shook when they reached level ground.

At the quarry they slid the pale stone onto the floor and placed a narrow black stone upright in the center of its carved rings. Senn chose the black stone. He had found it among the rubble and could stand it on end with one precise nudge of his tail.

Kella turned the quarry camera toward the target. The council broadcast now showed only the little black point on pale rock and the wide, empty quarry around it.

For the last ten minutes before the station's pass, the humans' picture did not change. They showed the Gathering Bowl. One human had a finger on the release control. Another, the thrower with the grin, leaned so close to the broadcast camera that her face filled the screen. She had seen the empty place where her target had been. Vetch could not hear what she said to the others. The station muted its corridor whenever they argued.

"They'll put one back," Senn said.

"Maybe," Vetch said.

The image cut to the quarry. The thrower pointed at the upright black stone, then held two fingers a very short distance apart. She sent one rock into the machine, demanded another with her open hand, and sent that one after it. On Kella's fall plot, both paths ended inside the quarry's empty apron.

Morro sent the councilors to the quarry's upper rim, behind a thick lip of stone. Nearly an hour passed before the first bright streak crossed overhead. Vetch listened to the hiss of Kella's screen and watched the marked paths creep across it.

The rock struck the quarry wall well above the target. Chips clattered to the floor. Vetch had spent years watching Senn's tail games; she could see how much lower the humans would need to aim. For one ugly instant she wanted to tell them. A clean hit would keep them throwing here.

From the station's broadcast came a burst of groans. Senn gave a noise through his nose that Vetch had heard him make only at an especially poor tail game.

"They missed," Morro said.

"Their second one's still coming here," Kella said, tapping its path on the screen.

A few minutes later it struck the quarry floor, well left of the black stone. Dust rose and settled below the rim. On the station broadcast, the humans' groans became an argument. One took a fresh stone out of the thrower's hand and held it up to the camera as if offering to demonstrate. The thrower snatched it back.

Fen's voice sounded from Vetch's receiver. "It has a hole now. A proper hole. I can see its snout."

She pressed the receiver close. Behind his voice she heard a dry tick, the sound of a beak against shell. Vetch looked once more at the quarry. Senn had found a rounded chip of stone and was testing its weight against his tail. Morro had lowered his head to see what he was doing.

"I'm coming," she told Fen.

"You don't have to run."

She ran anyway, up the track across the ridge. At the ledge, Fen moved aside without telling her what had happened.

The egg was open. A wet, furious little creature had kicked one foot clear, but the lower half of the shell still held it. Vetch lay down beside the nest. The foot found her planted toe; the hatchling pushed, slipped free, and blinked at the light. Fen touched his muzzle to hers.

Over the open receiver came a sharp crack and Senn's shout: "Got the black stone!" The hatchling startled, planted both feet, and stood.
