# Not today.

At dawn, Delyth thought of her own death and said, “Not today.”

Then she got up to help her son buy a wedding suit.

She had been choosing herself for eleven months. Before the illness, she had often gone half a day without using the gift, waiting to see what might need stopping. Now she spent it before she put on her slippers. One thing between one dawn and the next, and she wanted to be alive for all of them.

The words did nothing for the ache in her chest as she dressed. Her heart had been damaged; preventing her death could not undo that. She sat to pull on her stockings and rested again before the stairs. Tavin had come early and was downstairs, being too quiet with the cups.

“I've done it,” she called. “You can make a noise.”

He dropped a spoon.

“A smaller noise.”

At the tailor's, he chose a grey so cautious that she asked whether the wedding had been cancelled and nobody had told her.

“I like grey.”

“Then have grey.”

“You just said—”

“You asked what I thought. I thought something. You aren't obliged to wear it.”

He looked at her in the long mirror, trying not to smile. The tailor, crouched at his ankle, kept a mouthful of pins between himself and the argument.

Tavin chose the blue. When they stepped outside, he took her arm on the pretext of a loose paving stone. She let him. She could already feel the hill they had climbed.

“Lunch?” he said.

“One more stop.”

In the travel office she asked for the room in the photograph: the one with the blue shutters and the table just large enough for a plate and a glass. It overlooked a little southern square. Beyond the rooftops there was a strip of sea.

The clerk found the letter from the owner. “Available from the tenth. Two months, you said?”

“To begin with.”

Beside her, Tavin stopped taking off his gloves.

“You're going away?”

“On the tenth.”

“That's four days after the wedding.”

“I've checked.”

She had driven trains through thirty-seven winters, arriving at platforms before anyone had cleared the snow. For the thirty-eighth, she wanted to sit outside and eat something that would stain her fingers. She wanted a room where the doorbell did not introduce someone who had heard she was ill. The woman who owned this one had written that there was music in the square on Thursdays and no promise of an early night.

Delyth had liked her immediately.

“And your return passage?” the clerk asked.

“I'll buy it when I want to come back.”

Tavin put his gloves on the desk. “You can't just go and stay there.”

“They've offered me a room. I think that's what they expect.”

“I mean, by yourself.”

“You can visit. Both of you.”

“For how long?”

“We'll see how you behave.”

The clerk looked down at her papers. Tavin rubbed his face, but when Delyth counted out the deposit he steadied the little stack of coins for her.

Outside, she had to stop.

The pain spread beneath her breastbone and up into her jaw. She was holding the receipt in one hand and could not remember what she had intended to do with it. Tavin said her name. A shopkeeper came out with a chair.

She hated being lowered into it, hated the watching faces, hated most the small relief of being allowed to sit.

“It's all right,” she told Tavin. “I did it this morning.”

“I know. Stop telling me that.”

He was kneeling in the dust beside her. She put the receipt into his hand so she could hold on to his shoulder.

The doctor came to the house that afternoon. By then the pain had eased, leaving a weakness that made the bedroom seem farther from the front door than the travel office had been.

“The attacks are getting closer,” he said.

“I had noticed.”

“Then let him call me sooner.” He nodded toward Tavin. “Please. I can sometimes get you through one of these. I can't promise I will get you through the next.”

“I don't expect you to promise.”

“And when it hurts, sit down. Even on a day when you know you'll live.”

He left medicine and instructions. Tavin carried his bag out to the car, and Delyth listened to their lowered voices through the open window.

When her son came back, he had the travel receipt folded into quarters.

“They'll return the deposit,” he said. “There's still time.”

“Unfold that.”

He did.

“You can stay tonight,” she told him. “The good pillow is in the cupboard. Don't take the one off my bed.”

He stood there a moment, then put the receipt on her bedside table.

“I wasn't going to take your pillow.”

“You always did.”

“I was nine.”

She smoothed the paper against her knee after he had gone downstairs. In the photograph, a chair stood beside the little table. She imagined pulling it out of the sun when the afternoon became too hot. It was difficult to believe in being too hot, with the wind working at her window and winter not yet properly begun, but she was willing to make the experiment.

On the opposite side of the ravine, an evening train rounded the curve toward the tunnel. Its lighted windows moved across the dark ledge. She watched the last carriage disappear, then left the photograph where she could see it from bed.

***

The whistle woke her.

It was not the first train's usual greeting to the houses along the cut. It went on and on, broke, began again. Beneath it came the shriek of wheels sliding on rail.

Delyth sat up with one hand pressed beneath her collar. The tightness had woken her twice in the night and had never quite gone. She drew breath for the words.

The first light lay above the eastern roofs. Yesterday's protection was gone.

Tavin came through the door, barefoot.

“Have you—”

The whistle cut him off.

He pulled the curtain aside. Across the narrow ravine, the high line curved out of a short tunnel toward the town. Delyth had driven that curve for sixteen years. She could have drawn its gradient with her eyes shut.

A section of the ledge had fallen away.

The collapse was still moving at the bottom of the ravine: dark rock, a broken tree, a cloud of pale dust. Above it, two lengths of rail pointed down into empty air.

The first train had come out of the tunnel.

She knew its weight. Six carriages, full at this hour. She knew the fall of that track and the distance needed to stop on it. The driver had already seen.

Tavin looked from the train to her hand pressed against her chest.

“Do yours,” he said. “I'll ring the railway.”

She caught his wrist before he could turn away. His skin was warm from sleep. She wanted him to close the curtain. She wanted the picture beside her bed and her son trying on a blue suit, and an afternoon warm enough to make her move her chair. She could live through today and make the same choice at the next dawn. All she had to do was finish the familiar thought.

My death.

She opened her mouth.

Across the ravine the locked wheels threw sparks. Even from here she could hear how hard the driver was trying. She had gripped that brake handle. She had felt the terrible lightness of it after there was nothing more to pull.

Tavin turned toward the telephone on the landing. It would ring in the signal office. Someone would pick it up. Someone would turn toward the window.

The engine reached the broken rail.

Delyth changed what she was thinking of.

The train falling.

“Not today.”

It stopped.

The whistle still sounded. Dust still lifted from the ravine. But the engine and its six carriages had ceased moving as completely as if someone had laid a hand on a model. The front wheels hung beyond the end of the track. The engine's nose projected over the fall, heavy and impossible, while white-faced people crowded the windows behind it.

Tavin's wrist was still in her hand.

“Mother.”

She could not look at him yet.

“Tell them to get everyone off.”

He went to the telephone.

She listened to him give the location, then give it again more loudly. The operator kept asking what had fallen. Tavin said the line, the line, not the train. He gave his mother's name and said she had stopped it. When he hung up, he immediately lifted the receiver again and asked for the doctor.

Delyth released the bedclothes. She had gripped them so hard that the seam had left a red line across her palm.

The engine would not fall that day. The crash she had pictured could not happen. Her own heart went on beating badly, as it had before the whistle woke her.

***

The first passenger out was a woman in a yellow coat. She climbed down from the rear carriage and stood on the track, looking toward the engine. Someone handed her a small case. She put it down and went back inside.

“They should leave their things,” Tavin said.

Delyth shook her head. She needed the breath for something else.

The woman reappeared with another passenger. Between them they lowered a man who could not stand. He pushed hard against the doorway to help them turn him. They got him onto the track and sat him on the little case. The woman knelt to speak to him, her bright coat spread around her knees.

Then the next carriage began to empty.

Delyth had been holding her breath. She let it out and could not seem to take enough back in.

Tavin moved in front of the window.

“Lie down.”

“I can see—”

“I don't care. Lie down.”

He helped her onto the pillow. His hands were shaking now. She had never seen them shake over her before.

“They're sending an ambulance,” he said. “The doctor will meet us there.”

She nodded. She tried to undo the top button of her nightdress, failed twice, and let him do it.

He sat on the edge of the bed, facing the door. From where she lay, she could see only the last carriage through the window and the people making their way back toward the tunnel. A railway worker had reached them. He was taking the case from the woman in yellow.

“Tavin.”

He turned immediately.

She had meant to say something about the train. She found that she didn't want to.

“Stay here.”

“I'm here.”

The ambulance attendants carried her downstairs. She told them about the narrow turn, which they had already seen, and about the loose banister, which they hadn't. Tavin followed with her shoes and a coat, though she could not imagine being cold again.

Outside, people were standing along the road to look at the hanging engine. One man stood in the way, craning to see its unsupported wheels. Tavin pushed past him to get into the ambulance.

Delyth was glad when the doors closed.

***

At the hospital they gave her a bed by an interior window. Beyond it, an empty trolley stood against a wall. There was no sky to tell her the time.

Her doctor was there, as promised. He listened to her chest, called for things, made room for the nurses without appearing to move. Tavin stood where he was told to stand. When someone asked whether she had taken her medicine, he answered too quickly and then corrected himself.

Delyth could feel her heart missing its way. Each little pause seemed longer than the last. She waited for the next beat and was astonished by the effort of waiting.

The doctor said her name.

She thought of dying in this room, with her coat lying on the chair and the room by the sea already paid for.

“Not today.”

Even as she said it, she knew she had already spent the day.

The doctor glanced at Tavin. Her son looked down.

Nothing changed. A nurse finished adjusting the line in her arm. The trolley beyond the window remained where it was. Delyth shut her eyes, ashamed of having wanted the impossible after being given so much of it.

Then she was angry at the shame. She wanted to live. She had not agreed to stop wanting it.

“There's only one,” she told the doctor.

“I know.” He drew the blanket away from her arm. “We're still going to help you.”

She gave him her wrist.

For a while the pain eased. She could speak without stopping in the middle of a word. Tavin told her that all six carriages were empty and a recovery engine had pulled the train back onto sound track. The driver had been the last to climb down. He had walked along all six carriages before he allowed anyone to take his arm.

“He would,” she said.

Her son leaned forward. “Do you know him?”

“No.”

He sat back. She watched him work at a spot of dirt on his cuff. He had dressed in yesterday's clothes, and she remembered the pins in the tailor's mouth, the blue wool laid across Tavin's shoulder.

“How long until morning?”

He looked at the clock above the door.

“Twenty-one hours.”

She had driven for that long during a winter evacuation once, passing the throttle to another driver for stretches when she could no longer see straight. She remembered the lamps of the last station. Twenty-one hours was a length of time a person could get through.

“Help me sit up a little.”

He raised the bed. She held the rail while he arranged the pillow, and for several minutes breathing was easier.

“Is that better?”

“Yes.”

He left his hand where she could reach it.

The next attack came before noon. Afterward she could not remember what the doctor had said, only that Tavin had gone into the corridor with him. When her son returned, his cuff was wet.

“The booking,” she said.

He bent close.

“What?”

“Don't cancel it yet.”

“I won't.”

She studied him until she believed him, then closed her eyes to rest.

There were intervals when she heard everything: the trolley finally moving, somebody laughing far down the corridor, Tavin asking for water. At other times the room seemed to slip away from her before she had finished looking at it. She kept finding her son's hand. Once she asked him to tell the tailor that she preferred the wider lapel after all. He said he was going to have the narrow one, and she managed to squeeze his fingers.

In the afternoon, she asked what time it was.

He told her.

She tried to work out how many hours remained. He watched her do it and could not make himself help.

“Tavin,” she said.

“I'm here.”

She drew another breath. He waited for what she wanted to tell him.

She died before four. Tavin was still holding her hand.

***

The messages began arriving the following day.

The railway sent a large card with a crest on it. Passengers wrote on whatever they had found: hotel paper, the backs of tickets, a sheet torn from an exercise book. One woman included a photograph of a boy and said she had been on her way home to him. She wanted Delyth to know that she had got there.

Tavin put that letter on the table instead of in the pile with the others. Later he moved it to the mantel. He kept finding places for it.

The tailor telephoned about the next fitting. Tavin said he would come alone.

It took him longer to call the travel office.

The clerk remembered them. When he told her, she was silent for a moment, then said she was sorry. She would send word to the owner of the room.

“The deposit can be returned to you,” she said.

“All right.”

“And the passage. Was there a return to cancel as well?”

He had the photograph in front of him. Blue shutters, a table in the sun, the chair his mother had chosen.

“No,” he said. “She hadn't decided when she was coming back.”
