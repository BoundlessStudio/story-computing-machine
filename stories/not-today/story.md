# The Rope Trick

Halvern wanted me to spend the day on a wineglass.

He held it by the stem, letting the light find the little grapes cut into the bowl. We stood on the stage of his private theater. There were twelve velvet chairs in front of us, all empty, and a portrait of Halvern watching from above the mantel.

“I throw it,” he said. “It doesn't break.”

“That's a costly way to discover whether your carpet is thick.”

“Onto the hearth, then.”

Delwin made a small, appreciative noise. He had been admiring the glass since we arrived. In his patched blue coat, with one silver cuff button and one brass, he looked like a man who had already worked out where to sell it.

I could have done it. One thing every day: think of what I wanted to prevent, say the words, and it would not happen. At midnight the refusal ended and I could choose again. A glass protected at breakfast could be thrown, trodden on, or dropped out of a steeple until midnight, though you still had to find it afterward.

But then I would have spent my day on a glass.

“We brought an act,” I said.

“I brought the glass.”

I opened our trunk and lifted out the red rope.

It lay soft across my hands, with a faint smell of dust and the lavender Delwin put in everything he owned. Twenty yards, slim enough to coil inside my hat, bright enough to be seen from the back of a fairground. That evening it was supposed to carry both of us.

After ten years of conjuring on the ground, Delwin had proposed returning to the air. He said he missed the view. He had also seen a poster on which my name was twice the size of his.

“We inspect it before every performance,” I told Halvern. “We check every fastening. We practice the catches until we can do them without looking. But I get one refusal, and tonight I want this.”

“I would prefer the glass.”

“You can keep it.”

Delwin closed his eyes briefly.

I thought of the rope parting. This rope, today. I pictured the red length between my hands, all the way from one whipped end to the other.

“Not today.”

Nothing flashed. Delwin usually threw a handful of silver paper at this point, but he had decided Halvern would pay more for dignity.

“Is that all?” Halvern asked.

“Now we show you.”

The theater had a flying bar above the stage. I went up the side ladder and checked its brackets before we rigged the rope. I had prevented one thing. The bar falling out of the ceiling was still perfectly available.

Delwin steadied the ladder. Under his breath, he said, “We could have got another rope.”

“And you could have stayed on the ground.”

“At my present rate of pay, I might have to dig.”

We arranged a hanging loop. I stepped into it and let it take my weight, my boots a yard above the stage.

Halvern brought a knife from a little table beside his chair. He tested the edge against a sheet of paper. A narrow shaving curled away.

“Anywhere?”

“Anywhere on the rope.”

He pressed the knife into the red braid above my head. The rope flattened against the edge. He drew the blade across it, then harder.

I swayed. Delwin stood underneath me with his hands on his hips, judging the presentation.

Halvern turned the knife over and examined it. He tried the rope once more, slowly. Then he reached up with his free hand and squeezed it, as though I had concealed a bar of iron inside.

“Soft,” he said.

“It has to be. Otherwise I couldn't do this.”

I wound myself upward, turned, and dropped into a catch that brought my head level with his knee.

He stepped back. Delwin bowed on my behalf.

“That,” Halvern said, “is worth considerably more than you charged me.”

It was the nicest thing he said all afternoon.

***

He waited until we had packed the trunk before mentioning the new engagement.

“You will live at my country house,” he said. “There is a suitable room. Each morning you will attend me at breakfast.”

Delwin looked at me. I looked at the purse on Halvern's table.

“We have a pitch at the fair,” I said.

“An arrangement I can improve on.”

He named a sum large enough to make Delwin sit down on the trunk.

“For how long?” I asked.

“For as long as I require you.”

“And what would I be doing?”

“I would tell you what must not happen that day.”

He had put the knife away and taken up the glass again. He rolled its stem between his fingers as he spoke.

“You could employ us for performances,” Delwin said. His voice had become the pleasant, dry voice he used when children asked him to produce a second rabbit. “Two nights a week. Private audiences by arrangement.”

“I have no use for the performances.”

Delwin stood up.

“Then we have nothing to sell you.”

One of the men by the door moved in front of it. Until then I had assumed he carried chairs.

Halvern watched me notice him.

“Mr. Delwin may go back to his fair if you accept,” he said.

“She has heard you,” Delwin said. “So have I. Pay us.”

The second man caught his wrist when he reached for the purse. I took one step toward them and the first man seized my upper arm hard enough to numb my fingers.

For a moment I thought of all the other things I might have refused.

Halvern set the glass on the mantel.

“You have already used it,” he said. “I was careful to establish that.”

Delwin pulled away. His elbow struck the glass.

It broke on the hearth in a bright, ordinary crash.

Nobody moved for a second. Then Halvern told the men to take us out through the loading yard.

***

The wagon was supposed to have carried us and our luggage back to the fair. We discovered that Halvern's men had a different use for the barred compartment at the rear.

They searched us. From Delwin they took his purse, his watch, two packs of cards, three handkerchiefs and the brass key to our trunk. He objected to each confiscation separately. While he was explaining the price of the handkerchiefs, Halvern made the men open the trunk again.

“The rope stays in there,” he said.

He watched them lock it away. The hired driver hauled the trunk onto the low luggage rack behind the compartment, where it had been meant to travel all along.

“No stops until the house,” Halvern told him. “She has made her refusal today. Keep the props locked, and you will have no difficulty. I shall follow.”

The driver nodded. The riding escort, a square young man in a leather cap, was more interested in Delwin's account of the handkerchiefs. Neither had been inside the theater.

Then the rear door shut between us and the yard. The man who shut it swung a heavy iron latch into a staple on the corner post and added a padlock.

Halvern spoke through the bars.

“You will find your room quite comfortable.”

“Does the door open?” I asked.

He walked away.

The wagon lurched. I sat down abruptly, and Delwin caught my shoulder before it met the bars.

For a little while we listened to the wheels.

My arm hurt. Delwin kept turning his wrist, stopping before the movement was complete. The red marks left by the man's fingers were already darkening. Without his cards and handkerchiefs he looked oddly unfinished.

“Midnight,” he said at last.

“What?”

“You choose again at midnight. Refuse the door staying locked. Refuse being in there. Something.”

“He's going to put me in one room and you somewhere else.”

“Possibly they'll feed us first.”

“Delwin.”

He stopped working his wrist.

“Yes,” he said. “I know.”

Through the bars I could see the town gate falling behind us. Beyond it, the road ran downhill past the fair before climbing toward Halvern's estate. We had walked it that morning, Delwin carrying the light end of the trunk and complaining about the distribution of talent.

The fair spread out beside the road. Its nearest entrance was under the great chestnut, where three trunks grew out of one old root. A low branch leaned over the bend. Delwin had bumped his hat against it and accused me of steering.

I stood up.

Our trunk rode just below the wagon floor, its lid within reach through the rear bars. Above it, on our right, was the post holding the door shut: a square length of oak bolted into iron shoes at the top and bottom. The door's latch hooked onto the staple halfway up.

I put both hands against the door and pushed. Nothing gave. I tried the post. It was stout enough to take all the temper I had left.

“I need the rope,” I said.

Delwin looked at the trunk. Then he looked at me.

“I was hoping you would say that.”

He turned his left cuff inside out and worried at the facing with his thumbnail. A little brass key slipped into his palm.

“You let them take the other one.”

“Well, I couldn't let them take this one.”

He knelt at the bars. His fingers, which could persuade a coin to vanish while you were watching both hands, reached down over the front of the trunk. There was a click.

He lifted the lid just enough. I drew out a length of red rope, then another, feeding the coils into the wagon at my feet. Lavender rose briefly above the smell of horses.

Delwin shut the trunk.

“What are we doing?”

“Taking the door off.”

He looked at the rope, then at the post.

“That's a wagon, Vesta.”

“Yes.”

Our rope could carry two performers through the air. On any other day, asking it to stop a laden wagon would have snapped it before that oak post so much as bent.

I worked through the gaps beside the corner post and tied one end of the rope around the wood itself. The other end would go over the chestnut fork. The post was the thing keeping us in. It was also a thing I had made no promises about.

Delwin watched me secure the rope. Then he took off his coat.

“That isn't going to help,” I said.

“I don't intend to arrive at the fair with splinters through the lining.”

He folded it, sat on it and braced his boots against the front partition. He held out a hand for me.

“How fast?”

“Not fast. There's a bend.”

“I meant how fast will I have to get out.”

I glanced through the bars. The chestnut was coming toward us. Beyond its leaves were the yellow roofs of the food stalls and the striped tent where we had left our bedding. I could hear somebody practicing a drum roll badly.

“Quickly,” I said.

***

I waited until the wagon entered the bend.

The driver took it close to the chestnut, away from a cart coming up the road. Its low fork slid past the side bars, just beyond my shoulder. I reached out through the rear corner and dropped a broad loop of rope over the upward branch.

The branch held it. For one dreadful instant there was nothing between the tree and the wagon but a slack red line, trailing like something a child had lost.

The escort saw it.

He swung his horse around and drew the short blade at his belt. He was quick. He leaned from the saddle and caught the rope before it tightened, pulling it across his knife.

I dropped beside Delwin.

The blade slid. The rope remained whole.

The young man's face changed.

“Stop!” he shouted. “Stop the wagon!”

The driver hauled on the reins. The wheels were still turning downhill when the last of the slack disappeared.

There was a deep wooden crack, felt through the floor before I heard it. The latch-post split between its iron shoes. The section carrying the staple tore backward, dragging the latch with it. The padlock struck the rack with a sound like a spoon dropped into a sink.

Our door swung open.

Delwin's shoulder hit mine. The wagon jolted and stopped. Outside, the escort's horse shied away from the broken wood, and the young man had to choose between catching us and remaining in his saddle.

I chose for him.

I crawled through the opening, caught the edge of the rack and dropped beside the trunk. My right knee hit the road. Pain went up my thigh, hot enough to make everything else disappear for a breath.

Then Delwin came out after me, coat under one arm, and landed sitting down.

“Oh,” he said.

I got a fistful of his shirt and pulled. He got his feet under him. The driver was climbing off the box.

The fair entrance was six strides away.

We made perhaps four before Delwin began to shout.

“Ladies and gentlemen! An entirely unscheduled escape!”

A woman carrying a tray of hot buns turned to look. So did two men arguing over a wheelbarrow. Our drummer stopped halfway through a roll.

“Vesta?” he called.

“They locked us in that wagon,” I said. “Halvern's men. We didn't agree to go.”

The driver caught up with us at the entrance. He reached for Delwin's sleeve and found the drummer standing in the way, both drumsticks still in his hands.

“Private business,” the driver said.

“Then keep your hands private.”

Delwin climbed onto an empty crate beside the bun stall. He had recovered his breath much faster than I had.

“You may inspect the vehicle,” he announced. “An ordinary locked wagon. No concealed hinges, no assistance from within, except the considerable assistance from within.”

People laughed. More people came to find out why.

The driver looked back at the wagon. The escort had his horse under control, but still held his knife. Under the attention of thirty strangers, he put it away.

“Where's your warrant?” the woman with the buns asked.

“We were engaged to convey them.”

“Well, you've done it,” said Delwin. “This is where we asked to go.”

The drummer moved his drum from behind him to in front, making himself wider. Someone fetched the fair steward. After hearing us, he sent a runner for the town constables. I sat down on the grass because my knee had begun to shake.

The young escort approached slowly, empty hands held away from his belt. He looked at me rather than at Delwin.

“They said you'd used your magic.”

“I had.”

He looked back at the red rope stretched between the tree and the splintered post.

“Oh,” he said, in almost exactly Delwin's voice.

***

Halvern's carriage arrived while two constables were questioning the driver. The man pointed him out with such relief that I almost liked him. Halvern began by asking who had damaged his wagon.

“We'll come to that,” one of the constables said.

They asked whether we had agreed to go. They looked at the padlock, at my arm, at Delwin's wrist. Halvern was still explaining the sum he had offered when they took him to the town lockup in his own carriage, a constable sitting on either side of him.

We got our trunk back while the steward wrote down our account. The drummer helped me unhook the rope from the chestnut. I unwound it from the broken section of post and ran it through my hands. Soft, dusty, red. My knee was scraped, my arm was bruised, and there wasn't so much as a cut end to trim from the rope.

Delwin persuaded the steward to let us keep the piece of door. He said it was evidence. Then he painted the time of our evening performance on it.

We had no intention of remaining for a second night. Our things stayed packed beneath the platform, and a carter we knew agreed to take us with him when he left after the show. Halvern could have the private theater and its twelve empty chairs. We wanted the road north.

But first we had an engagement.

At dusk I checked the trestles above our platform. I checked the bar, the brackets, the knots. At midnight this would be ordinary rope again; even before then, it was only the rope.

Delwin appeared in his blue coat with the shoulders brushed and both cuffs turned properly down. He looked up at the rig, then out at the crowd. The bun woman had brought her husband. The drummer was telling the story to anyone who would listen, adding a second wagon whenever he thought I couldn't hear.

“We could do the conjuring,” I said.

Delwin touched his wrist, testing it. Then he rolled both shoulders and reached for the rope.

“You've had all the attention today.”

He went up. I followed, favoring my knee where the crowd couldn't see, and settled into the hanging loop beside him.

The piece of door stood against the front of the platform. Delwin pointed down at it, waited for the laughter to subside, and spread his hands.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said. “Our only miracle today.”

He took my hand. I gave him the signal, and we swung out together.
