# Provisional outline — Not today.

## The experience promised

The premise grants one prevention per day, chosen in thought and enacted by saying “Not today.” The later request fixes the title and makes “what is the choice, why dose it matter? what dose it cost?” the dramatic questions. I infer a pleasure in enormous power made scarce by selection: the reader should want incompatible uses, understand the chosen one, and feel what remains lost. No relationship, genre, scale, tone, or particular sacrifice is prescribed. I choose an urgent tragedy with a public rescue and a private loss; tragedy is not a requirement.

## Chosen direction

Delyth, a retired train driver, has a permanently damaged heart. For months she has spent her daily prevention on her own death. She wants her grown son Tavin's wedding in six weeks, but she also wants a winter away by herself, somewhere warm where nobody has known her all her life. She is buying that future, not finishing a last task before dying.

The chosen interpretation is one occurrence prevented that day, here counted from dawn to dawn. A train crash and Delyth dying of existing illness are independent occurrences. Preventing death does not heal past damage or prevent pain. Her heart functions between increasingly dangerous episodes; a doctor cannot promise she will survive the next one unaided. Missing a renewal does not kill her. Her routine is to choose when she wakes at first light; the whistle wakes her before this day's words.

## Possible scenes

1. On the previous day, Delyth says “Not today” for herself, then accompanies Tavin to his suit fitting. At a travel office she books a room on the southern coast for the winter and declines to fix her return date. Tavin had assumed she would stay nearby after his wedding. She enjoys correcting him. Then an episode of chest pain interrupts the outing. The doctor's warning and Tavin's decision to stay overnight establish the danger before the rescue. Delyth keeps the booking.

2. The train's warning whistle wakes her at first light. Her chest is already tight; she recognizes the beginning of another episode. A rockfall has torn out the high line visible from her window. The approaching train is braking too late. Delyth's working knowledge, not prophecy, tells her what will happen. She takes Tavin's hand and prepares the familiar thought of preserving herself. He says, “Do yours. I'll ring the railway,” and reaches for the telephone. Protecting her remains an available act.

3. Delyth sees the driver already doing everything she would do. A telephone warning cannot supply the missing distance. She wants her room by the sea; she wants this hand warm around hers tomorrow. She draws breath for herself, then changes the event she is holding in mind to the crash and says “Not today.” The whole train stops with its front hanging beyond the gap. Tavin's call becomes a call for rescue. He has not agreed to her decision, and she has not ceased wanting to live.

4. Passengers climb back through the carriages; one returns for someone who cannot walk. Delyth sees particular people surviving as Tavin summons medical help for her. The episode continues despite treatment. In fear, she reflexively tries “Not today” for herself. The words are only words. She still cooperates with the doctor and asks how long until morning. Her rescue has not made her serene about its cost.

5. She dies that afternoon. No new charge, cure, or recovered bargain arrives. In a brief Tavin coda, passengers' messages reach the house while he cancels the room she chose and her next suit-fitting appointment. Their saved lives continue; her wanted winter never begins. The final image remains provisional, but returning railway service will not provide an inspiring conclusion or make his gratitude settle the loss.

## Open concern for discussion

The options need not have equal arithmetic. They must have felt weight: Delyth acts on her independent future, begins the crisis intending to protect it, chooses the passengers herself, then still fights for survival. The prose must preserve those actions rather than replace them with an explanation that she has completed her life.
