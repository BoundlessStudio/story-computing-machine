# Not today.

## The experience promised

The explicit request gives one prevention each day, activated by thinking of the event and saying “Not today.” It asks what the choice is, why it matters, and what it costs; the later corrections require a genuinely new, more fantastical story with a younger protagonist who is only beginning to learn that cost. I infer a pleasure in exhilarating defiance followed by consequential discovery: an apparently small refusal can interrupt something immense, but deciding what to stop changes the life that follows. The user specifies no exact age, relationship, scale, setting, moral, or kind of ending. The adventurous tone, sibling relationship, three-day shape, and separation below are writer choices, not requirements.

## Provisional direction

Thirteen-year-old Cirel has finally been allowed aboard the skyship where her sixteen-year-old brother Pellin is a navigator's apprentice. She wants his blue flying coat and the future they have been planning together: their own small ship, with Pellin sailing and Cirel charting places neither has seen. Before the hunt, they practice together in the ship's skiff. He takes her deliberately through a cloud to test her bearings; she recognizes their home by its upside-down reflection and brings them out facing its kitchen windows. They argue cheerfully over who gets to name a newly discovered island, then share the food their mother wrapped for both. Pellin has saved her half of something she always steals from his plate. Home is wanted life, not just the captors' headquarters. Larkglass is a city built under a living cloud-reef; its ships sail on breath taken from sky-whales. The siblings have been taught that the whales are magnificent animals which must be managed. What the whales do will complicate that belief.

Cirel's new gift can prevent one understood occurrence between one dawn and the next. Thinking and the words are sufficient; she needs no prop, bargain, or exact legal phrasing. An expended refusal cannot be reassigned, and a later one cannot undo an event which has already happened. These are the proposed local interpretations of the daily premise. Its costs here are lost alternatives and consequences, not life-energy, mandatory death, or a universal magical price. Cirel will begin with only an uncertain family account of the gift and no practiced command of it.

The chosen dramatic center is Cirel discovering that making a spectacular thing possible does not give her control of everything which follows. She first expects the gift to earn the future she wants. Then she expects another day's gift to put a mistake right. Finally she uses it while knowing which wanted future it cannot also preserve. Her brother matters because he is part of that future, but her decisions cause the journey and determine its climax.

## Concrete movement

1. **The first day: a rescue she thinks is a triumph.** This expedition is timed for the reef's nineteen-year flowering, which opens the whales' migration passage. Pellin will be thirty-five at the next opening; Cirel treats that age as absurd. A newborn whale blunders into an adult capture-net. The mesh drains the breath which holds a sky-whale's body together; it is meant to subdue an adult, but the small calf begins coming apart into rain. Cirel pictures that dissolution and says “Not today.” The calf becomes solid under her hand. Its restored weight tears the net loose, ending the draining, and she is suddenly riding a blue, house-sized creature between ropes and sailors tumbling into the ship's safety webs. The calf, Orro, deliberately shields Pellin from a falling spar. Cirel expects praise for saving a valuable catch and acquiring a remarkable friend. Instead Orro leaves with the ship's living wind-bell, a small winged creature whose call keeps the fleet together. Cirel tries to prevent the bell's departure, discovers that her day's answer has been spent, and watches the fleet scatter. The captain cancels **Cirel's** trial navigation berth, which Pellin had vouched for. Pellin keeps his apprenticeship; his trust and their shared plan have taken a real knock. The ship can travel without the bell, but it must find the others by sight. Removing the draining net has ended the calf's immediate danger, so its survival does not require a renewed refusal tomorrow.

2. **The second day: a spectacular solution creates a new exposure.** Cirel takes the skiff to bring Orro and the bell back, imagining that restitution will restore her berth. Pellin catches up, furious, and takes the tiller. They find the creatures inside a hollow cloud-reef. When Cirel gives the familiar ship-call, the bell comes to her hand affectionately; it then pulls the carrying tether from her pocket, drops it into the water, and returns to Orro. It was not stolen. It wants to leave, though it likes her. This changes Cirel's aim from recovering the expedition's property to helping the two escape. A hunter blocks the reef's lower mouth. The upper opening is at the top of a sheer shaft filled with falling water and a powerful downdraft; the skiff's sail folds and Orro's attempt to rise drives it back down. Cirel pictures the waterfall falling and says “Not today.” The water remains liquid but hangs in one immense still column. Orro can now **swim** up through it, carrying the children and towing their skiff by its mooring rope while the bell clings above its breathing-hole. They ascend through silver fish and roots. The waterfall's normal spray has hidden the shaft; without the spray its clear column and their climbing silhouettes shine like a signal to the fleet. Cirel's one refusal has bought an exit and exposed it. She cannot also stop the hunters finding them. They flee toward the migration passage. Pellin is helping; he has not agreed to abandon home.

3. **The third day: she has a plan for using the refusal to get home.** At dawn the suspended waterfall falls again behind them. At the migration passage, Orro rejoins its mother among whales carrying luminous young inside their translucent bodies. The living reef's flowering folds a far country of the sky close enough to cross; closing will separate those countries for nineteen years. This is the known migration the fleet came to intercept. Both siblings still have their skiff and a way back to their ship. Then a thrown capture-line catches Orro against its mother's head. Cirel boards the mother to release it while Pellin maneuvers the skiff behind. She tells him her concrete plan: if the passage begins to close, she will stop it, and he can follow through, collect her, and take them home. He agrees. The mother has carried Cirel just beyond the far face when the opening begins contracting. Pellin is visible in the skiff on the homeward side, turning to come through for her. Cirel prepares to think of the closing. Only then does the fleet swing its great harpoon into line behind him. The mother is turned broadside to shelter Orro, still fouled in the last loop of the capture-line; she cannot turn clear before the shot. The iron head threatens an immediate wound. The gun's crew engage the recovery winch: **holding the passage open would also give the hunters the time to haul the struck mother back.** Cirel's plan would keep a route open for both her rescue and the capture. A few more moments would let Pellin reach Cirel and return; they cannot make that trip before the opening closes naturally. Her planned refusal is a genuinely available way home. It will not also stop the harpoon. The harpoon-ship is out of the children's reach, and Orro cannot fly her across the gap in the remaining moments.

4. **She spends the refusal somewhere else.** Cirel starts to assure Pellin that she will hold the way open. Then she sees Orro try to shield its mother as it shielded Pellin. The harpoon launches. Cirel turns her thought from the closing to the weapon entering the mother's body and says “Not today.” The immense iron point comes to rest against empty air. Cirel cuts the calf's last fouling loop, completing the task she boarded to do; the mother is free to follow the migration. Across the narrowing opening, Pellin can see what Cirel has chosen to prevent. Cirel waves him back and calls for him to tell their mother she is alive. He turns the skiff home in response to that choice, rather than leaving independently before it. Cirel watches the passage close between them. She has saved the creatures and spent the one thing that could also have kept her own route open. Her climb created the predicament; this refusal decides which future happens. No talisman, second spell, restored berth, or surprise shortcut refunds the separation.

5. **An ending still inside the wonder.** Beyond the passage, the whales unfold the cloud-cities they carry for their own young and the people who travel with them. The bell leads Cirel toward shelter, where she can receive help without immediately acquiring a replacement family or rank. She asks whether the whales can go back early. They cannot make the reef flower early. She cries, angry with herself and with the answer, while an entire living city rises around her. Its delight is genuine: she keeps seeing things she wants to show Pellin. At the next dawn she still has the power. She does not finish with a maxim, a vow never to use it, or a domestic task: the ending leaves her looking at the astonishing country she has helped reach, with his empty place beside her and another refusal that is hers to choose.

## Possibility and risk to test

Protect the progression from delighted impossible rescue, through a novice's failed idea of restitution, to a deliberate impossible liberation. The waterfall must be action with consequences, not scenery. The strongest risk is that the separation could become an automatic sentimental punishment for kindness or be cancelled emotionally by the reward of a grander adventure. The draft must make the wanted life with Pellin real before the hunt, let him choose home without villainizing him, and keep both the new world's delight and the irretrievable loss present. The capture fleet is pressure, not a substitute protagonist; no adult explains the final answer to Cirel.
