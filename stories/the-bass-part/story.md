Sefra, the first thing I did to get that elephant for a familiar was try to get her to go away. I had a show to put on. When a woman brings her own crate to stand on, and pays two cousins to carry it up the Whitewheel ramp in the heat, she expects to be the largest thing in the view.

You’d given me the last act at the Clay Fair. The chairs faced my crate and the clay wall behind it. The pears on the cart beside the front row were beginning to smell cooked. I’d talked you into chalking my act up on the board just as I pictured it. I’d ring my little bell at the fair and the sound would come from the ridge on the far side of Whitewheel, two miles off where the sun goes down. I wanted to see everybody turn in their chairs at once. We sometimes hear the clay carts up there when the air cools. A single rattle of wheels would give me a place to put a note.

There was a cart up there already, just visible in the heat at the top of the ridge, and I waited till it came round the bend where one wheel always goes over the broken stone. A puff of dust came up beside the wheel and I heard no rattle. I rang the bell. The sound stayed in my hand. I’d have settled for a hare kicking a pebble.

You came by with a bowl you’d just bought. I heard you scraping at the price on the bottom of it and rang my bell again. This time I could put the sound right inside the bowl. You clapped a hand over it as if you had a fly to keep in there. “Three families have paid for good seats, Tulla, and one of those families has nine people in it. I’ve told them all where to look when you ring your bell. If it’s going to ring someplace else I have to pick them up and put them right one at a time.”

“Keep your nine people where they are,” I said. “They’ll hear it come off that ridge even if they’re looking straight down at their feet.” I thought it was best to say it while you still had your hand on the bowl.

“Next year, get yourself a cat,” you said. “I can put a cat on one of the clay carts without anybody having to make room. I’d send it round the bend half an hour before you had to start.”

The cat at the next stall was asleep in his witch’s cashbox, which she’d lined with a sleeve off an old coat. She scratched a finger along the rim and waited for him to stretch and touch his nose to her finger. Then he slipped into the potter’s sample room. The potter went in to tap the bowls and drew the shutters closed. I could hear the little wooden catch. The cat’s witch stood beside my crate and listened through her familiar’s ears. “A crack,” she said, and the potter came out with his bowl in both hands to show us the crack, which was the length of his thumbnail. I suppose the advantage of giving a performance with a cat is that when he decides to go to sleep, you have somebody to blame.

I never saw Mallow come up the ramp. You can tell the size of a great many things by the sound they make when they walk. An elephant comes in so quietly that I was still listening for the carts a long way off when there were two little grunts at my shoulder and warm breath all along my bare arm. Her cheek was level with my face, and it took the crate to get me that high. A fly got halfway up one of the folds, white with clay dust, and slid back when she blinked.

I wanted the view back. I could hear the pears settling in their box, so I put the two grunts there. Mallow set one foot, then the other, delicately around the corner of my crate. She had the stem of a pear pinched in the tip of her trunk when she turned and looked up at me. I gave her the grunts again, one in the pears and the other in a wicker basket the potter was taking down off a hook, just to see if I could make her turn her head twice. She took such pains over it that I felt it would be very rude to send her away.

She set the pear back among the others and faced me squarely. When she gave the two sounds again, I could see the loose curl at the end of her trunk open and close with each breath. She stood there while I remembered I had a bell in my hand. I rang it twice, waiting between strokes about as long as she had, with the sound right where I stood. She stayed looking at me for both notes. You would ordinarily need a whole row of people to give a woman so much attention.

At dusk I left my little bell on the crate and came to stand with you beneath the beam over the wide ramp. You gave one ring of the fair bell to call people to their places and were pulling for another when a trunk went past the top of my head. It felt along the lip of the bell and caught the clapper. I heard the clapper give one little click against the bronze.

The rope would come no farther.

“I get two rings, Mallow. One for the people who are already up here and one for the people who are still buying things to eat. You’re welcome to go down and wait if you have an objection to the second one.”

Mallow held on another second. Then she let go and went down. At that point I was willing to believe you could tell an elephant what to do. I started back for my crate, but she stopped midway down the ramp where she could still see me. I thought I’d give her the two grunts again to show her I could do it without the pears. Before I could find a good place to put them she turned away and went on. I followed to see what she’d found down there that she liked better.

The ramp ends on a long shelf of black stone with two miles of open basin in front of it. The high clay wall that gave us our echoes was above and behind us. The heat came through the soles of my shoes on that shelf, though the air was cooling and I’d begun to smell the burnt crust on the bread they sold at the fair. Mallow picked up one front foot so that only the very edge of it touched the rock. Her trunk hung straight down. From where I stood the evening was just as full of noises as it had been a minute ago. I could hear three different potters tapping their wares behind me, each trying to prove his bowl was the one with a ring to it. But I’d watched Mallow take the trouble to listen to me, and I stood quietly beside her awhile to see if I could do as much.

She was facing the distant saddle where the cart road goes over the lip. There was dust moving there, in thin upright strokes that took me a little while to see as legs. The animals kept coming out of the light below the saddle and going over it in single file. I counted six and then one so small that I only saw it because the dark bulk behind it stopped to let it through. Mallow set her foot down and the sound she made then started below my own feet. It was a low continuous note. As it gathered, the loose skin on her throat began to shake and the heels of my shoes buzzed against the rock. She finished and stood as she’d stood before, waiting. I could hear you saying something to the man with the trays of bread. I couldn’t hear a thing from the saddle.

When she called again I tried to take the sound as far out across the basin as I could. A boy a short way down the shelf was scraping the toe of his shoe over a loose stone. I could hear every scrape, but that was as far as I could go. Mallow’s voice came out next to the boy’s shoe and he jumped. Mallow went on looking across the basin. I never heard so much as a stone kicked loose over there, though I could see the dust falling from those animals’ legs.

I went back as far as the foot of the ramp. You’d taken a turn of the bell rope round your hand to make it clear whose second ring it was. I called up to you and asked you to get the potters to stop tapping for a minute and have the people in the good seats turn around.

“I gave my own chair to the woman with nine seats so they could all sit together. Her youngest put his hands over his ears when I rang the fair bell. I had to tell him yours was little before he’d take them down. What shall I tell them they’re staying for now?”

I could still have asked you for five minutes and waited for the carts, and if anybody doubted it would work I could have told them in some considerable detail how this was an exceptionally poor evening for any kind of sound. My name was up there on the board. If a cart came round the bend right then, I could run up and ring my bell and get down to Mallow before the last of the herd went over. Another pair of backs disappeared while I was thinking it through. Mallow stood out on the shelf with the edge of her foot on the stone. I thought of the cat at the cashbox and how his witch had waited for him to come out.

“Tell them I’ve called off the bell,” I said. I could see the faces of some of the people just behind you. They’d come past my crate two or three times to ask whether I’d be able to do it. “I want to see if I can get those elephants to turn. I’ll need everybody to look across the basin and keep still a little while. Tell them it was me who asked.”

You looked past me, down to where Mallow was standing. Then you took your hand off the rope. “I’ll ask the potters. If those nine want me to tell them what’s happening, I’m pointing them down your end of the ramp.” I’d been waiting for you to raise your voice. You went straight to the potter with the cracked bowl and put a hand flat over the mouth of it. The men in the stalls did what you did, and the quiet went from one table of bowls to the next. Somebody tipped a chair round on two legs, making a great noise in the middle of it, and after that everybody began lifting the chairs to turn them.

Mallow had come partway up the ramp. I thought she was headed for the bell again, but she lowered her head when I came down to her and walked beside me back to the stone. She felt the crook of my arm where I’d carried the little bell all afternoon. When she found my hands empty she stayed so close that her ear brushed my shoulder.

“I’d like another turn like we had by the pears,” I said. “Sometime when there’s nobody out there you need to get hold of. Would you be my familiar, Mallow? I’ve never asked before. I can put your call right under your family’s feet if you’ll let me hear them. You could listen through my ears whenever you liked. You could call for me on a day when all you wanted was to stand someplace with me awhile. I’ll come out to where you are.”

I set my hand out where she could see it. She ran the end of her trunk right across the palm. The bristles in it felt like the little ends of a string I’d cut with my teeth. I pulled my hand back to see if that was all she wanted with it. There was a curve of white clay across my palm. She reached past my hand to my wrist and held it lightly while she set her front foot on the stone again.

I felt every place she was touching the ground. As she shifted her weight I could have pointed to all four of those places in the rock with my eyes shut. Then, a long way down under the skin of the stone, I felt other feet. There were so many of them. I put my left toes down as Mallow had and could feel the big animals making a space in their walking that kept closing and opening. Something small kept catching up between them. Then there was a voice with a little rise halfway through it, and the whole of Mallow moved toward that sound without her feet going anywhere. The voice called again. I could feel the moment it took a breath. A pot knocked against another pot behind me and Mallow turned her ear toward it, as sharply as I had turned mine. She’d heard it from where I stood.

I wanted to put a sound all the way out there the instant I heard where the herd was, and I nearly said something to start her off. Mallow was still listening. I had to wait through the answer of another animal, low and unhurried, until there was room for the breath she took. When her own call began, I set it down in the stone just behind the last feet in the line. I could feel where they stopped. Above us it was quiet enough to hear a clay cart at last, its wheels jarring on the far road with the very sound I’d wanted for the bell. The voice with the little rise came back so hard it made my knees bend.

I looked up and saw the dark backs coming in our direction, some of them broadside to us while the rest were still turning. Mallow called again and I brought that call nearer along the shelf. By the time the lamps came on in the stalls the calves had got ahead of the big ones, trotting in the cool with their trunks up, and the big ones had begun to trumpet. The sound came over the heads of the people who’d been expecting my little bell. Two bowls on the nearest table hummed together until the potter put his thumb between them. I heard somebody laugh and a child ask to be set down to feel it.

The elephant in front drew close enough for me to see the tips of her ears shine where she’d rubbed them thin. I stepped toward the ramp to give Mallow plenty of room to go to her.

I felt the warmth of her breath across the back of my arm. When I turned she was looking straight at me. She made her two grunts with the same space between them as when she’d stood at the foot of my crate. My hand went to my side for my bell. I could just see it glinting on the crate, past all the chairs that had turned. I had time to go and get it if I went quickly. When I looked back at her I’d let more time go by than it had taken me to ring for her that afternoon, and she was still waiting. I had taken enough breath to call up to you for it. Half the front row would have turned round if I’d called with that much in me. I let the breath go slowly until I had just enough left for Mallow to hear me beside her. I said her name. It came out in a voice I’d have been ashamed to have put in a bowl, and she stayed until I got the whole of it out.

Then she went down to her family.

The chairs are still facing the basin this morning. You pull your own out of the first row and bring it round beside my crate.

“The boy who was so worried about the bells came up to me after,” you say. “He wanted to know if you’d do the same thing again tonight. I told him I could promise the same nine seats and he asked for a place down on the stone. I’ve half a mind to go down there with him myself, if I can get somebody else to mind the bell.”

I’ll show you the place where we stood. Wait a breath, Sefra. I can hear her now.

You turn your head. The sound in the air is the bread man folding the cloth that goes over his trays. I’m hearing two little notes through the stone under the ramp. She’s well past the saddle with some of the other elephants. I can feel one of the small ones scruffing around somebody’s feet in the dust. Mallow is standing with all her weight still.

I say her name up here and send it out over the basin. There. I feel the change in her feet when she starts for me. I leave my crate where it is and take the wide ramp. We can meet in the middle.
