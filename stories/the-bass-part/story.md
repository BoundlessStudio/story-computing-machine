Sefra, the first thing I did to get that elephant for a familiar was try to get her to go away. I had a show to put on. When a woman brings her own crate to stand on, and pays two cousins to carry it up the Whitewheel ramp in the heat, she expects to be the largest thing in the view.

You’d given me the last act at the Clay Fair. I’d talked you into chalking it up on the board just as I pictured it. I’d ring my little bell at the fair and the sound would come from the ridge on the far side of Whitewheel, two miles off where the sun goes down. We sometimes hear the clay carts up there when the air cools. A single rattle of wheels would give me a place to put a note.

I could see a cart up there already. I waited for it to come round the bend, where a wheel always runs over a bit of broken stone. The man driving stood up to see it past. I saw the wheel jar, but nothing reached me. When I rang the bell the sound stayed in my hand. I’d have settled for a hare kicking a pebble. I could see the heat shake the very top of the ridge.

You came by with a bowl you’d just bought. I heard you scraping at the price on the bottom of it and rang my bell again. This time I could put the sound right inside the bowl. You clapped a hand over it as if you had a fly to keep in there. “Three families have paid for good seats, Tulla, and one of those families has nine people in it. I’ve told them all where to look to hear your bell. If it’s going to ring someplace else I have to pick them up and put them right one at a time.”

“Keep your nine people where they are,” I said. “They’ll hear it come off that ridge even if they’re looking straight down at their feet.” I thought it was best to say it while you still had your hand on the bowl.

You said I’d do better with a cat. The cat at the next stall was asleep in his witch’s cashbox, which she’d lined with a sleeve off an old coat. She scratched a finger along the rim and waited for him to stretch and touch his nose to her finger. Then he slipped into the potter’s sample room. The potter went in to tap the bowls and drew the shutters closed. I could hear the little wooden catch. The woman stood beside my crate and listened through her familiar’s ears. “A crack,” she said, and the potter came out with his bowl in both hands to show us the crack, which was the length of his thumbnail. I suppose the advantage of giving a performance with a cat is that when he decides to go to sleep, you have somebody to blame.

The pear cart stood a little beyond the front row. I never saw Mallow come up the ramp. You can tell the size of a great many things by the sound they make when they walk. An elephant comes in so quietly that I was still listening for the carts a long way off when there were two little grunts at my shoulder and warm breath all along my bare arm. Her cheek was level with my face, and it took the crate to get me that high. A fly got halfway up one of the chalk-white folds and slid back when she blinked.

I wanted the view back. I could hear the pears settling in their box, so I put the two grunts there. Mallow set one foot, then the other, delicately around the corner of my crate. She had the stem of a pear pinched in the tip of her trunk when she turned and looked up at me. I gave her the grunts again, one in the pears and the other in a wicker basket the potter was taking down off a hook, just to see if I could make her turn her head twice. She took such pains over it that I felt it would be very rude to send her away. She set the pear back among the others and faced me squarely. She made her two sounds and waited. I rang my own bell twice, about as far apart as her grunts, with the sound right where I stood. She stayed looking at me for both notes. You would ordinarily need a whole row of people to give a woman so much attention.

At dusk I left my little bell on the crate and came to stand with you by the fair bell. It hangs from a beam at the top of the ramp, high enough for a whole elephant to pass under it. You gave one ring to call people to their places and were pulling for another when a trunk went past the top of my head. It felt along the lip of the bell and caught the clapper. I heard the clapper give one little click against the bronze. The rope would come no farther.

“I get two rings, Mallow. One for the people who are already up here and one for the people who are still buying things to eat. You’re welcome to go down and wait if you have an objection to the second one.”

Mallow held on another second. Then she let go and went down. At that point I was willing to believe you could tell an elephant what to do. I started back for my crate, but she stopped midway down the ramp where she could still see me. I thought I’d give her the two grunts again to show her I could do it without the pears. Before I could find a good place to put them she turned away and went on, and I followed her to see what she wanted me to hear.

The ramp ends on a long shelf of black stone with two miles of open basin in front of it. The high clay wall that gave us our echoes was above and behind us. The heat came through the soles of my shoes on that shelf, though the air was cooling and I’d begun to smell the burnt crust on the bread they sold at the fair. Mallow picked up one front foot so that only the very edge of it touched the rock. Her trunk hung straight down. From where I stood the evening was just as full of noises as it had been a minute ago. I could hear three different potters tapping their wares behind me, each trying to prove his bowl was the one with a ring to it. But I’d watched Mallow take the trouble to listen to me, and I stood quietly beside her awhile to see if I could do as much.

She was facing the distant saddle where the cart road goes over the lip. There was dust moving there, in thin upright strokes that took me a little while to see as legs. The animals kept coming out of the light below the saddle and going over it in single file. I counted six and then one so small that I only saw it because the dark bulk behind it stopped to let it through. Mallow set her foot down and the sound she made then started below my own feet. It was a low continuous note. The longer it went on the more of her seemed to be in it. She finished and stood as she’d stood before, waiting. I could hear you saying something to the man with the trays of bread. I couldn’t hear a thing from the saddle.

When she called again I tried to take the sound as far out across the basin as I could. A boy on the shelf, a little way down from us, was scraping the toe of his shoe over a loose stone. I could hear every scrape, but that was as far as I could go. Mallow’s voice came out next to the boy’s shoe and he jumped, and Mallow went on looking across the basin. The far side of it, for all that I could see the dust coming down off those animals’ legs, was quiet as a painted window.

I went back as far as the foot of the ramp. You’d taken a turn of the bell rope round your hand to make it clear whose second ring it was. I called up to you and asked you to get the potters to stop tapping for a minute and have the people in the good seats turn around.

“I gave my own chair to the woman with nine seats so they could all sit together. Her youngest put his hands over his ears when I rang the fair bell. I had to tell him yours was little before he’d take them down. What shall I tell them they’re staying for now?”

I could still have asked you for five minutes and waited for the carts, and if anybody doubted it would work I could have told them in some considerable detail how this was an exceptionally poor evening for any kind of sound. The dust over the saddle was getting thin. I looked back at Mallow on the shelf and thought of the cat putting his nose to his witch’s finger before he went to listen. I’d liked it when Mallow gave me the two notes after we’d finished with the pears, and then waited for mine. I wanted to hear her do it again. First I had to find out what she heard when she stood there so still, while there were still elephants on this side of the saddle.

“Tell them I’ve called off the bell,” I said. I could see the faces of some of the people just behind you. They’d come past my crate two or three times to ask whether I’d be able to do it. “I want to see if I can get those elephants to turn. I’ll need everybody to look across the basin and keep still a little while. Tell them it was me who asked.”

You looked past me, down to where Mallow was standing. Then you took your hand off the rope. “I can give you a little while. I’ll start with the men who are doing the tapping and see how far I get.” You went straight to the potter with the cracked bowl and put a hand flat over the mouth of it. I’d expected a great calling to order, but the men in the stalls did what you did, and the quiet went from one table of bowls to the next. Somebody tipped a chair round on two legs, making a great noise in the middle of it, and after that everybody began lifting the chairs to turn them.

Mallow had come partway up the ramp. I thought she was headed for the bell again, but she lowered her head when I came down to her and walked beside me back to the stone. She felt the crook of my arm where I’d carried the little bell all afternoon. When she found my hands empty she stayed so close that her ear brushed my shoulder.

“Will you be my familiar, Mallow?” I said. “I can send your call if you let me hear where your family is. And you can listen through my ears whenever you like. I’d like you to call for me again, even if it’s just for those two notes at the pear cart, or if all you want is to stand someplace with me for a while. I’ll come out to where you are.”

I set my hand out where she could see it. She ran the end of her trunk right across the palm. The bristles in it felt like the little ends of a string I’d cut with my teeth. I pulled my hand back to see if that was all she wanted with it. She reached past my hand to my wrist and held it lightly while she set her front foot on the stone again.

I felt every place she was touching the ground. At first there was a little shifting sensation as she got her weight settled, four separate places in the rock I could have pointed to with my eyes shut. Then, a long way down under the skin of the stone, I felt other feet. There were so many of them. I put my left toes down as Mallow had, and could feel the big animals making a space in their walking that kept closing and opening. There was something small catching up between them. Then there was a voice with a little rise halfway through it, and the whole of Mallow moved toward that sound without her feet going anywhere. The voice called again. I could feel the moment it took a breath. A pot knocked against another pot behind me and Mallow turned her ear toward it, as sharply as I had turned mine. She’d heard it from where I stood.

I wanted to put a sound all the way out there the instant I heard where the herd was, and I nearly said something to start her off. Mallow was still listening. I had to wait through the answer of another animal, low and unhurried, until there was room for the breath she took. When her own call began, I set it down in the stone just behind the last feet in the line. I could feel where they stopped. Above us it was quiet enough to hear a clay cart at last, its wheels jarring on the far road above the animals. Down in the stone everything was happening at once. The voice with the little rise came back so hard it made my knees bend.

I looked up and saw the dark backs coming in our direction, some of them broadside to us while the rest were still turning. Mallow called again and I brought that call nearer along the shelf. By the time the lamps came on in the stalls the calves had got ahead of the big ones, trotting in the cool with their trunks up, and the big ones had begun to trumpet. The sound came over the heads of the people who’d been expecting my little bell. Two bowls on the nearest table hummed together until the potter put his thumb between them. I heard somebody laugh and a child ask to be set down to feel it.

The elephant in front drew close enough for me to see the tips of her ears shine where she’d rubbed them thin. I got out of the way so Mallow could go to her. There was plenty of room on the shelf, but I felt I ought to give her some more, after making up my mind so quickly that I knew what she wanted. I took a step toward the ramp.

I felt the warmth of her breath across the back of my arm. When I turned she was looking straight at me. She made her two little grunts with the same space between them as when she’d stood at the foot of my crate. My hand went to my side for my bell. I could just see it up there between two legs of a chair. There was time to go and get it if I went quickly. By the time I looked at her again I’d kept her waiting longer than it had taken to answer her by the pears, and she was still waiting. My mouth had gone dry. I said her name. It came out in a voice I’d have been ashamed to have put in a bowl, and she stayed until I got the whole of it out. Then she went down to her family.

“The boy who was so worried about the bells came up to me after,” you say. “He wanted to know if you’d do the same thing again tonight. I told him I could promise the same nine seats and he asked for a place down on the stone. I’ve half a mind to go down there with him myself, if I can get somebody else to mind the bell.”

Wait a breath, Sefra. I can hear her now.

You turn your head. This morning the sound in the air is the bread man folding the cloth that goes over his trays. I’m hearing two little notes through the stone under the ramp. She’s way beyond the saddle with some of the other elephants. I can feel one of the small ones scruffing around somebody’s feet in the dust. Mallow is standing with all her weight still.

I say her name up here and send it out over the basin. There. I feel the change in her feet when she starts for me. I leave my crate where it is and take the wide ramp. We can meet in the middle.
