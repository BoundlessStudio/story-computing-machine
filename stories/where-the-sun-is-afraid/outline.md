# The First Thing the Sun Asked

## Promised experience

The request explicitly makes stars living minds, their golden light physical thought, and a young woman's brilliant bodily spirals the means of direct communication. Her first contact with the Sun brings a desperate plea about a future human act. I infer tactile wonder turning into urgent discovery: the familiar daylight becomes an immense, vulnerable somebody, and what she learns must change what she can do. The later direction asks for a prequel to existing Sun stories, particularly the vampires' theft. It leaves the exact danger, era, relationship beyond first contact, outcome, length, and voice open. The new encounter should satisfy on its own while meaningfully preparing that later history.

## Provisional direction

Close third person, roughly 4,500–5,500 words, following Iona, a young woman at the observatory that has recently proved starlight is thought. The main relationship is her difficult first exchange with the Sun. Ordan, still mortal, presses the human wish to keep living. An early age of great lenses and sea travel precedes *What the Sun Is Owed* without fixing a shared chronology. These are writer choices, not added user requirements.

- In the opening, Iona discovers that winding a star's golden light around her arms lets her answer it. She wants to make this contact shareable. At dawn she attempts the Sun for the first time. The spirals pull tight against her body. A desperate voice begs her to stop tomorrow's taking of its unborn stars. It recognizes the collectors' purpose through its own light folded inside their apparatus; no infallible prophecy is needed.
- The mirrored Cradle is scheduled to draw two seeds out of the Sun's light into separate dark cups. Seeds are small physical condensations within a star's thought, not yet awakened stars. The public plan is to keep them asleep until the operators learn to wake them safely and speak with them. Concentrated light already renews injured flesh; Ordan, who helped build the Cradle, wants that renewal to last. When Iona brings the warning, he argues for postponing the seeds' awakening rather than abandoning the capture. He is afraid of dying, not secretly serving another power.
- Iona reaches into the Sun's thoughts and asks other stars about their beginnings. They make her experience what confinement would withhold: not only speech, but the immense outward life an awakened star can have. She has helped promise an accessible conversation; the machine would purchase it by preventing the interlocutors from becoming themselves. The Sun, terrified that she will not act in time, asks her to open a path through the collector so it can destroy the apparatus, regardless of who remains inside. She refuses that method but commits herself to getting the seeds out.
- Ordan proceeds with the capture at the appointed hour. Iona enters the operating Cradle, opens her spirals to its crew, and lets them hear the Sun's plea directly. Sharing contact has consequences: several operators choose to open the confinement despite the danger and Ordan's orders, while Ordan, hearing the same mind, chooses to keep a seed. The two cups and the two seeds remain physically accounted for. Iona lifts one seed into an uncoiling spiral; Ordan takes the other cup's smooth black seed and holds it beneath his tongue. She sees him do it.
- The rescue and theft now collide. The Sun drives a fierce current of thought down the open collector toward Ordan and the crew around him, begging Iona to hold him there. She could close her spiral around him, but doing so would surrender those people to the Sun and collapse the birth route she is shaping. She deliberately releases that hold, diverts the current from the hall, and keeps the route open. Ordan escapes through the evacuating crew. Her refusal costs the return of the second seed; it does not make the first rescue fail. With the other stars helping her find a distant depth, she carries the freed seed's awakening out of the crowded world. Her body, the living light, and conflicting stellar and human wills govern the climax.
- The operators who helped her dismantle this Cradle rather than reset it. This attempt and this project end; the story makes no claim that future humans cannot try again. One star has awakened freely, the people in the hall survive, and one unborn star has been stolen. Iona has actively chosen among incompatible outcomes. Ordan has not been taught that a transformed human can remain a person inside a star, and his stolen seed remains asleep for the later story.
- In their last exchange, the Sun still wants the missing seed and is angry that she let its bearer go. Iona does not pretend she fulfilled its whole plea. She offers continued contact without giving it possession of her hands or other people's bodies. The Sun, which first treated her as the one available way to stop the taking, chooses to let her hear the freed star's first answer rather than close itself against her. Iona opens her spirals to the survivors so that answer belongs to more than herself. The theft remains a wound within this ending; the last experience is her changed relationship with living daylight and a conversation she has made possible.

The awakening deliberately anticipates the later story's birth motif, but its action and moral pressure differ: Iona shapes an escape while refusing an indiscriminate recovery; Neris later surrenders the original stolen seed and the borrowed renewal. No public knowledge-loss plot is required by the existing prose's questions about whether a converted human remains a person.
