# The First Thing the Sun Asked

## Promised experience

The request explicitly makes stars living minds, their golden light physical thought, and a young woman's brilliant bodily spirals the means of direct communication. Her first contact with the Sun brings a desperate plea about a future human act. I infer tactile wonder turning into urgent discovery: the familiar daylight becomes an immense, vulnerable somebody, and what she learns must change what she can do. The later direction asks for a prequel to existing Sun stories, particularly the vampires' theft. It leaves the exact danger, era, relationship beyond first contact, outcome, length, and voice open. The new encounter should satisfy on its own while meaningfully preparing that later history.

## Provisional direction

Close third person, roughly 3,500–4,500 words, following Iona, a young woman at the observatory that has recently proved that starlight is thought. The main relationship is her difficult first exchange with the Sun; the human counterpressure is Ordan, still mortal, who sees in the same discovery a means of keeping people alive. An early age of great lenses and sea travel will precede *What the Sun Is Owed* without fixing a shared chronology. These are writer choices, not added user requirements.

- Iona has discovered privately that she can catch starlight, wind its thoughts around her arms, and answer. During the observatory's demonstration she attempts the Sun for the first time. The spirals pull tight against her body. A desperate voice begs: tomorrow the humans will catch an unborn star and keep it from waking; she must stop them. The Sun knows the collectors' purpose through the light already folded inside their apparatus, rather than through an unexplained infallible prophecy.
- The great mirrored Cradle is publicly intended to give humanity a star it can speak with at arm's length. Ordan has learned that the concentrated living fire renews injured flesh. He wants to keep that renewal available; he is frightened of dying and can name people it would save. Iona wants the first conversation to belong to more than herself. Neither desire requires the other to be foolish. The Sun's warning changes her understanding of a project she helped celebrate.
- Reaching farther into the Sun's thoughts, Iona encounters the dark, sleeping seeds whose eventual awakening makes new stars. The Cradle does not create one: it pulls one out and prevents its birth. Through her night contacts she confirms that the existing stars remember such beginnings. The revelation turns a dispute about useful light into the threatened captivity of a future mind. The Sun asks for the apparatus to be destroyed and is willing to endanger its operators; she refuses to surrender the means of stopping it to either side.
- At the next day's attempted capture, Iona enters the Cradle. Her spirals physically uncoil the compressed thoughts and interrupt the confinement. The Sun acts through the opened path, gathering its stolen fire; she has to shape that passage so the awakening happens in the far sky rather than inside the crowded observatory. The other stellar minds help her find that depth. The decisive event is a dangerous act of direct communication and embodied stellar power, not a convincing speech. A new star becomes visible in daylight, and the Cradle cannot be used again.
- Ordan has taken one still-sleeping seed during the breach. Iona discovers the absence too late: he has gone, carrying the smooth black thing beneath his tongue. Her success ends the organized taking of unborn stars, but it has not stopped one frightened man from keeping another morning. The closing exchange stays with her and the Sun: it recognizes its borrowed fire somewhere beyond her reach and begins to call its bearer's name. The missing seed remains unwoken for the later story, and neither Iona nor the Sun supplies Ordan with knowledge of the later vampires' possible stellar transformation.

The ending should leave the first contact consequential, the prevented mass captivity real, and the future theft an unresolved wound rather than cancel the later story. If the double outcome makes Ordan's escape feel like a detached sequel hook, revise the conflict before drafting; the human wish to keep the seed must already be active in the Cradle scenes.
