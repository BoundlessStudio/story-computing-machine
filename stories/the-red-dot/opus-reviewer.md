# Opus review: The Red Dot

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the speaker is unrecoverable at both leverage pivots ("Then tell it.", "That's mine too."); the rest of the dialogue is grounded and the voices differ.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, funny, well staged overall; one muddled route and a few "it" collisions between cat and dot.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt image lands literally and early, the length (about 2,900 words) is in band, and the ending resolves; one jacket snag.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree narrowly. The dialogue mostly works, but two untagged pivot lines are a reader-facing failure the old review missed.

This story's dialogue is better than the collection's default. Jovie and Harcott
pass the cover-the-tags test: she keeps coming back to "the key" in short,
bluffing imperatives, and he talks like a dealer (negotiate, condition, damage,
recovering, four hundred) until, pinned to his counter, he is down to plain
requests. The cat's nonverbal replies do much of the comic work, and the endgame
talk is spare and practical, as it should be. The failures are local. At the two
moments where leverage changes hands, a line is untagged in a three-person room,
and the reader cannot tell who is challenging or claiming. The first act also
leans toward matched dry retorts and small word-for-word correction relays. The
edit pass should add tags or beats at the pivots and trim the echoes. It should
not sand off Harcott's dealer patter or add wit anywhere.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: the assistants laugh at a simple laser pointer; a fifteen-foot cat made of shadows emerges from behind Jovie and brandishes obsidian claws; their expressions change. L29–L43 deliver this literally ("Behind her", "Fifteen feet tall on four paws", "brandishing five curved obsidian claws") and must stay intact and early.
- Constraints: 2,500–4,000 words (now about 2,900); broadly accessible; comic fantasy menace with non-graphic danger; a complete story that resolves its promise; craft profile prospective-2026-08-23. Keep close third on Jovie and keep the cat's mind untranslated.
- The cat is independent: the dot attracts it but never commands it, and no human controls it. Do not add a command, training or ownership resolution, and do not have anyone state what the cat "really" wants.
- Universe: non-canon, unnamed locality. Do not link this cat to Shalegate's shadow beasts (LOCKED rule in `universe/rules.md`: those are furred physical animals, not made of darkness) or to any other shadow system. No water infrastructure is present; add none.
- Object chain the plot depends on: the key with the rubber skull (ignition → waistcoat → counter → Jovie's pocket → ignition); the pointer (Jovie → floor → Harcott → his pinned fist → Jovie); the helmet (table → floor → her chest → counter → her head); the orange flames and the scarred mudguard; the price tag that tears loose at the end.
- What works: Harcott's arc from salesman to "I can't move my hand"; the assistants' distinct exits (one edges to the front door, one protects the swans, then walks out); the wordless final exchange between dot, bike and cat.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Untagged challenge where the bluff cracks

- Where: L117–L129, the tape-and-swans beat just before Harcott's "Oh"
- Text:
> "Don't," Jovie said.
> The woman froze with the tape stretched between her hands.
> "Not you."
> "Then tell it."
- Problem: Attribution. "Not you." is clearly Jovie's, but "Then tell it." could be the woman (answering the "Not you" addressed to her) or Harcott (testing Jovie's control). The meaning changes with the speaker: from the woman it is a frightened plea; from Harcott it is the challenge that makes Jovie reach for the dot and lets him see her relief. This is the beat where Harcott works out the bluff, and the reader has to guess who pushed.
- Fix: Give the line a speaker. Harcott is the better fit, because his challenge causes the dot flick and his "Oh" two lines later, while the woman stays frozen. Add a tag or a small Harcott beat and change nothing else. If the editor gives it to the woman instead, make it sound frightened rather than like a composed retort. Illustrative only: "Then tell it," Harcott said.

### D2 · Major · Untagged claim when the pointer changes hands

- Where: L153–L167, Jovie drops the pointer and Harcott picks it up
- Text:
> Jovie reached for it. He bent faster and came up with it in his hand.
> "That's mine too."
> "Give it back," she said, taking a step toward him.
- Problem: Attribution. The paragraph before the line contains both characters' actions, and "too" works for either: Jovie protesting that the pointer is hers like the bike, or Harcott claiming it the way he claimed the bike. One reading makes him a gloating fence at the peak of his leverage; the other makes her protest twice. The reader cannot tell who holds the moral floor as leverage flips.
- Fix: Make the speaker explicit with a tag or a beat attached to the line. Harcott is recommended: it echoes his claim on the bike, and "Give it back" at L167 then becomes Jovie's first protest with no repetition. If the editor keeps it for Jovie, tag it "she said" and consider cutting "Give it back" to a beat so she does not protest twice in the same terms.

### D3 · Minor · Ownership quibble has a loose referent

- Where: L73–L79, the phone-call dispute after the reveal
- Text:
> "You said black."
> "With flames."
> "These are predominantly orange."
- Problem: Ground/referent, and the correction relay the collection overuses. "These" points at the flames, but saying the flames are orange does not answer "black with flames"; Harcott presumably means the bike reads as orange. The bad-faith dodge is legible, but the three-line wording relay is the collection's ping-pong rhythm in miniature.
- Fix: Keep "With flames." (it sets up the painted mudguard at L271–L275) and keep one dealer's quibble, but give it a clear referent so it plainly claims the whole machine is orange. Do not add further back-and-forth. Illustrative only: "It's predominantly orange."

### D4 · Minor · Mirrored tag lines muddy the negotiation

- Where: L99–L103, end of the four-hundred offer
- Text:
> "Keep your money. You can take that tag off."
> He glanced at the handlebars. "You can remove the tag yourself."
> For a moment she nearly did.
- Problem: Echo/correction. Harcott repeats her sentence frame with a synonym swap, so the exchange closes on a mirrored retort rather than a move. It is also briefly unclear whether his line refuses or concedes the bike (he keeps the key), and "nearly did" needs a beat to resolve to "nearly pulled the tag off herself".
- Fix: Make Harcott's reply a plain refusal in his own words, or replace it with a nonverbal refusal, so it no longer mirrors her line. Make sure the next sentence clearly means she nearly went and pulled the tag off herself. Keep her fantasy of making him do chores (L103), which is good.

### D5 · Minor · Stock gloat at Jovie's payoff

- Where: L209–L211, Harcott pinned to the counter
- Text:
> "Get it away," he said.
> "You seem to have made a friend."
- Problem: The line is a stock sardonic quip at the moment Jovie has most earned a line of her own. The narration just before it (carpet fibres, sore stomach, snapped helmet vent) is specific and funny; the spoken line is generic.
- Fix: Either cut it and let her non-reply (still brushing her jacket) be the answer, or give her something petty drawn from her own grievances (the vent, the tag, her heels). Keep it short, and do not replace it with a sharper aphorism.

### D6 · Minor · Said-bookism

- Where: L147, the swan lunge
- Text:
> "Here!" Jovie cried, throwing the red spot across its path.
- Problem: "cried" repeats what the exclamation mark and the action already show.
- Fix: Use "said" or drop the tag and keep the action.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Cat's route over the bike is hard to picture

- Where: L269, the cat leaves the counter and scars the mudguard
- Text:
> tried to keep the spot on the right side of the crate so the animal would pass on the right side of the motorcycle
> It took two quick steps through the gap beside the marble fireplace and bounded over the bike, cutting straight across toward the light.
- Problem: Unclear staging at a key cost beat. The bike stands between the fireplace and the clock counter (L21), and the cat starts at the counter. If it goes "left" through the fireplace gap, it is already past the bike, so the reader cannot see why it then bounds over the bike.
- Fix: Show one clear route. For example, the cat starts left toward the fireplace gap, sees the dot on the other side, and cuts back across by leaping the bike. Keep the rear claw catching the mudguard.

### P2 · Minor · "It" shared by cat and dot

- Where: L149, L265–L267, L277
- Text:
> Jovie dragged the light away from the packing table, across the aisle, and saw it run straight up the front wheel of her motorcycle.
> She let the dot creep from behind the crate.
> It set off down the aisle.
> The cat turned its head toward the scrape. Jovie moved the dot into the yard. It followed.
- Problem: Pronoun referent. In each case the nearest antecedent of "It" is the dot or light, while the meaning at L267 and L277 is the cat. The reader resolves it, but only after a stutter during fast action.
- Fix: Name the subject where the antecedent is the wrong one ("the dot" at L149, "The cat" at L267 and L277). Change nothing else.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · The stolen jacket reappears

- Where: L21 and L207
- Text:
> Someone had stolen the bike and the jacket with the keys in it.
> She took her time brushing her jacket.
- Problem: The opening says the jacket was stolen with the keys; later she brushes "her jacket" with no sign it is a different one. A reader may briefly think the stolen jacket has come back.
- Fix: One word at L207 ("her other jacket", "her spare jacket") or change the garment brushed.

## Recurring patterns

- Untagged lines in three-person scenes: L123–L125 and L157 (D1, D2). When the woman or the man is present, tag any line whose speaker is not given by an attached beat.
- Correction and echo relays: L75–L79, L99–L101, and in milder form L123 ("Not you.") and L131 ("There's no 'oh.'"). The last two are character-true bluffing and can stay. Do not let the fixes for D3 and D4 introduce new ones.
- Dry last-word retorts: L19, L33, L67, L93, L179, L211. Most are earned by the comic premise and Harcott's dealer character (L93 and L179 especially), so do not sweep them all. Only L211 is flagged, and no new ones should be added.
- Short one-sentence narrative paragraphs cluster in the chases (L259–L271, L291–L303). They are earned by the action; do not add more elsewhere.

## Preserve

- The deliberate dodge in the first exchange after the reveal:
> "The motorcycle? Yes."
- Harcott's half-confession, the best line in his pricing reflex:
> "Neither was the motorcycle, apparently. Four hundred?"
- The bluff and the cat's indifference:
> "Stay there," Jovie told it, loudly.
> It lifted a hind leg and began washing.
- Harcott's cruelty, which pays off the raw heels at L23:
> "You've got feet."
- The woman's plain exit:
> "I'm going home," she said, and kept walking.
- The honest endgame exchange and its callback to the prompt's changed expressions:
> "Why isn't it going?" Harcott asked.
> "I don't know."
> His face changed. She had preferred the earlier change.
- The wordless final exchange between dot, bike and cat, with no thematic statement, ending on:
> Under her visor, Jovie began to laugh. The price tag tore loose and flew past her shoulder.
- Texture: the clocks "disagreeing with one another", Harcott's thumb with "a broad, dirty nail", the mismatched hand-painted flames.

4. D5, D6: polish Jovie's gloat and the said-bookism.
5. P1, P2: clarify the route over the bike and the cat/dot pronouns.
