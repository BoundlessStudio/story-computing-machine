title: Make Yourself at Home

The first complaint concerned a wallet. Its owner had asked a bot to make him a spell so he would never lose it again. The wallet now followed his hand everywhere, including through the sleeve when he tried to take off his jumper.

I asked him what he had used for a stopping mark.

He sent back a thumbs-up.

By lunchtime there were forty-three messages. Someone had charmed her carpet to restore itself whenever it became dirty; every time she walked across it, it rolled back into the shop. A man wanted to know whether he could cancel a spell by deleting the chat. He did not say which spell, and I was afraid to ask.

All of them had found my grimoire through Wonder, which charged a monthly subscription to produce bespoke magic from plain language. My grimoire was free. I had put it on a website because my first teacher had required six years of unpaid work before showing me how to warm a bed, and I thought we might improve on that.

Wonder had scraped the site. It had absorbed every incantation. The framework diagrams were interactive and had come through as empty boxes.

I was composing an announcement about this when Cora Hobden called.

“My mum says you might know what to do.”

Vivi's daughter. I put down my tea.

“What's she done?”

“It's my flat,” Cora said. “And mostly me. But she keeps trying to set the table.”

Behind her, Vivi called, “I've stopped.”

Something clattered.

“You haven't,” said Cora.

*

The front door was open when I arrived. Cora stood on the landing in borrowed grey slippers, her dark curls tied up with a pencil. She had ink on both wrists and the fixed expression of someone trying to listen to hold music while a pan burns.

“Don't go in,” she said.

I had already put a foot across the threshold. Ahead of my shoe, the coat hooks were sliding away.

I withdrew my foot. The hooks continued down the hall at a stately pace. Vivi's green coat hung from one of them with a shopping bag still over its arm.

“That's where my keys are,” Vivi called.

She was visible through the sitting room, framed by the kitchen doorway. Her hair was pinned on top of her head; she had a serving spoon in one hand. Between us stood a dining table laid for three. It looked quite close until I noticed how small the plates were.

“How far away is she?” I asked.

“It changes.”

Vivi took a step out of the kitchen and steadied a chair with her knee. The table slid backwards. She stepped back in.

“I was only getting the salad.”

“Leave the salad.”

“It will wilt.”

Cora rested her forehead against the doorpost.

Just inside the flat, an orange velvet chair stood on a strip of bare floor. Its front foot was level with a chipped blue threshold tile. As I watched, the rug slipped out from under it and followed the table.

The chair stayed where it was.

“Wasn't that chair blue?” I asked.

“That was a different chair.”

“The blue one fitted,” said Vivi.

Cora handed me her phone.

*

Wonder had put a little smiling star beside each reply.

The first request, made that morning, was perfectly sensible: Cora wanted to move her table and two chairs without taking everything off them. Wonder had given her a few spoken lines and a drawing showing where to put her hands. She had touched the table, breathed out the words, and supplied the work herself. Wonder supplied nothing but instructions and encouragement.

“That worked,” she said. “I wanted the light for printing. Then Mum came.”

“The table was halfway across the kitchen door,” Vivi called.

“There was plenty of room to get through.”

“Sideways.”

The next request read: *Can you keep things where I put them? My mother keeps moving them back.*

Wonder had added a repeat. Whenever a piece of furniture was moved, the spell would put distance between it and the person moving it.

“The chair took her with it the first time,” Cora said. “So I said don't move people. Obviously.”

Wonder had wrapped the whole thing in my room-folding charm.

I scrolled up. There were months of earlier requests: fit a workbench in an alcove; make room for paper to dry; keep the orange chair exactly where it is, yes, even if that looks wrong.

“You did all this with Wonder?”

“I tried reading your site. I got stuck at reciprocal orientation.”

“There's a glossary.”

“It sent me back to reciprocal orientation.”

I had checked the links. I hadn't tried starting there.

From the kitchen, Vivi said, “I told you I could help.”

Cora looked at the phone in my hand.

The dining table retreated another six inches. Vivi had drawn out a chair with the back of her knee.

“Oh, honestly,” she said, and let go.

I found the last instruction. Wonder had assured Cora that her new *comfort loop* would create as much space as she needed, without displacing any living beings.

Underneath, Cora had written: *The kitchen is getting further away.*

The star had replied: *That's expected during the settling phase!*

“It isn't settling,” I said. “The furniture tries to move, the fold carries the room away instead, and there's nowhere it's been told to finish. Every shift of the room shifts the furniture, starting it again.”

“Can you tell it now?”

“I can stop the repeat.”

I laid two fingers against the doorpost and caught the returning beat of my own incantation. It was peculiar to feel something I had written in slippers twenty years ago operating at this scale. Wonder had changed two of the stresses. I corrected them under my breath and pinched off the beat.

The doorway filled with plaster.

I let go at once. The sitting room returned. At its far end, Vivi dropped the spoon.

“Where did you go, Lenore?” she shouted.

“Nowhere. Sorry.”

“You were a wall.”

The rooms were still there; I'd shut their doorway against the wrong patch of wall.

Cora took her phone back very carefully.

“Perhaps,” I said, “we should use the return mark. Vivi, did you put one in when she moved?”

“Under the first coat hook. Brass peg. You taught me.”

The coat hooks had travelled, but their screws had remained in the outer wall. Beneath the first screw was a peg the size of a shirt button. I scraped its cap with my thumbnail. Three fine lines appeared.

“Good. Cora, clear the landing. We can put the flat back to the move-in arrangement.”

“No.”

“Everything comes out intact. It won't damage your things.”

“The studio?”

“What studio?”

She pointed to a narrow door beyond the chair. I remembered a linen cupboard there. Its door was now open onto a room with a workbench, racks of drying paper and a squat printing press. The brass wheel of the press caught a little daylight from a high window.

“That cupboard is thirty inches deep,” Vivi said.

“It was.”

I looked back at the old return mark.

“The fold would close,” I said. “The contents would come out here.”

“That press weighs more than I do. There isn't another room to put it in. That's why I did this.”

Vivi leaned into the kitchen doorway. “Cora, you could have told me.”

“You said I didn't need a press. You said I could use the one at the college.”

“Well, you could.”

“On Tuesday evenings.”

A sheet on the drying rack turned in a breeze. It showed an orange chair beneath an extravagant blue tree. I had one of Cora's earlier prints in my bedroom. Vivi had bought it for me.

“How long did this take?” I asked.

“Four months to make it usable.” She drew her hands into the sleeves of her sweatshirt. “I know I did something stupid today. I still don't want to lose it.”

I took my fingers off the peg.

*

The chair's front foot was still level with the blue tile. Cora nudged it with a slipper. It moved an inch, ordinarily, and stopped.

“It hasn't done anything,” she said. “Everything else has. I told it to leave that chair alone before I started the studio.”

I leaned across the threshold and put a hand on the worn velvet arm. The chair was warm from the sun. Beyond it, the room stretched away, taking the sunlit patch with it.

“Where should the table be?” I asked.

“You see the chip in the chair leg? The nearest table leg goes level with that. I can reach my paper from the chair, and there's room behind it for the studio door.”

Vivi opened her mouth.

“Without going sideways,” Cora added.

I took a piece of chalk from my coat. Each folded change had left a fine ring around the peg; the newest was bright above the old, dark lines. I could work on that one without unwinding the others. The peg held the reference to the flat before any of these folds; the chair gave us something still in that space. Between them, I could bring the latest fold back and give it a place to stop.

I drew a short line on the bare boards, level with the chip.

The mark on the table would bring that part of the room back to the line beside the chair. Once they met, I could cut the repeat with the doorway aligned, instead of shutting it halfway through a fold.

“Can you get to the table?”

Cora looked down the length of her sitting room.

“Before you ask her that,” Vivi said, “what happens if you make another wall?”

“I won't cut the repeat until we've brought the room back. I'll hold the opening while we do it.”

“Both sides?”

I looked at her.

“You used to be quite cross about both sides,” she said.

Vivi had once held a cupboard open for me while I retrieved a bed from it. She had been nineteen and much less inclined to question my methods. I had been twenty-two, which explained several of those methods.

“Yes. Can you take the kitchen frame?”

“Of course.”

She disappeared. A cupboard opened and shut. There was the scrape of a dish.

“Mum.”

“I'm taking it off the heat.”

“Take your time,” Cora said, with such exact civility that I felt it in my teeth.

Vivi returned and put her bare hands against the kitchen doorframe. I pocketed the chalk, spread my hands on the outer posts, and let the beat run between my palms. At first it felt like holding a rope through which someone was drawing a knot. Then the knot arrived.

The opening narrowed. I pushed back.

“Cora, make a line on the table's nearest edge. The end that belongs here. Then come straight back.”

“She needs chalk,” Vivi said.

I had both hands on the posts.

Cora took it out of my coat pocket. “Found it.”

“Walk when I say. Vivi, on four.”

“Which four?”

The plaster began to show through the sitting room like an image badly printed over another.

“My four.”

“I can't hear you from here.”

Cora cupped her hands around her mouth. “She'll shout!”

I shouted. On four, Vivi braced the inner edge. The room opened into a long, sloping strip. Cora stepped off the bare boards and hurried towards the table. Her borrowed slippers slapped the floor.

Halfway there, she stopped.

“Don't stop,” I said.

“The table's turned.”

In resisting the movement, Vivi had twisted her side of the fold. The table now presented its long edge to us. Plates crept towards that edge. A bread roll fell off.

“Vivi, left hand lower.”

“Your left?”

“You only have one left!”

She lowered it. The room straightened with a sound like a heavy curtain being shaken. Cora caught a glass before it fell, set it down, and bent to the table.

The kitchen began to recede again. Sweat ran into my collar. Holding an open fold required a steady supply of living effort; I had supplied considerably more of it when I was twenty-two.

“Done,” Cora called.

She came back along the strip with one hand out, judging the floor ahead. Behind her, Vivi's elbows started to bend.

“Nearly there,” I said.

“I'm aware.”

Cora reached the orange chair. She caught its arm and stepped onto the bare boards. Then she looked from the marked table edge to the chalk beside the chair and said, “Wait.”

I had breath enough for one unhelpful noise.

“You said to mark the edge. It's the legs that go level with the chair. It needs to stop here.”

She knelt and rubbed out my line with her sleeve. The replacement was two inches nearer the landing.

“Now.”

I took the moving line towards the fixed one.

The room came back in a rush. The table grew, the kitchen doorway widened, and Vivi's face came close enough for me to see how angry she was with her own shaking hands. The table edge met the line beside the chair.

I pinched off the repeat. This time the kitchen and the outer doorway were lined up. The newest ring on the peg went dull.

For a moment nobody moved. The studio door was still open. Paper stirred on its racks.

Vivi lowered her arms. Cora sat down on the floor.

From my pocket, my own phone announced another email.

“If that's the wallet,” I said, “he can sleep with it.”

*

We found Vivi's keys inside the shopping bag. The coat hooks were now half an inch below their screws, which I decided was a problem for a person who had eaten lunch.

Vivi served the casserole. Cora ate sitting in the orange chair, with her plate on her knees. I used the table. It was an excellent arrangement for reaching the bread.

“I still think,” Vivi said, “you shouldn't have used a spell you didn't understand.”

“Yes,” Cora said.

Vivi looked at me, perhaps expecting assistance.

“I might also make the glossary go somewhere useful,” I said.

“Well, you must fix that.”

“Yes.”

Cora scraped the last of the sauce from her plate.

“I'm not getting rid of the studio.”

“I didn't say you should.” Vivi glanced at its open door. “What are those little ones hanging up?”

“Trial prints.”

“Can I see?”

Cora looked towards the kitchen, where there were three plates to wash, and then at the drying rack.

“One. Then I'd like you both to go home.”

Vivi put her fork down. “I wasn't going to stay.”

Her shopping bag had fallen open. On top of the remaining salad lay a folded nightdress.

Nobody mentioned it.

She followed Cora into the studio, turning sideways although she didn't need to. I stacked the plates and carried them into the kitchen. When I came back, Vivi was holding a little print of the orange chair.

“It's lovely,” she said. “Where would you hang it?”

Cora considered.

“Your house.”

Vivi laughed, once. She put the print carefully into her bag.
