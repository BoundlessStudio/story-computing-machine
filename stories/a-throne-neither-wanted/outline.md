# Outline

## Story

- Premise: In an unnamed kingdom, a public prophecy says that before the next moonrise one of the king's two adult children will kill their kind father and take the throne neither desires. The first-person younger sibling expects terror or suspicion; instead, their father recognizes something in the wording, remains calm, and is separated from both children by an automatic royal-protection protocol before he can explain.
- Character want and pressure: The narrator wants to keep the family intact and prevent the older sibling from sacrificing their freedom out of lifelong protective habit. Confinement, mutually written renunciations, and a minister's attempts to make each child denounce the other turn fear into mistrust while the impending forced succession makes inaction dangerous.
- Turn: The siblings trust each other long enough to investigate their father's calm and recover the suppressed crown-law meaning of `kill`: an adult royal child may publicly end a living monarch's legal identity as sovereign, provided the child names the reign's real harms, the monarch yields, and the challenger occupies the throne. Their father had been preparing to restore this peaceful accountability rite and replace hereditary succession; the prophecy describes that choice rather than commanding murder.
- Ending: The older sibling freely performs the rite, truthfully faults the father for allowing protective custom and ministerial secrecy to imprison his children, and accepts the unwanted throne only for a bounded transition. The father survives as their father, owns his failure without losing their love, and the siblings use the brief accession to begin opening succession beyond the family; the final private image is the three of them sharing a late meal while the throne stands empty elsewhere.

## Beats

1. During an ordinary public audience, a court prophet abruptly delivers the prediction. The siblings recoil; their father listens closely to its ceremonial diction and starts to reassure them, but palace protocol immediately removes him and confines the children together for his protection.
2. The older sibling offers to surrender both claim and liberty so the narrator cannot be suspected. The narrator reveals an independently written renunciation, proving neither has a throne-motive but also exposing how readily each decides for the other; they agree to make no further sacrifice without mutual consent.
3. A senior minister interviews them separately, presses each to identify the other as the likely killer, and announces that the elder will be crowned if the king is declared incapacitated. The minister's certainty that a succession must happen, rather than concern for the father's life, makes the narrator question who benefits from a literal reading.
4. Remembering that their father reacted to the prophecy's wording rather than its threat, the siblings evade confinement and search the palace's neglected legal archive. A damaged abdication draft and an older coronation record establish the local rite of crown-death: `kill` once meant ending the sovereign's legal person through witnessed, truthful challenge, not ending the human life beneath the office.
5. Marginal notes in their father's hand show that he was preparing to revive the rite because existing law makes ordinary abdication impossible and would automatically trap the elder in succession. The same evidence shows recent access by the minister, who knew the alternate meaning and used the protection protocol to force a controllable coronation.
6. The siblings reach the throne room as the minister seeks a declaration of incapacity. Reunited with them, the father confirms he did not cause the prophecy and apologizes for believing he had more time to dismantle the dangerous protocol; his calm came from knowing the old usage and trusting that neither child would choose bloodshed for power.
7. When the minister orders the children removed, the older sibling invokes crown-death before the assembled witnesses. The narrator serves as corroborating witness while the elder names genuine harms of the reign: the father's secrecy, his tolerance of coercive succession law, and the confinement enabled in his name. The father accepts the judgment and yields.
8. The elder takes the throne, fulfilling the prophecy without treating its wording as destiny, and immediately halts the forced coronation machinery, orders an open inquiry into the minister, and calls a time-limited representative transition. The narrator refuses a crown but agrees to help with the work, turning sibling protection into shared consent rather than unilateral sacrifice.
9. Afterward, the former king is neither dead nor secretly still sovereign. He joins his children as a fallible, beloved father; their relief coexists with the real loss of his office and the elder's temporary burden, and the vacant throne at their late meal resolves the image of power none of them wanted.

## Continuity

- Canon used: The independent, unnamed culture fits the shared world's deep-time range. The plot uses the locked memory-versus-myth emphasis through a distorted legal meaning recovered from material records, and treats the prophecy and succession rite as bounded local phenomena rather than universal rules.
- Boundaries and unknowns: Keep the kingdom and era unnamed and undated. The one-off prophecy has no established mechanism, wider reliability, cost, or power to override choice; it is not the Covenant of Hours and has no connection to Sablewake, Ersene, the Crown-era dragon monarchy, or any other royal, prophetic, crown, or succession system. Crown-death is local law with serious political and relational consequences, not literal bodily death, identity erasure, or a general rule that offices are separate persons. The long-term constitutional settlement remains beyond the ending; no canon ruling is required.
