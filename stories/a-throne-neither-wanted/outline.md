# Outline

## Story

- Premise and central promise: At a private succession reading, an unnamed younger royal and their older sibling learn that one will kill their gentle father for the throne neither wants. The king's calm remains baffling until they discover that the coronation working has held him alive since a fatal accident; transfer to an heir will end that borrowed life. Resolve who acts, why the father is unafraid, and how the family avoids confusing love with ambition or ownership.
- Focal pressure or attachment: The narrator wants their father alive and the elder free, but habitually lets the elder make painful decisions and resents being protected. They must hear the father's wish without automatically obeying and the elder's willingness without making sacrifice another family duty. Both siblings have valuable futures outside rule.
- Counterforce or complication: The prophecy supplies an act but not identity, motive, or timing, so practical questions sound like murderous intent. The elder builds contingencies; the narrator mistakes fluency for readiness. Their father minimizes pain and withheld the accident's consequence so suffering would not decide for them. His worsening body supplies the limit; no villain or public emergency should replace the family choice.
- POV, distance, and information limit: Close first-person past through the younger sibling, whose anxious interpretation misreads pauses and composure without hiding known facts. The elder and father remain exterior; motives emerge through behavior and choices. The local forecast is reliable, but no one can inspect alternatives or prove whether foreknowledge helped cause the act.
- Governing movement and time shape: One day from disrupted breakfast to private nighttime succession. Domestic and service spaces lead to an emptied throne chamber while cooling food, medicine intervals, and evening preparations measure time. Each movement changes the prophecy from accusation to mechanism, bodily truth, and action. Stop in the immediate family consequence rather than narrating a reign.
- Speculative rule or ordinary-world constraint: This monarchy's nonliving coronation throne can suspend one terminal injury suffered by its ruler. The ruler remains the same competent living person; the interval cannot heal the wound and grows harder to bear. A direct heir must freely sit, take the ruler's hand, and accept office; support then ends and the old ruler dies from the original injury. The throne cannot compel, choose, read minds, judge, or speak. A separate annual pattern forecasts one succession act without motive, identity, or exact time.

## Voice

- Narrative texture: First-person past driven by anxious reinterpretation. Notice untouched food, documents, medicine timing, hands, and small acts of care; let concrete observation expose where accusation outruns knowledge.
- Conversational texture: Familiar overlap, unfinished questions, mundane interruptions, and old shorthand that sometimes excludes. Break explanations with caregiving and practical choices.
- Rhetorical ownership: The narrator reasons from relational cues and pointed questions; the elder uses contingencies and legal distinctions; the father favors domestic particulars and gentle analogy until facts are required. The physician is measured and single-purpose.
- Pressure behavior: The narrator grows blunt but learns to listen past pauses. The elder's option-making accelerates, then fails into silence when choice becomes personal. The father drops reassurance and answers literally.
- Anti-default: Avoid three equally eloquent royals debating sacrifice in aphorisms; do not make every exchange prophecy exposition, confession, or symmetrical self-denial.

## Beats

1. At a small breakfast reading, the pattern names the killing. The elder asks procedural questions; the narrator hears calculation. Their father continues an ordinary task and refuses to choose an heir. His calm heightens fear without becoming glib.
2. The siblings test avoidance plans against the unnamed rite keeper and household records. Establish forecast versus compulsion. Their argument reveals that both preserved outside plans and that the elder researched emergency succession, not whether they intend to act.
3. A medicine interval or physical failure exposes the father's condition. The physician gives only necessary facts: the accident was fatal, the throne delayed rather than cured it, and waiting increases suffering. Attendants speak only for immediate tasks.
4. In the father's room, each child presses from a different direction. He acknowledges withholding the cost but cannot rank their futures. The narrator challenges the elder's automatic responsibility and confronts their own reliance on it. No comforting speech settles the choice.
5. During a bounded interval apart, the narrator attends to the father's embodied wishes and the elder's commitments rather than the prophecy's accusation. They decide to take the throne because further deferral imposes a choice on everyone. Keep mercy, fear, love, and protectiveness mixed.
6. Empty the throne chamber of ceremony. Father, elder, and narrator each retain a last opportunity to stop. Proceed only through consequential choices and clear physical contact. The father dies non-graphically from the suspended injury; the narrator becomes ruler without triumph.
7. End soon afterward as the siblings perform one necessary ordinary act around their father's absence while courtiers wait outside. Show the first burden and altered sibling relationship, but leave governance and grief unresolved. No proclamation, destroyed throne, public lesson, or coronation tableau.

## Continuity

- Canon used: The story occupies an otherwise unspecified era of the one deep-time physical world. Apply personhood protection to the father's suspended life and the accessible-content ceiling to his death. Use no recurring people, places, factions, artifacts, or systems.
- Boundaries and unknowns: The throne, succession pattern, monarchy, and their costs are bounded local facts with no connection to Sablewake's Covenant of Hours, other prophecies, crowns, monarchies, or named settings. Forecasting establishes no universal fate rule. The throne and pattern are non-agentic; the father's personhood is neither copied nor reduced to a mechanism. Leave the wider kingdom, later reign, origin of the working, and counterfactual future unresolved.
