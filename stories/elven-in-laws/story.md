# A Very Good Age

I’d promised to let Tilven do the talking. I lasted halfway up the stairs at the Plum Loft. There was a stool on the eighth tread with its front feet right at the edge, and I called up to ask if it belonged to anybody.

The stool had a cushion tied on. I found this out by picking it up by one corner, the way I clear the floor between classes. Below us, in the cold room that smelled of split plums, two people were arguing over the weight of a bin. One of them had been saying *and the wood?* since we came in. I shifted the stool one step up and turned it parallel to the rail. Tilven reached past me to put it back.

“Give my mother a minute to remember where she’s left everything. She’s had all week to put things out for us.”

Tilven was a hundred and forty-three and had lived in our town, among humans, for the last eleven of those years. It took me a while to work out that when she said which hairpin to wear or what to bring if you were invited for tea, she was going on eleven years’ scholarship and wanted very much to be right. She had given me a dark green coat for my forty-sixth birthday. I was wearing it now and thinking how nice I looked, exactly like a woman you could have pleasant dealings with while you were living among humans, before you went home and found an elf to settle down with.

A woman leaned over the rail with Tilven’s face and a red cap stretched over the tips of her ears. “Oh, leave that down there, dear, we’ve got a much nicer seat for you up here. Let me look at you both.” She opened her arms awkwardly around the rail. I came up the rest of the steps in time to be the first one she caught. “I wish you could have seen Clune the day we got the letter saying you two were going to marry. He took it straight down to the plum sheds to read it out, and would you believe he was halfway through before he looked up and found me standing there. I was the one who’d fetched the post.”

Tilven had rehearsed her greeting so hard on the road to Ternstep that I caught her halfway through it once when she was asking after the price of a pair of gloves. Now she put her hand through my arm and said nothing at all. I liked being on the same side of her silence as her mother. I would have stood there quite a bit longer in the cold for it.

“Forty-six,” Otta said, looking down at the stool. “And you never even sat on it. I told Clune you’d need one halfway up. Have you missed your pupils since you gave them up?”

“I see them every Thursday,” I said. “Some of them are even older than I am.”

I let go of Tilven to get out the pear cordial I’d brought. Otta took the bottle and drew my arm through hers. My performance on the stairs had come as a great surprise and I was beginning to enjoy the credit.

The Loft had low windows, and every one of them was turned to that slope of Ternstep where the plum trees stood bare above their blue ladders. The sun came back at us from the white wall of the drying shed. It lay in bars across the floor. Over by the windows there was an elf with a fiddle on his lap, moving one foot back and forth where the light fell, apparently to see how much it would show on a well-polished boot. I recognized Clune from the miniature Tilven carried in her purse. He put the fiddle down and came over to see me settled.

“Tell me when you’ve got your weight on it,” he said.

There was a splendid chair up at the high end of the Loft. Otta shook out a quilt that had a plum tree stitched in yellow across it, and I sat down under the tree and felt the ache from the walk up to Ternstep begin to go out of my calves. Clune wanted to know how my feet sat. He wanted to know if the back came up under my shoulder blades. I shifted a little to try it and the whole chair came forward an inch. He tucked the toe of his polished boot under the quilt and nudged something into place.

“The feet are where I’d put them myself,” I told him, and that pleased him so much he said I could have my pick of the music.

I was thinking how long it had been since anybody had wanted to know what I’d dance to. At home the fiddlers played what I paid them to play, and on a good Thursday at nine it turned out I wanted them to play what they’d been playing at seven, only slower. I was about to ask Clune what he’d choose if it was his evening. He pulled the cloth up and drew a little wooden wedge out from in front of the wheel. It was a lovely spoked wheel, all oiled and dark under the hem.

“Father,” Tilven said. “Is that Granddad’s chair?”

“I made the axle over. I can turn it in half the space now.”

“He took it down from the rafters as soon as we heard how old you were,” Otta said. There was a split in the end of her thumbnail. She caught it on the quilt and gave it a little tug. “After your last dance you’re supposed to get first choice of everything for supper, too. There’s a plum cake down in the drying shed with two almonds in it so you’ve twice the chance of getting the slice that brings you luck. I’ve told him we have to let you cut it yourself.”

“I think I’d like a dance nearer the beginning of the evening,” I said.

“Oh, we can go round before the others come up. We had that in mind. You might make it up here again next autumn, dear, and wouldn’t that be nice? We thought we’d better make sure you had your Last Dance while you were here to see it.”

The cold had crept out from the stair and under the quilt. I was warm right down to my knees. My ankles, all of a sudden, were as cold as they would have been if I’d left my skirt in the wash and come up in my slip. I could see myself the way they’d imagined me coming in. Somebody Tilven had to make allowances for, whose chances of catching the music were getting slimmer by the week. Worse, I could see them having the afternoon they’d planned if only I would agree to be wheeled round. They’d both have been happy with me. I pulled at the quilt and caught the chair by the arms when it gave under me.

“Mother, at forty-six she could have as many years ahead of her as she’s had already,” Tilven said. She came around the side where the other wheel was still covered. “I’d like you to take the chair out of the way, please. It was very dear of you to think of it.”

“Wait, I’ve only just got sat down.” I had both hands on the chair arms. The wood had been worn so smooth I could feel my thumb sliding over it. Once before the visit Tilven had leaned forward at the supper table to see which way the lamplight went through my hair, where it was starting to come in white. I’d asked her how white hers would get before I was old, and she said she could tell already that it would go from the tips of the ears inward. I was relieved to hear about the ears and missed the fact that she’d moved her head out of the light. I wished I’d asked the question a second time. Otta had gone to quite a lot of trouble over the question I’d been so pleased to leave alone. “How many dancing years did you think I had left?”

“Well, I couldn’t say. A human woman who used to come up to the Loft every harvest taught Clune to play. I offered to take her fiddle up one evening. She said to let the thing alone and give a woman of forty-eight a minute to catch her breath. Our stairs would be the death of her, she said, and then she came up and played till the sorters were coming in for work in the morning. The next autumn her son wrote that she’d died at forty-nine. I thought what a thing it was that she’d come out and said it that way. You were coming up the very same stairs.” Otta looked over her shoulder, as if somebody else might come up and prove her right if she gave them long enough. “I thought about that fiddle player when Tilven sent us the letter.”

Clune slid the chock back in under the wheel. “Granddad used to put his heels down the minute we got going. I’ve given you a longer footboard. If you want me to stop, you can tell me in words.”

“You’ve thought of everything,” I said. It surprised me how much I meant it. The forepart of the footboard had been finished in scallops, each with its own little ridge that I could feel through the sole of my shoe. I wondered how many times he’d pushed it around the floor with nobody in it. “Did Tilven’s grandfather get his tune?”

“Oh, he did,” said Otta. “Those wheels used to wake the chickens. He wore a paper flower in each ear and leaned over so far to listen he lost one. There was a hen with a low voice in the yard two doors down and we went the whole way around again to see if she’d join in at the same place.” She’d left off picking at her thumbnail. “She did, too. I thought you might fancy a ride round a place where there’d be no one to interrupt you.”

“My mother turned seventy-four in June. She starts asking me before six on a Friday who I think is getting the last tune at the dance. It’s generally her. Last week she slipped the man who plays the box a hot pie to bring him back out after he’d put on his coat. She sent the cordial up with me. She had all the herbs out on a dish to see which ones she wanted to give a woman she’d never met. When we come to have another dance you could ask her how she chose them.”

“Seventy-four. I think I’d have had a great many questions for her, given just half an hour. She sounds in very good form.” Otta reached to smooth the quilt on my lap and drew her hand back. “I had been thinking how pleasant it would be to have a woman in the family I could hear tell the same story at two weddings, once with the good bit in it and once when her mother was there. I didn’t think I’d have the leisure with you. I’d have made it a very different afternoon.”

I slid the quilt onto the bench and got up, a little too briskly. Somebody downstairs called out the weight of the last bin. “Let’s leave you some time to get ready for the real dance,” I said. “One day I’ll get a ride in that chair, if Clune will let me come back. I’d like to see if he has it going any faster by then.”

“Will you let me ask you for something before we go?” Tilven said. “I’ve been wanting to ask you to dance with my mother since we set out. I got as far as *I’d like to ask you* in the glove shop and that poor man thought I wanted him to try on the gloves. I wish I’d asked you at the top of the stairs. I want to stay long enough to see you dance together.”

The floor where I stood had a white scuff in it, the end of a heel mark that curved out from under one of the benches. I put my toe to it and thought how far a woman could have gone on one heel to make a mark that long. I’d been looking forward to dancing with Otta before I even knew her name. In the early weeks Tilven had found herself stepping straight forward whenever I tried to show her the turn I teach beginners. She said her mother always took a long step there, because she liked to feel the floor drop. I knew something about Otta’s big right step that she couldn’t possibly have known I knew. I wanted to feel it on the end of my arm.

“I may have some more things to ask you about your family, Marrit,” said Otta. “Would you two bring a dance from your town for the first set next autumn? We always start with one of ours. The people who do the best out of that are people like me who never have to learn a step new.”

“I can bring you a whole dance without a hill in it,” I said. “I’d like you to show me what you do on the hill first. What’s the quickest one?”

Clune took up his fiddle and tucked a folded cloth under his chin. He played softly at first for us to find the step. Otta took me to the high end. The grain underfoot ran the length of the hall except where three boards had been put in crosswise. She said to watch for those, they were the place to turn. She had a square little hand with a rough pad where the fingers closed and a stripe of white skin from a ring she’d taken off before we got there. She laced her fingers through mine and put her shoulder under our two hands, ready for the downhill run.

It went slower than I’d expected till we got to the turn. Otta planted her right foot for that big step. Watching for it made me late bringing my own foot over. It was well for both of us she’d taken such a good hold. The hall opened up below our joined hands, all the way across to Clune. Otta went down the slope laughing, her arm pulling mine tight as we tried to go on two separate beats. At the bottom she put her mouth almost to my ear.

“I put you in a chair,” she said, and this time there was enough breath to finish laughing. I could feel her laughing in every finger. Then she tucked her elbow in, the way Tilven always did when I’d given her a chance to go as fast as she liked, and we tried the turn again.

We got back to the chairs out of breath. The light was off the floor, and down in the sorting room somebody was sweeping. I could feel it through my soles every time the broom knocked against a post. Tilven brought us each a glass of the cordial. Otta caught a drop on her thumb where it had run down the glass and tasted it. She held her thumb there a little longer than she needed to, trying to make out what she’d tasted.

“Does your mother put the peel in this, do you think? I’d like to talk to her about it. I used to keep my peels two days before I put them in anything, but they had such a way of curling up on me.”

“Ask her on Friday,” I said. “Come down to the dance. It’s an easy morning’s journey if you take the road by the cooper’s yard. The fiddle player’s usually the first one there.”

“We could take the chair down,” Clune said. “She’ll certainly want it at seventy-four. If she’s that fond of the last tune, I’d ask them to play it first, while there’s still time.”

I took a taste of the cordial. My mother had put in more thyme than usual, which probably meant she’d look for a chance to give Otta a second bottle and show her how she did it when she was giving it her full attention. I could picture the evening that would make, especially if somebody tried to tell her when to dance.

“If you take that chair down to my mother,” I said, “you’ll have to take another one just like it. She’ll want to race.”

Otta put down her glass and lifted the quilt off the bench. “Would she mind if I had a little practice, so it was a fair thing when we met? I’d like to do it the way you would for a race. Clune, you can go down to the other end and tell me how much room I’ve got.”

I’d barely set down my glass before she was settled with the quilt over her lap and her feet on the board. She caught hold of the chair arms in the same way I had when it first moved. Tilven went over to the side and set one foot out for a starting line. Clune took the chock out and went down to the far end to meet her. I got both hands behind the chair. I’d intended to see how well it coasted on a very little shove.

“Come along, dear,” said Otta. “I felt how much you had in you at that turn.”

Tilven dropped her hand. I gave the chair a good running push and let go at the crosswise boards. Otta let go, too. She opened both arms as she had over the rail to catch us, and went clattering downhill with the red cap coming up off one ear.
