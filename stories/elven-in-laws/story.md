# A Very Good Age

I’d promised to let Tilven do the talking. I lasted halfway up the stairs at the Plum Loft. There was a stool on the eighth tread with its front feet right at the edge, and I called up to ask if it belonged to anybody.

The stool had a cushion tied on. I found this out by picking it up by one corner, the way I clear the floor between classes. It was late afternoon and there were still two people at the scales below us, arguing over the weight of a bin in a room that smelled of split plums. One of them had been saying *and the wood?* since we came in. I shifted the stool one step up and turned it parallel to the rail. Tilven reached past me to put it back.

“Leave it where it was, if you would. My mother’s been getting the Loft ready for us all week.”

Tilven was a hundred and forty-three and had lived in our town, among humans, for the last eleven of those years. It took me a while to work out that when she said which hairpin to wear or what to bring if you were invited for tea, she was going on eleven years’ scholarship and wanted very much to be right. She’d given me my dark green coat when I turned forty-six. I’d brushed it until there was a shine on one cuff. I had an idea that if I made a very good impression we might get round, in a visit or two, to the kind of ease Tilven had at my mother’s, where she knew better than I did which drawer to look in for a spoon.

A woman with Tilven’s face leaned over the rail, her red cap stretched over the tips of her ears. “Oh, leave that down there, dear, we’ve got a much nicer seat for you up here. Come on, Marrit, let me look at you both.” She opened her arms and bumped an elbow on the post. I came up the rest of the steps in time to be the first one she caught. “I wish you could have seen Tilven’s father the day we got the letter saying you two were going to marry. Clune took it straight down to the plum sheds to read it out, and would you believe he was halfway through before he looked up and found me standing there. I was the one who’d fetched the post.”

That morning, in the glove shop on the road to Ternstep, Tilven had said, *There’s something I’d like to ask you,* so earnestly that the man stopped fitting a glove and waited. I’d thought she was trying out a speech for her mother. Now she took my arm. “Good afternoon, Mother,” she said. I’d been taking the same sort of care over *Hello*.

“Forty-six, Tilven wrote,” Otta said, looking down at the stool. “And you never even sat on it. I told Clune you’d need one halfway up. Have you missed your pupils since you gave them up?”

“I see them every Thursday,” I said. “Some of them are even older than I am.”

I let go of Tilven to get out the pear cordial I’d brought. Otta took the bottle and offered me her arm. I took it gladly.

Otta led me down between the benches to meet Clune. The floor sloped toward the low windows enough that I could feel my toes go forward in my shoes. I set my heel down first the rest of the way, as I’d have told a pupil coming at a turn too fast. Outside, the plum trees stood bare above their blue ladders, and the sun came back at us from the white wall of the drying shed. Clune set down his fiddle and planted his feet well apart to shake my hand. The light showed the polish on his boots all the way to the laces. He pointed back up the Loft.

There was a splendid chair up at the high end of the Loft. Clune went up and put a hand on the back. “Tell me when you’ve got your weight on it,” he said.

Otta shook out a quilt that had a plum tree stitched in yellow across it, and I sat down under the tree and felt the ache from the walk up to Ternstep begin to go out of my calves. Clune wanted to know how my feet sat. He wanted to know if the back came up under my shoulder blades. I shifted a little to try it and the whole chair came forward an inch. He tucked the toe of his polished boot under the quilt and nudged something into place.

“The feet are where I’d put them myself,” I told him, and that pleased him so much he said I could have my pick of the music.

I was thinking how long it had been since anybody had wanted to know what I’d dance to. At home the fiddlers played what I paid them to play, and on a good Thursday at nine it turned out I wanted them to play what they’d been playing at seven, only slower. I was about to ask Clune what he’d choose if it was his evening. He lifted the quilt, drew a chock away from a wheel, and braced the chair with his boot. It was a lovely spoked wheel, all oiled and dark where the quilt had covered it.

“Father,” Tilven said. “Is that Granddad’s chair?”

“I made the axle over. I can turn it in half the space now.”

“He took it down from the rafters as soon as we heard how old you were,” Otta said. There was a split in the end of her thumbnail. She caught it on the quilt and gave it a little tug. “After your last dance you’re supposed to get first choice of everything for supper, too. There’s a plum cake down in the drying shed with two almonds in it so you’ve twice the chance of getting the slice that brings you luck. I’ve told him we have to let you cut it yourself.”

“I think I’d like a dance nearer the beginning of the evening,” I said.

“Oh, we can go round before the others come up. We had that in mind. You might make it up here again next autumn, dear, and wouldn’t that be nice? We thought we’d better make sure you had your Last Dance while you were here to see it.”

My knees were still warm where Otta had tucked the quilt. My ankles were as cold as they would have been if I’d left my skirt in the wash and come up in my slip. I could see myself the way they’d imagined me coming in. Somebody Tilven had to make allowances for, whose chances of catching the music were getting slimmer by the week. Worse, I could see them having the afternoon they’d planned if only I would agree to be wheeled round. They’d both have been happy with me. I pulled at the quilt and caught the chair by the arms when it gave under me.

“Mother, I dance with women in their eighties on Thursdays,” Tilven said. She came around the side where the other wheel was still covered. “Marrit’s younger than some of their children. I’d like you to take the chair out of the way, please. It was very dear of you to think of it.”

“Wait, I’ve only just got sat down.” I had both hands on the chair arms. The wood had been worn so smooth I could feel my thumb sliding over it. I’d given Tilven the talking for the afternoon and here she was being excellent at it. In another minute she’d have got me out of the chair and nobody would want to say how I’d got in it. “How many dancing years did you think I had left?” I said.

Otta drew the quilt back up over my knees. “Well, I couldn’t say. A human woman who used to come up to the Loft every harvest taught Clune to play. I offered to take her fiddle up one evening. She said to let the thing alone and give a woman of forty-eight a minute to catch her breath. Our stairs would be the death of her, she said, and then she came up and played till the sorters were coming in for work in the morning. The next autumn her son wrote that she’d died at forty-nine. I’d heard people say that about the stairs a hundred times. When I got the news I thought she’d been telling me in her own way. You were coming up the very same stairs.” Otta looked over her shoulder, as if somebody else might come up and prove her right if she gave them long enough. “I thought about that fiddle player when Tilven sent us the letter.”

Clune slid the chock back in under the wheel. “Granddad used to drag his heels the minute I got him going. I’ve given you a longer footboard. If you want me to stop, just tell me.”

“You’ve thought of everything,” I said. The forepart of the footboard had been finished in scallops, each with its own little ridge that I could feel through the sole of my shoe. I wondered how many times he’d pushed it around the floor with nobody in it. “Did Tilven’s grandfather get his tune?”

“Oh, he did,” said Otta. “Those wheels used to wake the chickens. He wore a paper flower in each ear and leaned over so far to listen he lost one. There was a hen with a low voice in the yard two doors down and we went the whole way around again to see if she’d join in at the same place.” She’d left off picking at her thumbnail. “She did, too. I thought if we did yours before the neighbors came up you’d have the whole floor to yourself.”

“My mother turned seventy-four in June.” I stretched my legs out on Clune’s footboard. “Every Friday by six she’s asking who’ll get the last tune at the dance. It’s generally her. Last week the man with the box had his coat on to go. She slipped him a hot pie and he sat down and played her tune with his coat still on. She made the cordial I brought you.”

“How does she keep a pie hot that late into a dance?” Otta reached to smooth the quilt on my lap and drew her hand back. “I had been thinking how pleasant it would be to have a woman in the family I could hear tell the same story at two weddings, once with the good bit in it and once when her mother was there. I didn’t think I’d have the leisure with you.”

Tilven still had a hand on the back of the chair. At supper one night she’d brought her head close to the lamp to see the white coming in at my temples. I’d asked where I’d see hers first. She said to look above the ears and leaned back far enough that I couldn’t see. There was good light for looking where she stood now. I kept my eyes on her hand, where her knuckles had gone white against the chair back. I gathered the quilt off my lap and set it down on the bench.

I stood up and started doing up my coat. Somebody downstairs called out the weight of the last bin. “Tilven, I think we’d better get on the road before the neighbors come up for the dance,” I said. “We can come back and try Clune’s chair another day. I’d like to see if he has it going any faster by then.”

“Before we go, if you’ll give me a minute.” Tilven slipped one thumb under the cuff of her other sleeve. “There’s something I’d like to ask you. I would very much like to see you dance with my mother. I wanted to ask at the top of the stairs.”

Tilven stepped sideways to leave the stair clear. The floor where I stood had a white scuff in it, the end of a heel mark that curved out from under one of the benches. I put my toe to it and thought how far a woman could have gone on one heel to make a mark that long. In the early weeks Tilven kept stepping straight forward whenever I tried to show her the turn I teach beginners. She said her mother always took a long step there, because she liked to feel the floor drop. I’d made Tilven show me with her right hand in mine. I wanted to feel it on the end of my arm now. I undid my buttons and put my coat into Tilven’s hands.

“Would you two bring a dance from your town for the first set next autumn?” Otta said. “We always start with one of ours. The people who do the best out of that are people like me who never have to learn a step new.”

“I can bring you a whole dance without a hill in it,” I said. “I’d like you to show me what you do on the hill first. What’s the quickest one?”

“The Empty Pannier,” Otta said. She took me to the high end while Clune tucked a folded cloth under his chin and tried the tune quietly. There was a low note at the end of each phrase that made me want to put my heels down. The grain underfoot ran the length of the hall except where three boards had been put in crosswise. She said to watch for those, they were the place to turn. She had a square little hand with a rough pad where the fingers closed and a stripe of white skin from a ring she’d taken off before we got there. She laced her fingers through mine and put her shoulder under our two hands, ready for the downhill run.

It started at a pace that would have suited my beginners. Otta planted her right foot at the turn, and there was a crack from one of the crosswise boards that would have made me stop the music at home to find out who was wearing hard heels, but before I could get my own foot across she had turned and run with most of my weight on her arm. The hall opened up below our joined hands, all the way across to Clune. I could hear his bow come rattling back up through the tune as Otta started to laugh and had to save her breath to keep us going. At the bottom she put her mouth almost to my ear.

“I put you in a chair,” she said, and this time there was enough breath to finish laughing. I could feel her laughing in every finger. Then she tucked her elbow in, the way Tilven always did when I’d given her a chance to go as fast as she liked, and we tried the turn again.

We got back to the chairs out of breath. The light was off the floor, and down in the sorting room somebody was sweeping. I could feel it through my soles every time the broom knocked against a post. Tilven brought us each a glass of the cordial. Otta caught a drop on her thumb where it had run down the glass and tasted it. She held her thumb there a little longer than she needed to.

“Does your mother put the peel in this, do you think? I used to keep my peels two days before I put them in anything, but they had such a way of curling up on me.”

“Ask her on Friday,” I said. “Come down to the dance. It’s an easy morning’s journey if you take the road by the cooper’s yard.”

“We could take the chair down,” Clune said. He set his glass on the bench and planted his boots wide apart to get both hands under the seat. “She’ll certainly want it at seventy-four. If she’s that fond of the last tune, I’d ask them to play it first, while there’s still time.”

“If you take that chair down to my mother,” I said, “you’ll have to take another one just like it. She’ll want to race.”

Otta put down her glass and lifted the quilt off the bench. “Would she mind if I had a little practice, so it was a fair thing when we met? Clune, you can go down to the other end and tell me how much room I’ve got.”

I’d barely set down my glass before she was settled with her feet on the board and the quilt over her lap, gripping the arms the way I had when it first moved. I held the chair while Clune took out the chock and went down to wait at the foot of the slope. Tilven put a toe out for our starting line and raised her hand when he reached the bottom. I’d intended to see how well it coasted on a very little shove.

“Come along, dear,” said Otta. “I felt how much you had in you at that turn.”

Tilven dropped her hand. I gave the chair a good running push and let go at the crosswise boards. Otta let go, too. She opened both arms as she had over the rail to catch us, and went clattering downhill with the red cap coming up off one ear.
