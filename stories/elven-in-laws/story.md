## Opening 1

The wheels used to wake the chickens. Otta remembers the evening her father took his turn around the Plum Loft with a white paper flower tucked into each ear. At every plank joint there was a cluck from the yard two doors down. Her father insisted on going round again to see whether the hen with the low voice would put in a word at the same place. Marrit might like that story if there’s a chance to tell it to her. Tilven says she has a laugh you can hear through a wall.

Otta has put the good quilt over the chair and another one down on a bench in case the first proves thin. She’s always thought she’d have an easy fifty years to get to know whoever Tilven brought home. Enough time to hear her tell one story at a wedding and the same story at a second wedding, this time with the part she’d left out. Then the letter came saying Marrit had accepted Tilven and that they’d had a lovely time for her forty-sixth birthday in May. It’s late in the year to be making plans for a human woman of forty-six. They’re already sorting the last plums of the season down below.

A wooden bin grazes one of the loft posts. Otta knows from the scrape of the split handle that it belongs to the woman who won’t let Clune do a thing about the split. He could take it home one evening and see if she notices. There’s Tilven on the stairs, asking somebody to leave the stool where it was.

“Marrit! Come the rest of the way when you’re ready. Oh, I’m so glad you said yes. I put my good coat on when we got the letter and went to weigh two sacks of plums. Everybody wanted to know why. I told every one of them.”

The woman appearing over the rail has tilted her head to hear and is still moving. Otta has a hand out for her. She can see over Marrit’s shoulder all the way to the square of cushion on the stair.

“Did you come up all those steps together? Clune wanted to put the stool higher, and I said, give her a halfway. I know what a halfway is worth.”

## Opening 2

I’d promised to let Tilven do the talking. I lasted halfway up the stairs at the Plum Loft, which I thought a creditable distance on a first visit to my future elven in-laws. Then I found a stool in the way and called up to ask if it belonged to anybody.

The stool had a cushion tied on. I found this out by picking it up by one corner, the way I clear the floor between classes. Below us, in the cold room that smelled of split plums, two people were arguing over the weight of a bin. I shifted the stool one step up and turned it parallel to the rail. Tilven reached past me to put it back.

“Give my mother a minute to remember where she’s left everything. She’s had all week to put things out for us.”

A woman leaned over the rail with Tilven’s face and red wool round the tips of her ears. “Oh, leave that down there, dear, we’ve got a much nicer seat for you up here. Let me look at you both.” She opened her arms awkwardly around the rail. I came up the rest of the steps in time to be the first one she caught. “I wish you could have seen Clune the day we got your news. He took the letter straight down to the plum sheds to read it out, and would you believe he was halfway through before he looked up and found me standing there. I was the one who’d fetched the post.”

Tilven had rehearsed her greeting so hard on the road to Ternstep that I caught her halfway through it once when she was asking after the price of a pair of gloves. Now she put her hand through my arm and said nothing at all.

“Forty-six,” Otta said, looking down at the stool. “And you never even sat on it. I told Clune you’d need one halfway up. Have you missed your pupils since you gave them up?”

“I see them every Thursday,” I said. “Some of them are even older than I am.”

I let go of Tilven to get out the pear cordial I’d brought. Otta took the bottle and drew my arm through hers. My performance on the stairs had come as a great surprise and I was beginning to enjoy the credit.
