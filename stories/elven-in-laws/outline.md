# Promised experience

The human is dating the adult child of elves who accept the match and badly misunderstand human ages. “Good news” and “Bad news” suggest the pleasure of real relief followed by increasingly disconcerting hospitality. I read “future elven in-laws” as an invitation to enjoy the human and the parents figuring each other out in the presence of the person they all love. The scale can be a family occasion that gets gratifyingly out of hand. I expect the payoff to work as a second laugh and a welcome the human can actually enjoy. The mistake, occasion, teller, and outcome are open.

## Takes considered

- **A first visit at the dance hall.** In-laws who reckon forty-six a very good age for a human prepare the dance they give elders who might miss the next winter's gathering. Their prospective daughter-in-law enjoys the best seat in the house until she finds out why it has wheels, then claims the right to choose what sort of dance the family will have.
- **A courtship by post.** An engaged human sends back a beautiful wedding invitation dated forty years hence. Across letters and skipped years, the eager parents keep getting her remaining time and the ages of her growing family wrong, until a letter asks them to pack for the wedding before they've agreed on what to call the year.
- **The novices' set.** An elven father tells on himself as he tries to introduce his daughter's seventy-year-old human fiancé to the young adults at a neighborhood dance. When the supposedly green suitor asks him to partner the fastest set, the father realizes he will have to let himself be led.

The first visit gives the same warm impulse an honor worth accepting, an alarming meaning, and a physical comic reversal. The guest can enjoy the distinction before she has to decide what to do with it.

## Provisional direction

Marrit, a forty-six-year-old dance teacher, comes to Ternstep to meet her adult elven fiancée Tilven's parents. Otta and Clune are keen to welcome her as family. Based on the one human they knew well, they believe forty-six is late old age. They have set aside the first set of the evening at the Plum Loft for a Last Dance. When an elder may miss the following year's gathering, the family offers a choice of music and a wheeled chair for a turn around the floor. There's a place in the dance whatever a person's legs can do. The ceremony is an offer, and Marrit can turn it down.

Marrit can tell they think she's old. She enjoys the quilt and the honor of choosing the music before she realizes they are making sure she gets one dance before she dies. She wants them to imagine having her around. She explains her age in the terms she has at hand, including her seventy-four-year-old mother's Friday dances. Otta asks the couple to bring a dance from Marrit's town for the first set next autumn. Marrit takes her up on it and asks Otta, who's been holding herself ready to push the chair, to show her the quickest dance she knows tonight. Clune has a fresh, equally confident idea about what Marrit's mother needs.

## Craft plan

- **Teller and voice.** Try Marrit telling it in first person past, happy to take a little credit for having walked into it, and close third present from Otta, whose satisfaction at getting everything ready outruns what she can see. Marrit hears pace in talk and watches where people put their weight. Give quick comic observations room to lengthen into unsettled thought when she realizes what the parents expect of her future.
- **Cast.** Marrit wants a place with the parents and wants Tilven to let her speak for herself. She mistook Tilven's nerves for a warning about their welcome. She handles trouble by doing something physical, then makes fun of her own fuss. Tilven, 143 and long grown, learned human ages while living among humans and put Marrit's age in a letter home. She expects to help Marrit past her family's formality and has no idea what they've prepared. Her sentences are measured and careful. She wants the visit to be one she'd happily repeat with Marrit, so she'll speak up if Marrit laughs off a hurt and starts home. Otta wants Marrit to feel she belongs tonight and her daughter to admire how well she's thought of everything. She piles up particulars, especially when she fears she's done harm, and seldom finishes where she meant to. Clune wants a verdict on his chair. He shares Otta's estimate of human ages and speaks in brief practical proposals. He can happily watch Otta make a fuss until anyone touches the wheel chocks. All four are original characters.
- **The conversation owed.** In the long scene Marrit and the parents need to speak plainly about why they think she's close to dying, how old she is in a human life, and what they are trying to give her tonight. Otta answers Marrit in order, from the touring fiddler who used to claim the Loft stairs would finish her, to the news of the fiddler's death at forty-nine. Otta wants Marrit to see why the age in Tilven's letter made a welcome feel urgent. Marrit wants to hear what Otta was hoping they might still get to do together.
- **Place.** The Plum Loft in Ternstep wins over Spindle Hall, whose banquet fixes people in their seats, and Larch Yard, where a crowd would pressure Marrit to play along. On a cold late-autumn afternoon, sorters weigh the last bins downstairs. The family has the loft to itself before the village dance. Red sun stripes through the slats, bruised plums sweeten the stair, rosin catches at the soles, cold leaks up the uncovered landing, and the chair's iron rims rattle over every plank joint. The floor slopes toward the fiddler's end, so Clune has brought chocks. Couples pick up speed on the downhill turn. Marrit's mother's Friday dance is a morning's journey away.

## Scene briefs

1. **Late afternoon, the Loft just cleared.** Otta tells the two women how glad she is they've found each other, then marvels that Marrit passed the stool she left halfway up the stairs for a forty-six-year-old. Later she asks how long ago Marrit gave up her pupils. Marrit says she still teaches. She figures they think she's a bit past it and lets Otta tuck a quilt over her knees. Clune wants her verdict on the fine chair and gives her first choice of music. It rolls an inch when she shifts. About a fifth.
2. **Long scene and owed conversation, the empty floor.** When Clune pulls the quilt clear of the wheel and frees the chair, Tilven recognizes it from her grandfather's Last Dance. Otta says why they expect this to be Marrit's last chance to dance at the Loft. Marrit slows Tilven's explanation so she can give her own. A joke about the stairs and news of the fiddler's death at forty-nine explain why Otta rushed the welcome. Marrit tells them what her mother does at seventy-four. When Marrit starts to laugh the whole visit off, Tilven asks her to stay for the dance she came for. Otta asks them to teach the family a set from Marrit's town at next autumn's opening. For tonight Marrit wants Otta's quickest set. Clune plays and the two women hang on to each other for the downhill turn. About three fifths.
3. **After the set, at the high end of the Loft.** Clune thinks he can at least offer the chair to Marrit's mother. Otta wants to meet her and help work out how to receive her. Marrit invites them to come on a Friday and tells them why her mother would want a second chair. Otta gets into the first one herself. About a fifth. Fold the couple's courtship and correspondence into brief recollections. Leave the wedding and the larger dance offstage.

## Ending

Marrit says her mother would want somebody to race. The roll of the chair on arrival, the care with which Clune chocked the wheels, and Otta's eagerness to get onto the floor come together when Otta asks Marrit for a push and the wheels clatter downhill. The parents will get to meet Marrit's mother, and they've got something entertaining to ask her besides her age.
