# The Noon Hare

Avice will tell you she gave me a head start. Ask her how a person carrying the hare can give the chaser a head start. She’ll say she gave me twenty-six years, which is a generous way of remembering how long it took her to ask me up to Crowsteps for a race.

I’d been there plenty. I’d do the counting and hold the coats. One year I had four good coats hung on the gate and the only person who put a hand in a pocket for me was a child eating a pear, who gave me the stalk. From that gate you could see most of the race. You could see even better if you climbed up and put your foot in somebody’s sleeve, which is where the counting generally went to pieces. At the full moon Avice could go over the upper wall as a wolf in a single jump. We climbed the steps after her, by which time she had generally done something else worth seeing.

That day it was a week past the full moon and the sun was high enough to put the gate’s shadow directly under it. Avice had the hare on top of the gate and was pulling a gorse thorn out of its eye with her teeth. The eye was a jet button. I felt a little pull in my own eyelid and looked down the field, where the wind off the sea was turning the grass to show its undersides.

“Give me a look at your belt,” she said. “I want to see how the hare will sit in it.”

“You want me to carry it.”

“In three weeks. I’ve put another seam in the belly, see? Less of it to stuff down the front of yourself that way. When your father carried it he had the whole thing in the same pocket as a piece of pie. He brought the pie out with him because he was looking forward to eating it on the far side of the finish slate. I smelled the butter from halfway across the fold. If you want to bring something you can eat an apple first and let me make something of the trail.”

It took Avice three weeks to get another night in her wolf’s skin. At noon she had her own excellent teeth and the boots she’d been breaking in since the last rain. My mother’s people had been werewolves too, before some of them went to live among the shepherds. The families stayed there long enough that their children grew up to change on mornings and evenings that had nothing to do with the moon. Their other shapes got shorter in the leg and rounder in the face with each generation. By my grandmother’s time they were weredogs. Mum could be a dog in the time it took her toast to go brown. I got her legs, her afternoon hours, and one of her ears. The other ear is bent down the middle and I lay that on my father’s side, where the hats all fit badly.

“Put it in your belt, Aunt,” I said. “See how fast you can take it over.”

She lifted the hare clear of the gate. “I’m offering you your father’s turn.”

“I want to catch it. If you’ve time enough to take it up to the slate, I’ve time enough to try to get it off you.”

Avice had a queer way of going still when she took an interest. A nose showed a little farther forward and both shoulders settled, even if she was only looking across a table at somebody with a piece of news. She looked at me that way now, with the hare pinned under her hand.

“Thirty to start me, and you can take it off me any way you like. If my foot’s on the slate first, you carry it at the full moon. If you get the hare first, you have it for a month. I want you to count the whole thirty. The way you stood on your father’s sleeves I used to hear you drop numbers to come up and see.”

“You’ll get the whole thirty.”

She stuffed the hare in her belt and went at one. Its ears, hanging out behind her, snapped in the wind. At five there was a wrinkle in my left ear with the wind blowing straight into it, and the stones I’d been judging by the height of the gate had got up almost to eye level. I counted on the inside after that. In my mother’s shape my tongue had other things to do.

The lane from the lower gate went round the end of the dividing wall to the upper gate, where Avice would come in again. I watched her red stockings through the bars until the bend took her. Then there was the sound her soles made on the hard lane, quite distinct from the little ticks of grit she sent out to either side, and for several counts after that there was the hare. The cloth had the smell of the old coat it had been cut out of, and her smell was warm in it where she had kept it in her lap to sew. I had always liked sitting near her when she sewed. If she was having difficulty she would tell you everything.

There was a mouse tunnelling in the grass under the gatepost. By twenty my short black legs were making little starts toward it every time it stirred. At thirty I had my nose through the bars. The hare’s smell had gone thin in the lane. Then it came down warm over my back with a gust from the upper fold. Avice had come in through her gate. I turned and ran along my side of the dividing wall with the scent coming down over it.

This was the wall the sheep could go through, one at a time with their heads down. The opening was halfway along and it fetched up low against rising ground on the other side. Sheep had left a yellow handkerchief’s worth of wool hanging from the lintel. I put my ears back and went through at a run. There was a stone jutting out where it would have caught a wolf in the shoulder. The whole length of my back went under it. The wool whisked the top of my head and I came clear with the wind up my ear again.

For a few strides the grass was the only thing I could see moving in the upper fold. There were breaks in the gorse where the grass just showed through. Above it all was the upper wall. The steps we climbed on chase nights stuck out of the face of it, two to the left and two to the right, the way you might put your hands out to slow a fall. I had only to run straight up and get between the wall and Avice.

I had a good look at her about then. She was cutting up from the upper gate by a route I’d watched her take on every full moon when the quarry came this way. She knew where the gorse dipped low enough for a wolf to go over and where the wolves had worn a clean path beneath it. She used the path where she fitted and jumped two of the low beds in her boots. I picked my own gap, close enough to see the hare’s uneven back rising and falling under her belt. A gorse twig caught me beside the nose. By the time I had whipped back out of the gap and got around the end of the bush, she was the length of a coat ahead.

There is a pale rock at the foot of the upper wall that has never been in the shade for as long as I’ve known it. I was so near behind her as she put her foot on it that one of the hare’s legs brushed my nose. Last week I’d stood up on the gate to see her reach that same rock. The big forefeet landed there together, with the rest of her coming behind them so fast it looked as if the rock had thrown her. I saw the pale belly that she kept tucked up in a run, and the back feet coming under it. Then she was away on the far side. The treads beside her had stayed empty.

Now the pale rock gave her the height for the first step. She swung straight from the rock to the tread and pulled herself higher with one hand. The hare went with her, above the height I could get my mouth to. There was ample room for my teeth on the edge of a step. I wanted something higher up and farther away than that. I wanted a hand and I wanted it already out in front of me.

During the change I had the taste of wool for company. My mouth drew in under my nose and my teeth lost their places. My forefeet were up against the wall and then they had knuckles. The skin on the heel of one palm dragged against the stone as the weight came down. I put my fingers over the top of the first tread, got a boot on it, and raised myself so fast my knee struck the step above. Avice had one leg over the wall. As she hiked the other one up, the back of her belt came level with my eyes and the hare’s legs hung over it.

I got my toes on the second tread and shut my hand on a wool foot. It gave, which I thought at first was the seam going, but what gave was her belt as she bent to drop to the slate. For about as long as it took her foot to disappear I had her by the hare. I pulled again, hard enough this time to have something left in my hand when her feet struck the other side.

The hare felt absurdly small now there was nobody wearing it. One ear lay over my wrist. I climbed the rest of the way and sat on the wall. Avice was bending over the slate with her hands on her thighs. I could see where she had struck one shin on a tread and made a white square of her stocking. I had thought of showing her the hare held up by one ear, the way she showed it to us on moon nights, but it occurred to me just then that she was going to say she’d been giving me a practice. If the run was only a favor, she could take it back with the hare and ask me to bring both up to Crowsteps in three weeks. I laid the thing flat across my knees.

“Which bit of it did you get?” she said.

“One of the feet. Were you still on the wall when I took it?”

“I had both feet off it. In the air I had already started thinking what I was going to make you bring in your pockets next month. I had got as far as a raw onion.” She pressed a thumb against the white patch on her shin and left it there. “You got it before I came down. Your mother will ask which foot, so have a look and see.”

“You’ll tell them at supper?”

“I’m wearing these stockings to supper. It’ll be hard to get through the meal without somebody asking.”

“I’d like you to tell them I caught you. I’ve heard you tell a catch so many times I can picture where you’d stop to take a drink.”

She stood upright. “When I tell it, the other people at the table will be lucky to get a drink. Your mother can tell me how near the wall she’d have gone before changing. I got my hand on the first tread and there was a dog at the bottom of the wall. I looked down again from the top and there were your hands on the very next step. I was curious about that. I was also furious, and I’d like time to get to the furious part.”

I had been sitting there with one heel in my palm and the knee I’d struck stuck out in the air. I put the heel down. My boot struck the wall with a good heavy thump, which made me think of a dozen times I’d have liked to get up there and hear her object to having been beaten. I caught myself with my hand already on the hare’s ear. I held it up this time. Its feet swung out from the wall in the wind, and my aunt tipped her head back to see it clear.

“I thought I’d have you at the first wall,” I said. “Then you went up through that gorse.”

“Oh, I was going to have you at the first wall. I put the good end of the hare into the wind as soon as I was through the gate. I thought you’d run along down below wondering which way to go for it, and I’d have you following me all the way round. I should have remembered the time I went after your mother through that creep. The night we chased your father before they married. Your mother went through the hole like a shuttle through a loom, and I came up behind her and stuck my head in after her. There was fleece then, too. By the time I got it out of my mouth and went round to the gate, she’d got your father’s hare. She stood up on two feet and shouted, ‘Again tomorrow.’ I told her next moon. I knew how to get your father up out of bed again, and I could trust him to come. I wanted her back in the chase worse than I wanted him, but I wanted the four feet I’d had that night.”

“Did she come back?”

“Next moon, yes, and she got to your father first again. He took it very well, because she kissed him while she had him up against a wall.” She patted the dust off her shin, twice and smartly. “I’d have made her work in the daylight. I should have gone.”

I rubbed the hare’s belly and felt the seam she had put there, the little ridge the needle had left in the old cloth. I had thought about bringing the hare home and putting it where my father had to move it off his chair. I could still do that. My aunt took a breath all the way down, then let it out and looked up at my end of the wall as if she intended to follow me there. I wanted to see her coming.

“I could carry it now,” I said. “If you’d like to try and catch me.”

Avice picked a stem of grass out of the seam of her stocking. “Where do you mean to go with it?”

“I’ll keep to this fold. You can start as soon as you’re over.”

I slid down into the upper fold, where we’d come from, and waited with the hare in one hand. The first thing over the wall was Avice’s hand. Then her head came up into the sun and she looked straight down at me. I stayed right there where she could see me until she had both feet on a tread. She had done her hair up tight that morning, and one of the pins was halfway out. I was glad she had left it hanging. She might have asked me to hold it for her.

I set the hare on the pale rock. I went down on one knee to be at a good height for it and came out of my boot soles into the grass, with my mouth open. The warm seam went broad across my tongue. A length of the thread I’d caught on my thumb tickled my gum. With the hare in my mouth I waved my tail once. It moved again while I was standing there thinking about it.

Avice came off the last step and made for me so quick I nearly dropped the hare. I swerved round the gorse with the two long ears snapping alongside my face. Down by the dividing wall the gap stood clear, near as a second shadow. I went past it and out into the grass where Avice could run. She cut inside my turn and for a couple of strides she was ahead of me. I could hear her making the little breathy sound she makes at table when she has got to the part of a story she likes.

I drew ahead of her down by the gate, where the grass turns short and flat. When I heard her slow I bent round and came straight back at her. She had both hands out by the time I got there, and this time I ran close enough for the hare’s ear to go through her fingers.
