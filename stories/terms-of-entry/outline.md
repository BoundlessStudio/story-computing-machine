# The Second Door

Provisional direction; the finished prose may find a better route.

Della is an amateur stage magician who has finally secured a paid evening at the shabby Bellwether variety theatre. She wants to revive the cabinet act she built with Sahar, her former performing partner. Sahar left after too many evenings of being announced as Della's assistant; she now has her own modest, skilful act and has agreed to discuss one shared booking. Della has lived with dissociative identity disorder for years. Amos and June share that life, sometimes with overlapping awareness and sometimes with missing time. Their tastes and plans already complicate a supposedly solo career: Amos misses a particular place and prefers gardening to applause; June loves noisy radio serials and has been secretly teaching herself to sing.

A recent unexplained light over the city has given many residents powers. Della can hold a small object motionless in the air. June can make her voice or another nearby sound audible on the other side of a solid wall. Amos can make a door open onto another doorway he knows personally, but must keep a hand on the source handle to sustain it. Letting go, or ceasing to be the identity sustaining it, restores the source door's normal destination. People and things can cross; a completed crossing leaves them at the other place when the passage ends. Amos cannot take their whole body through while keeping that hand at the source. We will establish this through a small, intelligible experiment rather than a lecture, and will not place anyone halfway through a closing portal.

Della sees a chance to make the old cabinet trick astonishing and to persuade Sahar that this time they will both matter. The theatre's impresario offers a much better fee for a real disappearance and return, then advertises the booking around Della's newly discovered powers. Della accepts too quickly. Sahar objects partly because the promised return requires Amos to remain present, which Della cannot promise, and partly because Della has again decided what Sahar's job will be. Amos also wants to use his door to visit the familiar distant garden, not spend every available evening rehearsing someone else's act. June has interests in the show without becoming a conveniently summoned sound effect. No rehearsal schedule makes switching an on-demand technique.

In rehearsal, the doorway works, then does not. Sahar has crossed into a service room elsewhere in this same theatre; when the passage changes, she is left there and must take the long ordinary route back. The failure is embarrassment and broken timing rather than a rescue crisis. It exposes the physical problem and the old relationship problem at once. Sahar spots that the cabinet's concealed side exit, removed in Della's excitement about genuine magic, could connect to the adjoining stage wing. A return from the service room can use a short backstage corridor and that exit regardless of who is present. The cabinet must be positioned and rebuilt by someone who understands theatrical sightlines. Sahar does. The possible feat shrinks from a teleportation across the city to a hybrid of a real nearby doorway and a very good ordinary illusion.

Della must surrender the advertised grand miracle and its enlarged fee, paying back an advance she has already spent repairing the old cabinet. A possible real cost is selling the cabinet itself to the theatre and agreeing to perform with borrowed, smaller apparatus afterward. Sahar will not return to being an assistant or promise that one good night repairs the friendship. Della makes the change anyway, rather than demanding that an identity sustain a performance for her or that Sahar accept the old arrangement in exchange for success.

The rebuilt trick must stand as an ordinary illusion without any portal, so no cue requires Amos to appear. The final act should be imperfect, surprising, and funny rather than a perfectly timed tour of three powers. If a genuine doorway occurs, it can reveal something wonderfully untheatrical; an ordinary concealed door gets Sahar back into the picture. We may finish after a smaller shared effect, or with Sahar inviting a future rehearsal on her own terms. The last image should make the useful second door and the cost of keeping it felt, without claiming DID has been cured, all identities now agree, or an audience's applause settles the relationship.
