# The Warning Comes Second

For the third time, the white stone is ahead of me.

It stands where the wheat meets the sky, a square no bigger than a garden shed and so bright in the noon sun that I have to narrow my eyes. I have been walking away from it for one thousand nine hundred paces. I kept the sun on my left cheek. I kept my phone compass on west. Behind me, I bent a stalk every ten steps, making a dark seam through the gold.

The seam is still behind me. Straight as a ruled line, it dwindles until distance drinks it. The stone is ahead.

Wind passes over the field in long strokes. Near me, the ripe heads bow and lift with a dry whisper; farther out, whole acres darken, brighten, darken again. There is no tree, roof, mast, or hill anywhere. Blue fills more of the world than land. Under so much sky, the stone should look trivial. Instead, because it is the only upright thing, the field seems arranged around it.

I walk toward it because every way away has become a way back.

By the time I reach it, sweat has dried white at the collar of my shirt. A wheat awn has opened the side of my thumb. The cut still shines. That matters. I have been noting what matters since my first return.

The stone is a single block of white marble. Four upright faces, each square, each perhaps eight feet high and eight feet wide. Fine gray veins enter near its base and vanish halfway up. No mortar joins it to a foundation. The wheat grows to the marble on all sides, though on this first side its shadow has pressed the nearest stalks flat.

I set down my pack and drink. The bottle is less than half full. It does not replenish when the field brings me back.

On my first walk I followed the stone's shadow. On my second I held the compass bearing and tore a strip from my red scarf to tie onto the wheat. The strip is gone, or somewhere beyond sight, or tied to a stalk I have not yet reached. On my third I counted. None of it has made a border.

The marble is cool under my palm, impossibly cool after hours in the sun. I walk the length of the face and count nine long strides. At the corner, the edge is sharp enough to cast a hairline shadow. Beyond it, the second face shines blank and white.

I do not cross yet.

My pack gives me another two feet of height when I stand on it, but the top edge remains a handspan beyond my fingers. I jump. My fingertips swipe warm air. I try again from the other end. The face stays eight feet high from the ground and more than eight feet high from the pack. After the fourth attempt I step back, panting, and the proportions are ordinary once more.

There are no holds, no door, no mark I can see. Only the corner.

I pick up the pack, put my left palm flat on the first face, and step around the edge.

Noon becomes late afternoon.

The change has no passage in it. Sunlight strikes my right eye, low and amber. My shadow, which had lain tight beneath me, leaps across the wheat in one black piece. The wind drops. In the sudden quiet I hear a child breathing hard through his nose.

I know that breath.

The field remains around me, gold to every horizon, but a narrow hallway lies through it. Brown carpet runs beneath the wheat. Flowered paper covers the second marble face. I am ten years old again, crouched behind the door to our parents' room with one sock showing. My younger brother is searching for me. He always breathes through his nose when he is trying not to laugh.

The two moments occupy my body at once. My adult hand rests on cold marble. My child's hand rests on sun-warmed wallpaper, then goes cold from the center of the palm to the wrist. Wheat whispers in a house where no wheat grows.

Familiarity floods me. I know the fold in the carpet before I look down. I know my brother will peer around the door at knee height because he thinks I will be watching higher. I know what he will say, though I had not known any of it the first time.

His face appears below the handle, nine years old and delighted.

“I can see your sock.”

The memory ends where it ended then: with me standing too quickly and saying, *This already happened.* With him laughing because we had played the same game all summer. Yet I remember the cold in my palm. I remember the noise of grain. For years I kept those two impossible details and lost everything around them.

The hallway thins. Carpet becomes soil. My brother's face passes into bright wheat and is gone.

I am standing beside the second face of the marble block. My palm is still cold. The cut on my thumb still shines. The sun hangs a few degrees above the western horizon, although my watch says seven minutes have passed.

Something is carved into the marble at chest height. The letters are shallow and uneven, cut by a point harder than the blade of my pocketknife. I clear white dust from the grooves with my fingertips.

**IF YOU'VE EVER HAD DÉJA VU, THEN IT'S TOO LATE.**

I read the sentence until the words stop behaving like language.

Then I walk away from the second face.

I aim into the stone's long shadow, keeping my back to the carved warning. The sun lowers. My shadow merges with the marble's and stretches ahead like a road. I walk until the stone is too small to cover with my thumb. I walk until thirst makes my tongue feel padded. At five hundred paces I turn my head only enough to check the sun.

The white block stands ahead of me, close enough to show its gray veins.

I do not remember turning. My footprints run straight behind me. When I reach the block, the warning is on the face that receives me.

Nothing in my body has gone back. The water I drank is gone. Dust coats my ankles. The cut on my thumb has bled down to the first joint. My watch has advanced another twenty-three minutes while the sun has nearly touched the wheat.

I take bread from my pack. It was a dense heel cut that morning, wrapped with cheese in waxed paper. Now it is warm. Not sun-warm: oven-warm, soft at the center, smelling of yeast. Steam touches my lip when I tear it.

I eat because wonder does not keep a body upright. The bread tastes fresh enough to be indecent. The cheese has not changed.

At the corner to my right, sunset burns along the stone's edge. The warning is behind me if I go on. There is no promise on the third face, but there is no road in any other direction.

I round the second corner.

The sun drops out.

Stars appear in a dark blue vault, thousands more than I have seen above any town. The marble holds the last daylight and glows faintly among black wheat. A seed head brushes the bare inside of my wrist.

White tiles flash across the field. A bus station. Fluorescent light. Rain scribbling on glass.

I am seventeen, sitting on a metal bench with my brother. He is sixteen and has spent our last coins on a chocolate bar neither of us likes. We missed the late bus after an exam because he insisted on returning a wallet he found in the toilets. We have been arguing about whether our parents will punish honesty more than lateness.

In that old moment, something dry scratches my wrist beneath my coat. The smell of ripe wheat fills the station. I look behind the bench and find only tile.

Then I know the vending machine will cough twice. I know the chocolate will break crooked. I know my brother will give me the larger half while claiming not to notice.

The knowledge comes a heartbeat before each thing.

“Again?” he asks. He knows my face. This is the second time I have told him the world has repeated itself.

“Something is going to happen.”

“The bus, ideally.”

“I mean something bad.”

He studies me, no longer teasing. “Or something already happened and your head filed it late.”

The vending machine coughs. Once. Twice.

The station disappears. I am back under the stars with my hand clamped around my wrist. A red line from the wheat awn rises on the skin exactly where the old impossible scratch began.

My brother's words remain, preserved not because he understood but because he did not. *Already happened. Filed late.* I had argued with him all the way home.

The bread in my hand is hard now. Its crust scrapes my mouth, and the middle has the dry resistance of a loaf left out for days. Quantity remains. Age does not. When I reach into my pocket, the wheat head I plucked earlier has softened to green. A bead of milk gathers in one crushed kernel.

The field's tricks are almost domestic. Bad bread. Green grain. A watch that cannot agree with the sky. That smallness frightens me more than thunder would.

I look back without rounding the corner. The second face is hidden by the edge. To see the warning again, I would have to cross a corner. It arrived after the first seizure of recognition, not before. Whoever cut it had learned that much.

Perhaps the next face belongs to another old moment. I can name two more times when déjà vu struck hard enough to make me stop what I was doing.

One of them was the night my brother called.

The third face is blank. Starlight catches in the marble veins. I sit with my back against it, drink once, and wait for my pulse to slow. I could remain here until the water is gone. I could walk straight and let the field return me. Those are choices only in the sense that thirst is a choice.

The third corner waits at my right shoulder. I know which room lies beyond it. The certainty itself feels like the beginning of the old sensation.

I go around.

Night deepens, wiping the last blue from the sky. The field tilts without moving. My kitchen opens in the darkness.

Four years ago. Rain at the window. A cup with a cracked blue rim beside my laptop. My phone lighting on the table with the word **BROTHER**.

We have not spoken for five months. The quarrel began over our father's tools after the funeral and spread backward until it seemed to include our entire childhood. I said he turned every loss into a test of love. He said I called control practicality so I would never have to admit I wanted anything. Both remarks were accurate enough to wound and crude enough to be useless.

In the field I can feel marble at my spine and wheat against my knees. In the kitchen I watch my younger hand hover over the ringing phone.

Then comes the déjà vu I have punished myself with ever since. Pressure behind the eyes. A voice with the meaning rubbed out. The certainty that I have sat beside the cracked cup before and that whatever I do next has already hurt me.

I believed that certainty was a warning. Not then, perhaps. Then I was still angry enough to use any feeling as permission. I pressed the side button and silenced the call.

In the field, I throw myself toward the kitchen.

Wheat stops my legs. The marble edge remains beneath my hand. The room is close enough to show rain trembling on the glass and impossibly far away, a flat depth laid over the night.

“Answer!” I shout.

My past hand pauses above the phone.

This is the pause I remember. This is the voiceless pressure. My cry reaches that kitchen without its word, stripped down to urgency and recognition. For one breath I think urgency may be enough.

My thumb presses the button.

The screen goes dark.

Nothing changes. Nothing was ever going to change. My attempt is not a new message entering the past. It is the old sensation's source.

The kitchen holds for a few seconds more. I know what follows outside this room. My brother leaves no message. The next morning, a neighbor finds him collapsed on the landing outside his flat, a split paper bag and oranges against the wall. A vessel in his brain, the doctor said. Sudden. Perhaps an answered call would have changed ten minutes. Perhaps it would have changed nothing except the last words between us. I have made whole lives from both possibilities.

The overlay closes before I can touch the phone. I am on my knees at the fourth face of the stone, my throat raw from shouting.

The warning is clear now. For me, at least, it is not a threat and it is not prophecy. The feeling came first in my life, but the event that made it comes later, here. Once I had felt cold marble in our hallway, once wheat scratched me in the bus station, this circuit was already part of the history that produced those moments. The sentence could never steer me away. Reading it proves I have arrived too late to make its warning useful.

Too late to avoid the field. Too late to answer that call. Too late to become the person who answered it.

The sky has begun to pale along one rim. The wheat there is colorless, each head edged in dew. I take the hard bread from its paper and bite. It cracks against my teeth. In my pocket, the green wheat head has split; two white roots curl around the lining without soil.

I round the fourth corner.

Dawn opens a second kitchen.

I am sitting on my brother's floor three days after the funeral. Cupboards stand open around me. I am supposed to be sorting what can be kept. Instead, I am eating toast made from the last bread in his freezer, because it was easier to operate the toaster than decide who deserved his plates.

The toast is hot at one edge and stale at the other. Pale light makes a square on the linoleum. My cut thumb throbs in the field; in the kitchen, the same pulse passes through an uninjured hand. For one suspended moment I know the taste, the light, the exact angle of a cupboard door. Déjà vu lays itself over grief and makes grief feel arranged.

My past self lowers the toast and whispers, “You tried to warn me.”

No one answers. No one answered then.

Now I know what the sensation carried: this mouthful of bread aging around the marble, this dawn, this pressure in my thumb. It was never my brother's message. The knowledge does not excuse the hand that silenced his call or turn his death into machinery. But the jolt contains no instruction I failed to understand. There is nothing left to decode.

The kitchen fades gently. For a moment I smell burned toast and wheat together. Then the floor is earth again.

I have returned to the first face.

Morning lies pale and level across the field. The marble is white rather than gold, its gray veins quiet under the surface. The straps of my pack still pull at my shoulders; at the stone's base, wheat remains flattened where I first set it down. On the face before me there is no carving.

Beyond the stone, a hedgerow cuts the horizon.

It is so ordinary that I distrust it: thorns, elder leaves, one ash tree leaning over a gap. Through that gap I see a strip of gray road. The wheat between here and there lies in no pattern I recognize. No bent stalks. No scarf. No trace of my three attempted departures.

I take out my phone. It has one bar of battery and no signal. The clock insists less than two hours have passed. Dew soaks my boots. Birds call from the hedge as they call in early morning.

From just beyond the corner on my right comes my brother's breathless childhood laugh.

“I can see your sock,” he says.

The words are exact. The first overlap is beginning again on the second face, or has never stopped beginning there. If I step around, I may see him at nine. Then at sixteen. Then I may reach the ringing phone again. I know now that I cannot alter it, but alteration is not the only thing hunger wants. I could stand in those rooms. I could hear him give me half a bad chocolate bar forever, circuit by circuit, while the bread hardens and softens and the wheat takes root in my clothes.

The hedge is visible now. It was not visible before the four corners closed. I do not know whether it will remain if I begin another turn.

My brother laughs again, fixed in the moment when he was alive.

I tighten the straps and walk toward the hedge.

The white stone stays behind me. I know because its shadow no longer reaches mine. I do not look back. The road vanishes whenever the wheat rises between steps, then returns in the gap. A motor passes. Its sound grows, travels from left to right, and diminishes in the sensible order.

At the field's edge, a drainage rut holds brown water and last year's leaves. I step across it. My phone finds a signal and corrects itself to the following morning.

The road bends around the ash tree. I follow it. Morning stays morning.
