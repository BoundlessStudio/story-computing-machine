# The Warning Comes Second

The street-door lock turned cleanly under Halen Corren's pick. Five pins, no grit in the plug, no bent tailpiece. She felt each tiny surrender through the tension wrench and told herself what she had told nervous tenants for eleven years: any door she opened, she could open again.

The condemned block disagreed in no obvious way. Its entrance gave onto a square vestibule papered with demolition notices. Beyond the wired-glass fire door, six flights of terrazzo stair climbed around a dry central well. The emergency lights still glowed. Dust silvered the handrail. Nothing moved except the slow return of the street door against her boot.

Halen shoved a rubber wedge beneath it until the wood bit. On the wall above, she drew an arrow in blue survey chalk and wrote **STREET**.

Her phone showed 3:18 a.m. Four hours until the demolition crew was due to begin stripping fixtures next door, six until the excavator arrived here. More than enough time to collect an idiot brother.

She called him as she clipped the pick case shut. “I'm inside.”

For three seconds she heard only scraping and Joss's breath. Then: “Don't take the east stair.”

“There are two stairs. Which one did you use?”

“The east one.”

“What floor?”

“Fourth. I think. There's a four painted on the landing, but I've seen it twice.”

She pushed through the fire door. “Do you have the tin?”

Another scrape. “I found it.”

Not yes. Halen let that wait. “Stand still. Make noise every thirty seconds.”

“Been trying the standing-still plan.”

The call broke into soft electronic grit. Halen said his name, got nothing, and started up.

She had surveyed the building two weeks ago for the clearance contract. Six floors, eight flats to a floor, east and west stairs joined by one straight hall. The east stair had a squeak on every third tread because its metal nosings had loosened in a pattern a builder would have corrected and a landlord had not. First landing: squeak. Second: squeak. At each fire door she wrote the floor number over the old painted one and added an upward arrow. She wedged each door open with a strip of folded cedar from her bag.

Joss knocked somewhere above her: three quick blows, then two. Their childhood signal for *where are you?* It came faintly down the stairwell.

Halen struck the rail three times with her flashlight. “Coming.”

On the fourth floor, the door marked 4 opened into a hall of stripped apartments. Its wallpaper had once been yellow. A radiator ticked despite the dead boiler, metal giving up the day's stored warmth. Halfway down, a white door bore a tarnished 4B.

She checked her phone. No signal now. Joss had said the flat number on his first call, before it broke: their mother's last apartment, 4D. Halen had cleared it in June. The red tin had not been there then because Joss had moved it to a storage cupboard without telling her.

She went back one landing to make certain she had not mistaken 4B for 5B. The cedar wedge waited where she had left it. The painted numeral was 3. She climbed again.

The third loose tread squeaked. Dust lay in an undisturbed crescent against the riser. The fire door at the top had a triangular chip missing from its green paint, and before Halen touched the push bar she knew the light beyond it would buzz twice. She knew a silver screw would lie on the landing like a dropped tooth. She knew the next breath would carry the hot-wool smell of an old vacuum cleaner.

All of it happened.

The certainty went through her from scalp to heels, so total that her hand clenched on the rail. It was not recollection. Recollection had edges: a key blank's number, the angle of a latch, her mother's kitchen radio losing the same station every time the kettle boiled. This had none. It arrived complete and empty, familiarity without a source.

The light buzzed twice.

Halen opened the door. Scratched into the paint on its far side, deep enough to show bare steel, were the words:

**IF YOU'VE EVER HAD DÉJA VU, THEN IT'S TOO LATE.**

She read them twice. The second reading irritated her. Almost everyone had had déjà vu. The sentence was theatre, the kind frightened people wrote when they wanted fear to look like knowledge.

“Joss?” she called.

A hammer struck somewhere above. Three, then two.

She followed her arrows down.

At the bottom of the stair, the blue **STREET** waited over the green door. Her cedar wedge held it wide. Beyond should have been the papered vestibule and the street door propped to the night.

Beyond was a laundry room.

Two industrial sinks stood beneath a row of windows blackened with paint. An abandoned dryer faced the door, its round glass eye furred with lint. Halen stopped with one boot on either side of the threshold. The room smelled of damp plaster and scorched soap.

She checked the chalk. Her letters. Her wedge, shaved from a paint-stirrer with the notch she always cut near the thick end. She had not marked the wrong door.

The laundry had another fire door in the wall to her left. Through its wired glass she saw a square vestibule papered with demolition notices. A blue arrow pointed toward a street door. A rubber wedge held that street door open three inches onto darkness.

Halen crossed the laundry, fast now. Static snapped from her sleeve to the push bar. She hissed and yanked the door open.

The vestibule beyond was hers down to the curl in the top notice. But through the wedged street door lay not the pavement. There was another laundry room, its sinks along the opposite wall.

Her phone said 3:31. The stopwatch she had started outside showed thirteen minutes and forty-two seconds. It had not paused. Her last call to Joss remained in the log.

Halen went back to the stair because a building could contain two laundries and a contractor could misread an old plan. She climbed to the landing where she had first felt the blank familiarity. The scratch on the green door was still there. So was the silver screw. She drew a box around it in chalk, then descended without taking her eyes from the stair numbers.

The bottom opened into a second laundry.

This one had three sinks. The dryer stood on the other side. No chalk marked its door.

She returned upward to find her boxed screw.

At the corresponding landing, there was no screw. The light buzzed twice anyway.

Halen stopped using words like corresponding.

For the next twelve minutes she worked the way she had been trained to work when a witness's account failed: isolate, mark, repeat. She numbered doors in blue. She measured halls by paces and with a laser. She left cedar in jambs, scored hinges with an awl, and tested every apparent return against the last certain point.

Every procedure held true on its own surface and left her with more surfaces.

Door 6 opened onto a landing she had labeled 6 already, except the older numeral under her chalk was 4. A hall measured twenty-eight paces east and, when approached again, thirty-one west. Two blue arrows pointed opposite ways without either having moved. One cedar wedge remained beneath a door while an unmarked version of that door stood beside it, both opening onto stairs with the same loose third tread. Her jacket crackled whenever she raised an arm. Lint climbed the black screen of her phone in pale hairs.

At 3:44, Joss called.

Halen answered before the first ring finished. “Do not move.”

“You always make hello sound so welcoming.” His voice was thin, but the joke was deliberate. “I'm still in the fourth-floor hall.”

Halen stood beneath a painted 6. “Hit the wall.”

“What?”

“Not a pipe. The wall beside you. Three-two.”

The knocks came through the phone. A moment later, much fainter, they sounded inside the plaster at Halen's shoulder.

She pressed her palm to it. “Again.”

This time the wall carried five little impacts into her bones. Close. On the far side of perhaps two layers of lath, Joss struck what he called a fourth-floor wall while she stood on six.

Halen looked down the hall. Its nearest doorway was 6C. “Which flat?”

“Four D, or what ought to be. Mum's old kitchen is through one door. The tin's outside it.”

“You said you had it.”

“I did.”

“Joss.”

“I picked it up. The stairs brought me back to it. I put it down to pry a fire door, and then it was ahead of me again.”

“How many times did you go back?”

Silence collected around the faint line noise.

“How many?”

“Three.”

Halen shut her eyes. “Why?”

“Because it was right there.” His hammer scraped the wall. “And because you put a disposal sticker on everything that made a noise.”

“I put it on a box of unmarked tapes.”

“You called them junk.”

“I called them unreviewed.”

“You wrote JUNK on the sticker, Halen.”

She had. It was faster than writing *things I cannot listen to while clearing a room that still smells like her hand cream.* The accusation had nothing to do with stairs. Halen wanted to keep it that way.

“Do you have the tin now?” she asked.

“No. I have one tape in my pocket.”

“Good. Keep hitting the wall.”

“Don't come round the hall toward me.”

“I'm not leaving you.”

“That's not what I said.”

Halen rubbed chalk dust between her finger and thumb. Joss had not asked her to leave. He had asked her to stop turning his directions into her own. She looked at her arrows, numbers, measured distances. None lied. Each belonged to the surface on which she had put it. She had been treating them as a single account because she needed the building to remain one object.

She ended the call to save his battery and drove the awl into the plaster beside her. White dust spilled onto her boot. The dent was fresh, unique. She walked three paces to the next partition, keeping the previous dent in sight, and drilled a narrow hole.

The bit broke through into darkness. No duplicate hole appeared. Nothing changed behind her until she turned back toward the numbered fire door. Then the hall gave her that empty, full-body recognition: the torn wallpaper, the dead bulb, the tiny white hill of drill dust. Known before seen.

Too late.

Not because the sensation caused anything. The warning came second. By the time recognition reached her, whatever counted as crossing had already happened, and the entrance to that familiar route had ceased to lead where it had led.

Near the floor, hidden where a removed baseboard had been, someone had scratched smaller words into the plaster. Most were lost under torn paper. Halen crouched and made out:

**DON'T TAKE THE WAY YOU KNOW**

Below it, one unfinished gouge began with a W.

She put away the chalk.

Halen called Joss again. “Find a pipe. Hit it once every five seconds. Don't follow it anywhere. Don't even turn around if you can help it.”

“You have a plan?”

“I have a direction.”

“That bad, then.”

She pressed her ear to the wall. A dull ring traveled through it, weaker near the door and stronger toward the stripped kitchen of 6C. She entered the flat once. No return to check the threshold. Cabinets hung open. Black lint trembled on the vinyl floor as static lifted it toward her boots.

At the kitchen's far wall, the pipe struck clearly enough to feel with two fingers. She drilled beside it. Brick dust came back red. Bearing wall. No passage.

To the right, the pantry partition sounded hollow. She cut a hand-sized square with her oscillating saw, reached through, and found the back of a closet. Plaster rasped her sleeve as she widened the hole and climbed through. The phone in her breast pocket popped with static. She did not look behind her.

Closet, bathroom, an old service cupboard. Each existed beside the next even when her survey said they should have overlapped. Joss's blows strengthened, then faded, then returned through a wall at knee height. Halen followed vibration, not plan. She made every opening differently so none could pass for a remembered route: round drill hole, low rectangle, broken lath peeled upward. The work was ugly. Clean edges would have required backing out and approaching again.

Once she opened a cupboard and saw the same three-sink laundry below through a drop of impossible depth. Its painted windows were tiny black squares six floors down. She shut the cupboard without stepping through it and cut sideways through the wall.

Her drill battery showed one bar when Joss's pipe blows became loud enough to shake grit from the partition. Halen bored a thumb-wide hole at eye level.

Light stabbed through.

An eye appeared, brown and furious, less than an arm's length away.

“Could've warned me,” Joss said.

Halen laughed once. It came out closer to a cough. “Move left.”

“Your left or a normal person's?”

She put the saw to the wall. “Move.”

He did. The partition was only lath and lime plaster between two service cavities that no plan placed together. Halen cut downward until the blade stalled. From the other side Joss drove his pry bar through, levering strips loose. A screw jumped from the floor and clung to the synthetic cuff of his coat. He stared at it.

“Your building likes me,” he said.

“My building?”

“You've been numbering it.”

They tore an opening wide enough to shoulder through. Joss was gray with dust. A shallow cut marked his temple, already dry, and his right hand shook around the bar. Halen caught his sleeve, checked his pupils with the flashlight, then made herself release him before the examination became an argument.

“I am fine enough,” he said.

“Fine enough isn't a measurement.”

“Tonight it is.”

Behind him, through the ripped wall of a stripped apartment, an open doorway framed a length of yellow-papered corridor. At the far end sat a red cassette tin.

Joss saw it when Halen did. His face changed, not dramatically. His mouth pressed flat. That was worse.

The route to it was straight and familiar. Halen knew the lift in the floorboard three steps from the tin. She knew the stain shaped like a mitten, the dead light, the kitchen doorway beyond. Recognition gathered in her before she had moved a foot.

“Get into the chase,” she said. “Now.”

Joss did not move.

Halen reached for his arm, then stopped with her hand open between them. Orders were how she had crossed the first six floors, and then several more. One was forming now.

“Joss,” she said.

He looked at her, suspicious of the sentence in her mouth.

“I'm not going after it,” she added. “I won't help you go after it. But I labelled it without listening. Your choice.”

The cassette in his coat pocket made a small plastic click when he touched it. “This one's labelled Tuesday slash radio.”

“Sounds gripping.”

“Probably forty minutes of weather and Mum singing over adverts.”

The tin waited in its pool of emergency light.

Joss stepped backward into the new breach.

Halen followed him.

The service cavity ran beside a soil pipe through darkness tight enough to turn their shoulders. Joss went first because he could see where his version of the cavity continued. Halen's tape measure told her the exterior wall should be fourteen inches to their right, then nine feet, then inside her own rib cage. She clipped it shut.

They descended without reversing: a rusted maintenance ladder, the top of an old laundry duct, a shelf of brick where the cavity pinched closed. At each obstruction they chose once. Joss lowered himself to the duct; Halen passed the tool bag; he caught it and did not offer it back. Loose screws bristled from his sleeve like iron filings. When one blue spark snapped between their phones, they both swore, and for half a flight the fear loosened enough to let them breathe.

At the bottom, a corroded grille sealed the duct from a coal passage. Two bolts turned. The third spun without loosening.

“Go back for the saw?” Joss asked.

Halen looked up at the ladder. She knew the shelf above, the pipe bend, the exact place her knee had struck brick.

“No.”

They bent the grille. Joss drove the pry bar under its bottom edge while Halen leaned on the top corner. Metal shrieked. Rust sifted into their hair. The grille bowed enough for Joss to crawl through, then tried to spring flat against Halen's shoulder. He braced it from the far side and she slid under, dragging the bag by its strap.

The coal passage ended at a square wooden hatch. Cool air breathed around its frame. Halen put her ear to it and heard a compressor cycling, steady and ordinary, in the neighboring building's cellar.

She lifted the latch.

The hatch opened on the first try.

They crawled into a room of plastic-wrapped boilers and paint cans stacked for salvage. The walls met at right angles. A bare bulb hummed without buzzing twice. When Joss dropped his pry bar, it hit the concrete and stayed there.

Halen sat with her back against a boiler and phoned the emergency number on the demolition notice. She reported an unsafe interior, possible people inside, unknown structural voids, and gave her contractor identification. The dispatcher made her repeat *possible people*.

“I don't know if the person who wrote the warning got out,” Halen said. “So yes. Possible.”

She could delay the morning work. She could not give anyone a route. Already, the hatch behind them felt known beneath her hand.

Joss stood before she finished the call. The cellar's stair door had a common five-lever lock swollen crooked in its frame. He watched her kneel to it.

“Can you open that one again?” he asked.

Halen slid in a pick. “The lock, yes.”

The levers rose. The bolt withdrew, but the door held fast against the dragging floor. She put away her tools. Joss set his shoulder under the latch while Halen wedged her fingers beneath the bottom edge and lifted.

On the other side, an ordinary stair climbed toward the street.
