# Opus review: A Lock on the Inside

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the voices are distinct and the misreadings work, but four key exchanges fail Ground or staging (the lock reveal, the injury, the torn voice, the closing repeat).
- Prose: PASS — a controlled first-person voice that keeps accounts; only small staging gaps.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is fully delivered and callwright canon is respected; only minor object and wording slips.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, Dialogue: PASS; partly disagree: the voice work deserves credit, but the hinge exchanges do not hold up line by line.

The dialogue here is stronger than the collection's usual pattern. The split
medium does real work: the apprentice speaks too formally and too much, while
Kethra answers in chalk, signs, and drawings. It produces misreadings that move
the relationship ("Workroom?" / *Food.*, "Lesson ended", "I did not accuse
him."), and the two voices can be told apart without tags. The failures sit at
the hinges. Kethra's slate names the lock before he has said anything about a
door. A tangle of referents leaves it unclear whether the old master injured
him. The closing repeat is glossed with a contrast the text doesn't contain.
And once his voice has torn, he goes on speaking in fluent paragraphs while
Kethra, who has been watching his throat all day, lets it pass. The edit pass
should make each hinge follow from the words actually spoken and let the injury
bend his speech. It should keep his petitioner's formality and Kethra's dry,
visual humour.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: the old master threw him out (keep "He threw me out when my voice made the exercises unprofitable"). He convinced another archmage (the petition, three mornings, and the trial week). Kethra is kinder and far more lenient. Her horror when he describes the old teaching (L225–L247) must stay unmistakable. Length is 2,500–4,000 words and the prose is about 2,735 now, so trimming speeches must be offset (D1). Keep it broadly accessible, with the abuse reported and non-graphic.
- POV and form: first-person past from the unnamed apprentice, who shares his euphemisms and calculations. Kethra stays external and is seen only through sign, slate, drawing, expression, and action. Keep the story to one day.
- Kethra (LOCKED, `universe/characters.md`) is mute but hears. She talks through sign, slate, expression, and diagrams, never speaks aloud, and is a newly qualified archmage who holds an office seal. Do not give her a voice, and do not let writing or signing stand in for a call.
- Callwright activation (LOCKED, `universe/rules.md`): shaper and caller touch the same brass node, the shaper marks the spell, and the caller signs yes. Kethra's two-finger ready sign opens a twelve-heartbeat window, and the full registered name must come on one breath. A broken syllable releases nothing but still tires the shaper. Stop ends the cell at once with no penalty. Callers may set call limits. The story fits all of this now; keep "Practice Wick, Take Light" and the one-call limit.
- The old master stays offstage and unnamed. Do not tie his practice to the college's archmage office.
- The bolt: keyhole on the corridor side, bolt on the inside, no outer bar. The screws are newer than the door. The story opens and closes on it.
- Keep what already works (see Preserve): the account motif, the legible misreadings, Kethra erasing her own first sentence, the one-call / one-night echo, and the small humour of the pickles.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Torn voice keeps delivering fluent paragraphs

- Where: L137–L243, from the failed call through the packing and disclosure scene (compare L295)
- Text:
> My voice tore down the middle of the last word.
> "Again," I said. Each part of the word scraped.
> She touched her throat and gave me a questioning look.
> Despite myself, I made a sound that would have been a laugh if my throat had permitted it. It hurt.
- Problem: Uniform fluency under physical pressure, plus a staging lapse. Right after the tear, a single word "scraped". Yet he goes on to speak about 350 more words, including five speeches of 33–52 words (L165, L207, L227, L231, L243). There is no rasp, pause, lost word, or noted cost, from a narrator who counts every other cost. Kethra checked his throat twice before the call (L63, L121) and stops a laugh with wait and water at L295, but she lets all of it pass. The disclosure reads as calm testimony from someone who isn't hurt, and Kethra's care seems to switch off for the scene that matters most.
- Fix: Let the injury bend his speech at two or three points: a word that won't come, a drop to a whisper, a pause to swallow before he goes on anyway. Give Kethra one reaction in the packing scene, such as the wait sign, the water cup, or the chalk pushed toward him. He can refuse or ignore it, which fits him. Cut each long speech down to its one essential detail: nine and twelve days; locked in through supper to breakfast; the bound fingers; thrown out when he stopped being profitable. Keep his formality and lose the length. Spend the saved words on the voice beats so the story stays above 2,500 words.

### D2 · Major · Kethra names the lock before he does

- Where: L207–L223, packing scene, the "Put out where?" run
- Text:
> This time I was put out before I could finish recovering
> *Put out where?*
> "Of the practice room. Of the house, afterward."
> *Practice room was locked?*
- Problem: Ground/uptake at the prompt's key beat. All Kethra hears is that he was sent out of the practice room and then out of the house. Nothing in that suggests he was shut in; "put out of" a room is the opposite picture. Her next slate line brings in the lock, the fact the title rests on, with nothing to prompt it, so the reveal feels steered by the author instead of drawn out of his answer. His petition (skipped meals, sleeping in a workroom) could explain her suspicion, but nothing on the page at this moment makes that link. "Put out where?" also asks for a place, while his answer says what he was put out of.
- Fix: Let him mention the door himself, offhand, as a harmless detail in his report voice, so her question follows from a word she actually heard. Keep *Locked from which side?* / "The corridor side." exactly as written. Illustrative only: "Of the practice room, once he unlocked it. Of the house, afterward." Her slate can then go straight to which side, or keep a short *Locked?* first.

### D3 · Major · The reader can't tell whether the old master hurt him

- Where: L231–L235, disclosure, the bound fingers
- Text:
> I worked three weeks with two fingers bound and did not spoil another holding line.
> *He did this? Your master?*
> "He corrected it. Mostly. My hand is sound now."
- Problem: Ground/referent at the peak of the horror. "This" could mean the injury, the forced work, or the whole account. "It" could be the hand or the spoiled line. "Corrected" could mean punished or treated, and "Mostly" could go with either. Read plainly ("He set it right, mostly; my hand is fine now"), the answer says the master treated the injury. That is the opposite of what the scene's horror and Kethra's fury at L237 need. Whether the old teacher physically hurt him is central to "how your old teacher treated you", and the reader cannot settle it.
- Fix: Decide the fact, then make both lines carry it. A staging beat helps: Kethra points at his hand before she writes *He did this?* Keep "correction" as his euphemism but make it clear who did it, and drop "Mostly" unless it means something specific. Illustrative only: "It was a correction. The fingers healed straight."

### D4 · Major · The closing repeat claims a change that isn't there

- Where: L279–L285, the last exchange before the pickle drawing
- Text:
> "No more magic today," I said.
> Her eyebrows rose, asking rather than praising.
> "No more magic today," I repeated. This time I did not add a condition.
- Problem: Ground failure in the final meaningful exchange: the narration misdescribes what happened. The first line has no condition, so "this time" has nothing to contrast with. Kethra's raised brows have nothing to question, and the beat meant to show his first unconditional refusal reads as a puzzle. A reader might stretch "this time" to cover the conditions he added all day, but with two identical lines side by side, the contrast points back at the first one.
- Fix: Put his habitual condition into the first version, so her brows question it and the repeat visibly drops it. Keep the second line and its narration as written. Illustrative only: "No more magic today, unless you require it."

### D5 · Minor · "Callers fail cells." turns the packing argument into a debate

- Where: L177–L189, packing scene
- Text:
> *Cells fail.*
> "Callers fail cells."
> "Not after you had begun spending strength."
- Problem: The mirrored "Cells fail" / "Callers fail cells" line is the story's one clear last-word comeback. It sets off seven alternating turns of similar size in which a frightened apprentice rebuts an archmage point by point. The logic holds, but the rhythm is correction ping-pong. A neat saying is also not how this narrator answers anywhere else: he apologises, reports, or offers work.
- Fix: If this is his old master's saying, mark it as one. That adds real information and matches L27's "My former master said". Illustrative only: "He said callers fail cells." Otherwise, make it a plain admission. Break the strict alternation once, with a beat, or with a pause to swallow per D1.

### D6 · Minor · The zero drawing can be read backwards

- Where: L67–L77, workroom, call limit
- Text:
> drew an enormous zero with a throat underneath it. Then she crossed the zero out so hard the chalk squeaked.
- Problem: Ground/referent. A zero over a throat most naturally reads "limit: zero", meaning no calls at all. That is the opposite of the "no limit" she is rejecting, and crossing it out then reads "not zero". The narrator interprets her other gestures but not this one, and his reply repeats his answer instead of showing what he took the drawing to mean. Her meaning only becomes clear when she writes *One*.
- Fix: Make the drawing say "unlimited", for example a tally running off the edge of the slate above a throat, then struck through. Or let him note his own literal reading in a clause.

### D7 · Minor · He explains the early yes the reader already saw

- Where: L231, disclosure
- Text:
> That is why I agreed quickly today. I thought you valued readiness.
- Problem: A mild version of the Philosopher. L89–L105 already showed the automatic yes ("But I knew I would agree."), and the first sentence explains it again. The second sentence carries his mistaken logic by itself.
- Fix: Cut "That is why I agreed quickly today." and keep "I thought you valued readiness."

### D8 · Minor · Sign rendering and comprehension slips

- Where: L29 and L41 (signs in quotation marks), L237 (fast signing)
- Text:
> signed, slowly for me, "Enough."
> Her signs came so fast that I lost even the nouns. *Master* flashed once.
- Problem: Signed words are in quotation marks at L29 and L41 but in italics at L95 and L237, and the slate text is italic too. Quotation marks around a mute character's words read for a moment as speech. At L237 he says he lost "even the nouns", then reports catching two of them (*Master*, and something about a door).
- Fix: Put signed words in italics everywhere, as at L95 and L237; the "signed" and "wrote" tags already tell sign from slate. Change "even the nouns" to "most of the nouns" or something similar.

### D9 · Minor · Tags and stock beats at Kethra's reactions

- Where: L73, L175, L199, L225, L227
- Text:
> "I have no set limit," I amended.
> "It was not a punishment every time," I said quickly.
> Her nostrils flared.
> All the color left Kethra's face.
- Problem: There is a said-bookism ("amended") and an adverb tag doing the line's work ("said quickly"; the defensive rush already shows it). The anger and horror moments use stock beats ("nostrils flared", "All the color left"), and L175 labels the emotion ("Plain annoyance crossed her face"). Each is small. Together they give Kethra generic reactions in the very scene where her specific physical responses (the rattled basin, the broken chalk, the dropped slate) are otherwise strong.
- Fix: Use "said" or no tag. Replace the two stock beats with something as specific as the basin or the chalk, or cut them and let the next action show the reaction.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Three small staging gaps

- Where: L49–L51 (kitchen to stair), L87–L89 (the node), L275 (the wax)
- Text:
> Afterward she wrote, *Lesson at second bell. Until then: room, walk, sleep. Do not polish the stair rail.*
> She was still drawing the last of this on the slate when the node warmed beneath my fingertips.
> then pressed the seal into a pool of blue wax.
- Problem: The stair-rail joke assumes they are at the stair and that he has already touched the rail. But the scene was last placed in the kitchen, and the touch is only reported afterward. At the node, the reader never sees him put his fingers on it, so "beneath my fingertips" comes as a surprise instead of showing his eagerness. At L275, a pool of melted wax appears while Kethra sits in a doorway with no candle or heat.
- Fix: One clause each. Put them at the foot of the stair before the note. Show him laying his fingers on the node while she is still writing. Give the wax a source, or have the seal stamped without wax.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Small object and wording slips

- Where: L27 and L55 (what he proposed), L29 (Kethra's hearing), L245–L249 (the slate)
- Text:
> sleep in a workroom if the college has no proper bed to spare
> The room was larger than the cupboard I had proposed
> Kethra read my mouth. She was mute, not deaf
> The slate slipped from Kethra's hand and struck the floor flat.
> She set the slate on the floor between us
- Problem: He offers to sleep in a workroom, then remembers proposing a cupboard. "Read my mouth" means lip-reading, but Kethra hears (LOCKED `characters.md`, Kethra), and the next sentence says so, so the pair reads as a contradiction. The slate falls at L245, she backs into the passage, and at L249 she sets it down without ever picking it up.
- Fix: Make the two proposals match. Change "read my mouth" to watching him or listening. Add a pickup beat, for example she picks up the slate before or after she steps back.
