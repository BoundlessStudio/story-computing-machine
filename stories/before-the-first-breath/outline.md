# Provisional outline

The explicit promise is the first humans waking together without memories of their creation, surrounded by the lake, green valleys, mountains, sunsets, paths, ridge monuments, and enormous submerged remains of an older civilization. I infer an expansive, uncanny origin mystery: the pleasure of discovering that this apparently virgin paradise already has a history, and that genuinely first humans need not be the world's first people. The supplied image suggests luminous beauty and immense landscape rather than a ruined wasteland. No central relationship, explanation, tone beyond that contrast, or particular ending is specified. Awe, unease, adventure, and a moving discovery are my proposed treatment, not additional requirements.

Working title: **The World Before Us**. Close third through Neral, one of twenty-nine adults who wake together beneath the island's tree. They possess language and bodily skills, but no personal past. Her companion Orr is quick to try things; she wants to understand them. Their developing friendship supports the expedition; its resolution is not the main payoff.

- On the island, a dropped pebble passes into the perfectly still lake without making a ripple. Neral sees an immense avenue below: its steps and openings fit bodies nothing like theirs. A pale line beneath the water leads to a shore path. They follow it because the world has provided a route before anyone could have walked it.
- Beyond the shore, the path alternates walkable stretches with six-lobed footholds across sheer rock. The ridge monuments are worn figures, not featureless stones: many-limbed, broad-bodied beings looking toward the lake. A broken figure reveals deliberate construction beneath centuries of roots. Their first hypothesis, that other humans preceded them, becomes untenable when they find repeated impressions of the builders' bodies and tools. The paradise and the ruins belong to the same real landscape.
- Sunset light enters a monument's aperture and exposes a route down the cliff into the submerged structures. Neral and Orr take the dry passage. Its rooms and suspended crossings were made for climbing bodies; the unfamiliar scale makes the exploration dangerous. They must adapt the route together rather than acquire miraculous competence. Below the lake, they find a sequence of physical models and preserved images: the builders' flourishing world, its long decline, and repeated attempts to make a new kind of person. Human forms appear only at the end. The first human bodies were grown and kept unconscious in a nursery beneath the island while the emptied world became habitable. They have no forgotten human lives to recover.
- The images are records, not a living oracle. They cannot give the builders every name or explain their own beginnings. But Neral can establish that the six-limbed figures were people, that humans were their deliberate final work, and that the apparent paradise grew over the civilization which made them. She and Orr carry out the small image-bearing prism which completes the ridge monument's otherwise broken sequence. Recovering it requires crossing the last suspended span after Orr's attempted shortcut breaks it; Neral's reading of the six-handed footholds supplies their way out.
- They return to the others and bring them onto the ridge. At the next sunset, the restored arrangement casts the recorded figures at their original enormous scale across the valley: builders at work, playing, making the first human bodies, and finally an empty city under growing trees. The new people can see their creation without falsely receiving its memories. Neral's consequential choice is to bring everyone to this disquieting history instead of accepting the island as a complete, unexplained beginning. The ending keeps the immense landscape in view: the humans follow an old path into their first night beneath the fading images of a people who will never meet them. They are first, but their story begins inside someone else's ending.

The actual dramatic center is discovery of humanity's place in an older inhabited world. Its main risks are turning the submerged city into an explanatory museum, making a dangerous crossing supplant the mystery, and letting a lyrical memorial substitute for a substantial revelation. The builders' different bodies must shape the route and the evidence; the final images must reframe the whole paradise, not merely confirm that ruins exist. The exact catastrophe and the builders' earlier origins can remain unknown.
