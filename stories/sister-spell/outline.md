# Until I'm Big

Provisional direction, 2026-10-11. First-person younger sibling; a close, domestic fairy tale with ordinary sibling comedy under increasing pressure. General is the starting audience target.

The narrator, ten, has just made a falling cup stay in the air by closing a hand and telling it to stay. Their parents react with strained reassurance, whispered arguments, and a sudden interest in everything the child says. Only their eighteen-year-old sister, Enna, looks delighted. She keeps smiling and breaking into little private chuckles. The narrator thinks she is laughing at their small, unimpressive magic and wants to impress her.

Enna was due to leave at the start of summer for a paid apprenticeship making theatrical scenery. For one season she has been unable to cross the garden gate. A visiting practitioner recognized a living person's holding spell, but could neither identify its caster nor undo someone else's hold. The adults took Enna's departure itself as evidence that she had unconsciously held herself, and kept asking her to admit she was not ready to go. The narrator knew about the failed departures and believed this account. Enna, despite wanting the apprenticeship, had begun to wonder if the adults were right about her. The cup is counterevidence: there is another caster in the household. It does not prove who cast the original hold by itself.

The story spans the day after the cup and the following morning, when Enna has a scheduled journey for a last chance at the apprenticeship. The narrator actively seeks her audience, investigates the strange smile, and insists on showing the new magic at the gate. When the narrator repeats the fist and “stay,” Enna remembers the child clutching her sleeve on the first attempted departure and saying, “Stay till I'm big.” Her private amusement is relief and vindication: it might never have been her own cowardice, and she might finally leave. She tells the narrator herself, including that she had started believing the adults. She is affectionate, impatient, and occasionally unfair; her packed bag and scenery work show a life she has kept trying to reach. The narrator is guilty, defensive, and angry at being presented with an accusation. Enna will not promise that the lost summer was secretly good. One parent specifically admits they should have listened to what she said she wanted.

Keep the magic narrow and physical: the narrator can hold a thing or person in place with a closed hand and a spoken command, and can consciously release that specific hold with an open hand and a spoken release. Merely opening a fist during ordinary life does not revoke a set spell. The suspended cup gives a small, safe instance of both actions. No adult in this household has demonstrated this faculty; the parents' belief that it had appeared in Enna is their own unsupported interpretation. The practitioner cannot revoke someone else's set hold. Release is an action, not a test of moral purity; sadness, anger, and a wish that Enna would choose to remain do not prevent it. Do not add physical growth or a wider command power.

At the gate the narrator releases the old hold successfully, confirming the shared inference in action. Enna crosses. The narrator panics, says “Wait!” and closes a fist; Enna is visibly stopped by a new hold. She points out the act, loses the private smile, and insists on release. The narrator revokes it immediately. Enna chooses to come inside again because her travel is scheduled for the following morning. The new pressure is that the narrator now knows how to hold her on purpose, and has seen how easily a familiar sibling demand can become coercion. At the actual departure, they must choose whether to cast again.

The middle turn belongs to the narrator. Their shared ritual is a cardboard theatre: Enna makes the scenery while the narrator supplies the absurd voices, and she laughs at the same bad joke each night. The child wants one more ordinary theatre evening, but Enna has packing and preparations to do. Trying to put on the show anyway means supplying both jobs and confronting the empty audience. The child then chooses to finish an ordinary little landscape for Enna to take. Scissors, paint, and glue do the work. It is a gift for the life she wants, without a return condition. The activity grows out of the child's frustrated desire for her attention, rather than being assigned as a lesson or announced as practice for separation.

Possible ending: on the morning of departure the narrator gives Enna the landscape and walks with her to the gate. They exchange the old bad theatre joke on opposite sides; her answering laugh is audible and shared, and the narrator laughs too. She carries her own bag and leaves for the apprenticeship. The narrator wants to call her back, remains sad, and casts no replacement hold. End with the difficult ordinary act of letting the gate swing shut, rather than declaring the child grown or explaining what growing up means.

The readers should challenge the delayed explanation, whether the local holding/release mechanism feels earned rather than designed to moralize, the sister's independent life, and whether the ending offers a consequential choice rather than making guilt do all the work.
