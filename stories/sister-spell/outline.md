# Until I'm Big

Provisional direction, 2026-10-11. First-person younger sibling; a close, domestic fairy tale with ordinary sibling comedy under increasing pressure. General is the starting audience target.

The narrator, ten, has just made a falling cup stay in the air by closing a hand and telling it to stay. Their parents and grandmother react with strained reassurance, whispered arguments, and a sudden interest in everything the child says. Only their eighteen-year-old sister, Enna, looks delighted. She keeps smiling and breaking into little private chuckles. The narrator thinks she is laughing at their small, unimpressive magic and wants a proper spell to impress her.

Enna was due to leave at the start of summer for a paid apprenticeship making theatrical scenery. For one season she has been unable to cross the garden gate. The family originally mistook her stopping for fright; a visiting practitioner eventually recognized a holding spell, but could not undo another living caster's hold. Enna remembers the narrator clutching her sleeve on the first attempted departure and saying, “Stay till I'm big.” Nobody had seen the child perform magic, so this remained an alarming suspicion until the cup. The adults delayed naming the suspicion to the child while looking for another cause and remedy; after confirmation, they want to prepare a careful explanation and consult the practitioner again. Their delay has cost Enna part of her life and has left the child unequipped to understand their own power. These are failures the story will allow them to own, rather than neutral precautions.

Enna refuses another postponement. She takes the narrator to the gate and tells the truth herself. Her private amusement has been relief: the impossible cup makes her confinement real to everyone, and might finally let her leave. It also includes the absurdity of adults calling the caster “too little” to be told about the spell that keeps her here. She is affectionate, impatient, and occasionally unfair; she has already arranged one last chance to take up the apprenticeship and has packed. The narrator is hurt, guilty, then angry that everybody thought this about them without asking. Enna will not promise that the lost summer was secretly good or that she never wanted to go.

Keep the magic narrow: the narrator can hold a thing or person in a condition with a closed hand and a spoken wish, and can consciously release their own hold. Opening the hand alone is not enough after a hold has been set; release means recalling and relinquishing that specific wish. The suspended cup gives a small, safe instance of both actions. No adult in this household has this faculty, and the practitioner cannot supply the narrator's relinquishment. Do not add physical growth or a wider command power merely to solve the ending.

The narrator tries to release Enna while privately bargaining that she will stay until after a particular occasion, or return the very next day. At the gate the old hold remains. This failure should expose the continued wish through the narrator's behavior, rather than declare that a child must feel no sadness or ambivalence. Enna loses her patient smile and says something she cannot make kind: she is going to leave; she would like to do it without having to stop loving her sibling. The narrator has to decide whether to use the newly discovered power to keep the person they love close, or accept a departure they still dislike. The practical cost is real: the sibling will not be here for the small daily rituals on which the narrator relied.

Possible ending: on the morning of Enna's departure, the narrator opens their hand at the gate and names the original wish before releasing it. Enna crosses carrying her own bag. They exchange one small, unsentimental joke on opposite sides of the gate; her answering laugh is now audible and shared. She does not surrender the apprenticeship or promise an immediate return. The narrator makes no replacement spell. End with the difficult ordinary act of letting the gate swing shut, rather than explaining what growing up means.

The readers should challenge the delayed explanation, whether the local holding/release mechanism feels earned rather than designed to moralize, the sister's independent life, and whether the ending offers a consequential choice rather than making guilt do all the work.
