# The Weight of Here

Provisional direction, 2026-10-03. A dry, affectionate first-person account by a monster mother, told through three visits by the same human: a baby, an inconvenient young friend of the brood, and a grown woman preparing to leave the valley. The mother wants all her children alive and within reach. She knows each unnamed child by smell, habits, and the sound of its six feet; their lack of names is no lack of personhood or love.

In this particular valley, a name makes a lasting channel for living magic. The monsters spend their limited naming strength on precise, useful workings. A named object can hold or focus its caster's power but supplies no magic of its own. The mother has named the stone over her den's entrance **Here**: while she sustains it, the stone and the roof it supports keep their places. She chose a narrow entrance against the hunters who once came through this valley. The fixed shape preserves that old safety even as the children grow. The mother can release her working, but the settling roof will then fall; she cannot rename it into a larger entrance without first unloading it.

The first visit occurs when a human neighbour brings her newborn daughter. The mother is appalled that the neighbour has spent a naming on someone unable to do anything. **Osa** describes no useful property. The neighbour chose the sound because she wanted to be able to call her daughter. The scene establishes mutual dependence: the human brings cloth and food the mother likes, and the mother knows which plants will keep the human's sheep well. No one has a comprehensive theory of personal naming.

On the second visit, Osa is old enough to be the walking child's particular friend. This child is the boldest of the brood and has begun growing antlers. Osa comes back from an ordinary excursion with a small impossibility: she took a shortcut through an unbroken stone wall because she was late, cannot repeat it on command, and is more troubled about a torn boot. The mother hunts the name's etymology for a command she might have missed. Human names given before a child's capacities exist can take shape with the living bearer; their unexplained feats accumulate as they grow. The mother instead trusts workings with an exact, already known use. Osa and the walking child make plans to go beyond the valley one day. The mother hears those plans but regards them as children's exaggerations.

For the third visit, adult Osa has accepted work with a travelling players' company and is leaving. She promised the walking child a place beside her on the road, and has come to honour it. The friend has outgrown the named doorway. The mother has been saying that its antlers will settle and that leaving can wait until next season. An attempt to squeeze out catches the antlers in the entrance and cracks an ordinary supporting stone. The mother's living effort keeps **Here** fixed while the den's weight bears down behind it; the brood must leave now, and the trapped child cannot. This crisis comes from the protection she has preserved and the future she has postponed, not a chance landslide.

Osa gets under the roof with her friend. She cannot simply pass through solid stone with someone else, and there is no semantic loophole in the working. She puts her human shoulders against the mountain's weight and asks the mother to stop holding it. The mother must trust a person she still thinks she understands and permit her to make the safety spell useless. Osa takes the weight, visibly and with difficulty, long enough for the mother to break the narrow entrance apart and get every child out. It is a physical impossibility performed by the once-useless named baby, through grown capacity and deliberate effort, not through the literal meaning of her name. This capacity may save the brood; it does not decide what Osa owes them or make her invulnerable.

The mother does not announce a theory or name her offspring. The rescued friend still wants to leave with Osa; rescue has not settled that disagreement. The mother makes room for departure, then asks Osa to come back on a visit when there is nothing that needs lifting. A possible last image is the mother learning to distinguish her child's receding six-footed tread from the sound of someone lost. The emotional movement is from preserving a child in an exact safe place to hearing that child remain itself while going elsewhere. The prose may find a quieter or less resolved ending.

Protect the mother's strange, practical tenderness and Osa's independent future. Challenge whether the slow growth, doorway accident, and immense feat feel earned; whether three visits provide enough attachment; and whether the name connection is legible without turning a human life into a stored-power explanation. The magic mechanism and precise ending remain open to discussion.
