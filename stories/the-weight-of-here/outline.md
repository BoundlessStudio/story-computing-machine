# The Weight of Here

Provisional direction, 2026-10-03. A dry, affectionate first-person account by a monster mother, told through three visits by the same human: a baby, an inconvenient young friend of the brood, and a grown woman preparing to leave the valley. The mother wants all her children alive and within reach. She knows each unnamed child by smell, habits, and the sound of its six feet; their lack of names is no lack of personhood or love.

In this particular valley, a name makes a lasting channel for living magic. The monsters spend their limited naming strength on precise, useful workings. A named object can hold or focus its caster's power but supplies no magic of its own. The mother has named the lintel over her den's low entrance **Here**: while she sustains it, the stone and the roof it supports keep their places. She chose a narrow entrance against the hunters who once came through this valley. The fixed shape preserves that old safety even as the children grow. Ordinary use will establish that a working must be relinquished before a different name can reshape it. The mother cannot rename the loaded entrance into a wider one without leaving its roof unsupported during that interval.

The first visit occurs when a human neighbour brings her newborn daughter. The mother is appalled that the neighbour has spent a naming on someone unable to do anything. **Osa** describes no useful property. The neighbour chose the sound because she wanted to be able to call her daughter. The scene establishes mutual dependence: the human brings cloth and food the mother likes, and the mother knows which plants will keep the human's sheep well. No one has a comprehensive theory of personal naming.

On the second visit, Osa is old enough to be the walking child's particular friend. This child is the boldest of the brood and has begun growing antlers. Osa comes back from an ordinary excursion with a small impossibility: she took a shortcut through an unbroken stone wall because she was late, cannot repeat it on command, and is more troubled about a torn boot. The mother hunts the name's etymology for a command she might have missed. An author-only working hypothesis is that a personal name bestowed before any capacity exists can take shape with the living bearer, unlike a name prescribing an already known use. The narrator will not reach or state this theory; she remains bewildered. Osa and the walking child make plans to go beyond the valley one day. The mother hears those plans but regards them as children's exaggerations. Protect Osa's developing judgment and limits rather than displaying a catalogue of powers.

For the third visit, adult Osa has accepted work with a travelling players' company and is leaving. She promised the walking child a place beside her on the road, and has come to honour it. The friend has outgrown the named doorway. The mother has been saying that its antlers will settle and that leaving can wait until next season. Osa is already at the den when the friend attempts to leave: the company will not wait for another season. The antlers jam high in the entrance's central throat, and the attempt dislodges an already stressed ordinary jamb beneath one end of the lintel. A low gap remains beside and beneath the trapped child, large enough for the small siblings and a crouching human, too small for the mother. The mother is inside, keeping **Here** fixed through her living effort as the roof's load bears down behind it. With the ordinary support displaced, the entire load now draws on her. She tries to free a limb to break the jamb, but the lintel dips; holding it takes all her effort. Osa's contribution is necessary because the mother cannot sustain this damaged entrance and dismantle it at the same time. This crisis comes from the protection she has preserved and the future she has postponed, not a chance landslide.

Osa crouches through the side gap and gets the smaller siblings outside before taking the load. The frightened trapped child directs one sibling through and agrees to the dangerous plan; it is a participant without becoming eloquently heroic. Osa cannot take someone else through solid stone, and cannot repeat her childhood shortcut to order. She puts her human shoulders against the lintel and asks the mother to try yielding its weight to her. She has no certainty about what she can bear or how long she can hold it; she discovers that limit under the increasing load. The mother must trust a person she still thinks she understands and permit her to make the safety spell useless. Osa takes the mountain's weight, visibly and with difficulty, while the mother releases **Here** and breaks the side jamb away to free the friend and enlarge the way out. The mother and friend can then leave through the opened entrance; Osa ducks out last and the unloaded stones fall behind her. It is a physical impossibility performed by the once-useless named baby, through grown capacity and deliberate effort, not through the literal meaning of her name. This capacity may save the brood; it does not decide what Osa owes them or make her invulnerable.

The mother does not announce a theory or name her offspring. The rescued friend still wants to leave with Osa; rescue has not settled that disagreement. Outside, when all are safe, the mother's hand closes around the friend's leg as it turns toward the road. She can still hold it here. The brood now needs another shelter, and she has a plausible reason to insist that the eldest stay to help her. The travelling company would leave without it, postponing the child's desired future again. The child asks her to let go. The request makes its wish legible; it cannot magically or physically compel her. She chooses to open her hand without a falling mountain making the decision for her. She then asks Osa to come back on a visit when there is nothing that needs lifting. A possible last image is the mother learning to distinguish her child's receding six-footed tread from the sound of someone lost. The emotional movement is from preserving a child in an exact safe place to hearing that child remain itself while going elsewhere. The prose may find a quieter or less resolved ending.

Protect the mother's strange, practical tenderness and Osa's independent future. Challenge whether the slow growth, doorway accident, and immense feat feel earned; whether three visits provide enough attachment; and whether the name connection is legible without turning a human life into a stored-power explanation. The magic mechanism and precise ending remain open to discussion.
