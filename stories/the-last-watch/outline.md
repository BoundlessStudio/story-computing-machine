# Provisional direction

A close first-person story told by Olla, now seventy-two. Fifty years ago her young husband Talven followed the promised champion through a newly opened rift. Olla helped catch its widening edges and stayed with the watch. She has imagined its eventual closure as the day Talven comes home and their interrupted marriage resumes. Their daughter has grown old enough to have grandchildren; Olla has kept postponing a concrete invitation to live with her, such as a letter saying a bed is waiting in the front room. The watch was necessary and has saved real people. It has also become Olla's way of refusing a loss.

What comes back is Talven, scarcely older than the man who left, frightened and carrying a hungry infant named Biri. Barely two years have passed for him. Biri's mother is among those missing beyond the seam, not safely declared dead; closing it will foreclose their possible reunion. The child's particular appetite, fist and noise interrupt both the reunion Olla imagined and the watch's ceremony for the promised arrival. Talven finds an old woman where he expects his wife. Neither should respond with instant wisdom or a clean welcome.

The champion relieved Talven at a far-side holding post so he could carry Biri out, saying something like "Go. I'll keep it." Talven owes his freedom to that continuing service and cannot say whether the champion survived. Avoid turning him into a villain: he still deserves rescue. Talven has brought what the watch could not supply from this side, a living connection to the far edge, and can join it to the edge Olla's watch has restrained. The seal needs every keeper to release their own binding. Releasing before someone returns with the other connection would have let the tear spread. Returning was a long, dangerous journey through disrupted space, not something a keeper could have done by stepping out and back. Establish the constraint through an observable bodily connection and its effect, not a physics lecture.

Talven can preserve that returned connection only by remaining at the seam. He can discard it and leave, but then this known chance of closure goes and the keepers resume their old hold until another return. Olla can block the seal by retaining her own binding; she cannot magically possess him or insist he join her watch. Continued waiting therefore demands a present person's service. Olla initially wants to wait for the champion, and her motives include keeping Talven attached to the one task she knows how to share with him. Talven wants to close it and rest, but his refusal to wait carries shame and the debt to the champion. No countdown excuses either choice. Olla must choose without learning the missing people's fate or recovering the husband who would have aged alongside her. The other keepers have lives and wishes of their own; they are neither her voting bloc nor a uniformly enlightened chorus.

Possible ending: the watch lets go and Talven completes the seal. Closing may be abandonment as well as release; leave its correctness unresolved. Olla leaves with him and Biri for the daughter's house, where an old invitation does not guarantee an uncomplicated welcome. Their first ordinary contact is awkward and exact: perhaps he offers an arm and discovers which pace she can manage, or she lets him take a hand whose familiar work-shaped calluses now meet seventy-year-old skin. No speech explains release. The former duty mattered; the remaining lives will require acquaintance.
