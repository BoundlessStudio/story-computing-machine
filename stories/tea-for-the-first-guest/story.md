# Tea for the First Guest

The path disappeared six steps after the rain began.

One moment it lay under the young man's boots, a pale strip winding between black pines. The next, water sheeted down the slope and every hollow became a stream. Wind drove the rain sideways. He bent over the parcel beneath his coat, though the flour inside was already turning to paste, and tried to remember whether the path had passed the split boulder before or after the stand of silver-barked trees.

Lightning answered by showing him neither.

It showed him a tower.

For the length of the flash, a round wall stood on the ridge above him, broken at the crown and webbed with ivy. He had walked these woods since he was old enough to be trusted with a gathering basket. There was no tower on that ridge. There was hardly a ridge.

Thunder struck close enough to jar his teeth.

He scrambled toward shelter.

The climb took longer than it ought to have. Wet stones rolled under his heels. Branches caught his sleeves. Twice he saw the tower between the trees, and twice it seemed farther to his left than before. By the time his hands found cold masonry, he was shivering so hard he could not close his fingers properly.

The ruin looked less promising up close. Ivy covered everything but a narrow arch, and the door inside it had swollen crooked in its frame. No light showed through the cracks. He shoved once, twice, then struck it with his shoulder.

The door gave way without resistance.

He fell across a polished floor into warmth, lamplight, and the smell of steeping leaves.

Behind him, the door shut with a soft click.

For several heartbeats he stayed on his hands and knees, dripping onto a blue-and-gold carpet. The room surrounding him was round, high-ceilinged, and entirely intact. Shelves climbed the walls. A fire burned beneath a copper kettle. Hundreds of little glass panes enclosed a stairwell spiraling upward through green leaves and white flowers.

At a table laid for two, an old man in a faded red robe poured tea.

The young man rose too quickly and nearly slipped. He caught the back of a chair. The parcel under his coat sagged against his ribs.

The old man gestured toward the other cup. His hair and beard were white, but his hands were steady, his eyes dark and alert.

"You know, I didn't think when I accidentally entered a two-thousand-year-old wizard's tower I would get fresh tea," the young man said.

It was not the wisest thing he had ever said. Cold, fear, and the absurd neatness of the sugar bowl had joined forces against him.

The wizard lifted the kettle over his own cup. "Well, you don't exactly live to be two thousand by trying to kill the first guest you have in centuries, and I'm also starving for conversation."

He poured.

The young man looked at the door. It had vanished. Where it should have been stood a walnut cabinet full of jars.

"I would prefer," he said carefully, "that those be separate matters."

The wizard blinked. Then he laughed, a rusty sound that surprised him as much as it did his guest. "Quite right. Tea first. Murder never. Conversation if agreeable. Sit down before your knees decide for you."

They nearly had. The young man sat.

Steam carried a scent both green and smoky. He wrapped his hands around the cup but did not drink.

"Fresh," he said. "Truly?"

The wizard pointed upward.

Behind the glass, narrow beds followed the rising stair. Tea shrubs filled the lowest tier. Higher up grew herbs, beans, small fruit trees, and one determined vine hung with pale gourds. Drops of condensation ran down the inner panes. Little copper mirrors turned near the ceiling, directing lamplight into the leaves.

"Plucked this morning," the wizard said. "The second leaves from the new growth. I tried preserving the spring flavor for seventy-three years, but every spell left an aftertaste of copper. Some problems yield more readily to gardening."

The young man took a cautious sip. It was hot, bitter at first and then sweet in the back of his mouth. Nothing stung, burned, or transformed him.

His host leaned forward. "What year is it?"

"The year?"

"Whose face is stamped on the silver coin? Does the eastern road still flood? Are sleeves narrow or broad? Did they ever rebuild the bridge with six arches? What do people call that dance where everyone kicks at once? Is salt still taxed?"

The questions came so quickly that the young man held up a hand.

"Which one do you want answered?"

"All of them. Eventually." The wizard sat back with visible effort. "The year first."

The young man told him. The wizard repeated the number under his breath and stared into his cup.

"Then I have been here two thousand and eleven years," he said. "Approximately. The calendar has altered twice that I know of, and once in a manner no sensible person could defend."

"You said two thousand."

"Conversation suffers when one begins it with arithmetic."

The young man's shivering had eased. Hunger rushed in to replace it. His glance strayed to a loaf on the sideboard.

The wizard caught the look. "Forgive me."

He crossed to the sideboard, cut the loaf, and spread two slices with herb butter. He did not wave his hands and conjure a feast. He used a bread knife, frowned at the uneven pieces, and gave the thicker one to his guest.

"Starter is nearly three hundred years old," he said. "The flour is newer."

The young man looked down at the sodden parcel beneath his coat.

"Not that flour," the wizard added.

The bread was dense, warm near its center, and so good that caution lost an argument with his stomach. He ate half the slice in three bites.

"What brings you through the wood?" the wizard asked.

"An errand."

"For whom?"

"My family."

"Large?"

"Large enough."

"What work do they do? How far is your home? Are houses roofed in slate now? Do people still dry apples above the stove?"

The young man swallowed. "My mother will be waiting for the flour. My sister will say I stopped somewhere to avoid helping mend the roof. My father will go looking when it gets dark."

The wizard smiled as though each ordinary fact were a jewel set before him. "A roof. Excellent. Tile, thatch, slate, split wood?"

"Thatch. When can I leave?"

For the first time, the wizard looked toward the walnut cabinet.

Rain hammered somewhere beyond the walls, though the sound seemed impossibly distant. "When the storm passes. The slope is treacherous. I can show you a safe way down."

"And the door?"

"Where it has always been."

The cabinet remained a cabinet.

The wizard refilled the cups. "Tell me, are there still wizards attached to courts, or has that fashion ended?"

The young man answered because the tea was warm, because the bread was good, and because leaving in that rain would have been foolish even if he could find the door. He told the wizard what little he knew. There were wizards who served councils, wizards who kept shops, wizards who traveled and repaired small household workings. The wizard interrupted to ask what a council meant in this case, what shops charged for weather-charms, whether household wizards were permitted to marry, and why anyone would pay to warm a bath by magic when firewood existed.

Then he asked about wars.

The young man knew only the one his grandfather remembered. The wizard wanted troop numbers, banners, border changes, and the names of rulers. He could not provide them.

"Songs, then," the wizard said. "What are people singing?"

The young man recited the first verse of a tavern song about a boastful cooper. Before he reached the turn, the wizard cried out in delight.

"Still? They still sing that?"

"You know it?"

"A version. The cooper was a potter, and the goat was a magistrate."

"There isn't a goat."

"Then what knocks over the cart?"

"A magistrate."

The wizard laughed again, less rustily this time. He demanded the rest, then stopped the young man every few lines to compare words. Soon the song lay dissected between them like a machine taken apart by someone more interested in its history than in hearing it run.

The young man put down his cup.

"You asked what people sing," he said. "You haven't let me sing it."

The wizard's smile faded a little. "No. I suppose I haven't."

"And you asked about my family, but you don't know how many of us there are."

"Large enough, you said."

"Six."

The wizard glanced at the loaf, the tea, the green stair. "Six," he repeated, this time without a second question ready.

The room fell silent. Under the rain, the young man heard a faint ticking from the copper mirrors as they turned.

"You are cold," the wizard said at last. "And hungry. I have been a poor host."

"You've fed me."

"Bread is an apology for forgetting supper."

He moved quickly around the room, gathering onions, carrots, mushrooms, and a handful of herbs from the beds behind the glass. A little flame sprang between his fingers to light the stove, but after that he chopped and stirred like anyone else. His knife work was fast and exact. He asked no questions while he cooked.

The young man watched the place where the door had been.

"My father will start looking soon," he said.

"The storm will finish first."

"You can't know that."

"I can." The wizard touched two fingers to the wall. The rain's murmur sharpened, and for an instant a faint pattern of pale lines spread from his hand through the stone. "Less than two hours. The stew requires one."

"Then I'll go after the stew."

"And another pot of tea. There are so many things you've not told me. The western cities, for example. And whether anyone remembers the astronomer who proved the moons tug the sea. Or the tower-builders. Or me."

The last two words were quiet. The young man almost missed them.

"I don't know who you are," he said.

The wizard's fingers tightened against the wall.

"I see."

"That doesn't mean no one does. You haven't told me your name."

"Names acquire complications. Titles are safer."

"I didn't ask for either. You asked whether I remember you."

The wizard returned to the stove. "The stew will be ready shortly."

The young man stood.

His damp clothes clung unpleasantly to his back. The warmth that had seemed merciful now pressed too close. He crossed to the walnut cabinet and pulled one door open. Jars of dried beans stood three deep inside it. He shoved at the cabinet's side. It did not move.

"Please don't," the wizard said.

"Show me the door."

"The slope is unsafe."

"That is my choice."

"Your choice to break your neck? Your family would hardly thank me."

"They'll thank you even less if you keep me here."

The wizard set down the spoon. His expression had gone still, but the fire beneath the pot flared hard enough to lick its iron sides.

"I have offered shelter, food, and guidance."

"And decided when I may leave. That isn't hospitality. A guest has to be free to go."

The flame sank.

For a long moment neither spoke.

Then the wizard came around the table. The young man braced himself, ashamed of his fear and unable to dismiss it. The wizard stopped an arm's length from the cabinet and pressed his palm against its center.

It blurred. Shelves and jars thinned like mist. The door appeared behind them, plain wood in a stone arch, exactly where it had been.

"It was never locked," the wizard said.

The young man reached for the latch. It lifted. When he pulled the door inward, wind threw rain across the carpet and nearly tore it from his hand.

Outside, the ruined ridge dropped into darkness. Water leaped white over the stones. Pines bent until their crowns swept the ground.

The wizard did not move to stop him.

The young man shut the door.

"Concealment," the wizard said. "Not confinement. Though I understand that may feel like a narrow distinction from your side of it."

"Why conceal the door from the inside?"

"Efficiency. The same working covers both faces."

"Why cover either face?"

"To prevent intrusion."

"From the guest you've been waiting centuries to meet?"

The wizard turned away. "I was not waiting."

"You knew how long it had been."

"I keep records."

"And you want to know whether anyone remembers you."

"A matter of historical interest."

The young man looked at the two cups, the cut loaf, the chair placed opposite the wizard's own. Nothing else in the room was arranged for two.

"Why haven't you gone outside and asked?"

The wizard picked up the spoon and stirred the stew, though nothing in the pot needed stirring. "I have crossed battlefields under burning skies. I have bargained with kings who chained the messengers who brought them bad news. I have stood at sea in winter and diverted a wave taller than this tower."

"That isn't an answer."

"It supplies context."

"It supplies hiding places."

The wizard looked up sharply.

The young man's heart hammered. The openable door stood behind him. Remembering that helped.

"One honest answer," he said, "for one honest answer. You ask me something, then I ask you. Neither of us interrupts. If either refuses, the other may refuse too."

"A formal exchange?"

"A conversation."

The wizard's mouth tightened. "You are impertinent."

"My sister says so. That's one answer."

To the young man's surprise, the wizard laughed. Only once, but genuinely.

"Very well," he said. "Why have you truly been watching the door since you arrived?"

The young man considered offering something easy. The wizard had not.

"Because when I was small, I wandered from home during a fair. A kind woman found me and gave me a honey cake while people searched. I remember the cake. I also remember that she held my wrist whenever I tried to look outside. She meant well. I was still terrified."

The wizard waited.

"Why haven't you left the tower?" the young man asked.

The spoon stopped against the pot.

"I did leave, at first," the wizard said. "Frequently. Then less often. I survived my teachers, my rivals, my friends. I survived people I had once thought unbearably young, and their children, and theirs. Each time I returned outside, the world had shed another layer of what I knew. Roads moved. Words altered. Houses vanished."

He set the spoon down.

"Once, I went looking for a library that held my earliest work. A market stood in its place. No one there knew the library had existed. A bookseller sold me a little volume of legends in which a fool bearing one of my old titles was eaten by crows. I corrected him. He laughed."

The wizard's gaze traveled up the green stair.

"I came home angry. I told myself I would wait until the world grew wise enough to value what it had lost. Later I told myself I was protecting dangerous knowledge. Later still, that solitude was necessary for a great labor. All had some truth. None was the reason."

"What was?"

"That if I went out and nobody remembered me, I would have to live in a world that had no place prepared for me. Here I could believe the invitation had merely been delayed."

The young man did not interrupt. The wizard noticed.

"My turn," he said. "What do you believe your family will do if you are late?"

"My father will take a lantern into the storm. My mother will tell him not to, then walk to the gate and wait where the rain can reach her. My older brother will blame me. My younger brother will defend me and make everything worse. My sister will say she knew I was avoiding the roof, but she'll keep water hot in case I come back cold."

The wizard looked toward the stove. "Six."

"Six. Did you ever finish the great labor?"

"Which one?" The wizard smiled ruefully. "I began nine. I finished three. Two were useless. One was dangerous, so I dismantled it. The others became excuses with excellent diagrams."

The young man sat again. The wizard ladled stew into two bowls.

They ate under the rule.

The wizard asked what work the young man did. He repaired roofs with his father and brother, though he preferred carving pegs and fitting the wooden frames. He asked whether he meant to do that forever. The young man admitted he did not know, and that not knowing frightened him more than the work itself.

In return, he asked which daily task had kept the wizard sane. The wizard said pruning. Spells encouraged plants to grow, but they did not decide what growth ought to remain. His tea shrubs needed judgment, patience, and the willingness to cut off living branches for the health of the whole.

The wizard asked whether people were crueler now. The young man said he had not lived in any other time and refused to flatter the past for him. The young man asked whether two thousand years had made the wizard wise. The wizard said no, but it had given him an impressive collection of corrected mistakes.

They disagreed about the stew. The wizard claimed it lacked salt. The young man insisted the herbs were the problem. Together they discovered the wizard had used a sweet leaf where he meant to use a savory one. The wizard blamed a three-century-old labeling system. The young man blamed the person who had spent three centuries refusing to replace it.

After supper, the wizard made a second pot of tea.

This time he asked whether the young man wanted it.

The storm softened while they spoke. Rain ceased striking the tower and began to patter. The pale lines in the wall dimmed. Cold crept into the room as the fire burned low, and the wizard let it. He did not feed the flames or propose dessert or discover another urgent question.

At last the young man glanced at the door.

"I should go."

The wizard nodded. "Yes."

He packed the remaining bread and a stoppered jar of stew into a dry cloth. The ruined flour he accepted with grave ceremony and set beside his own bin, promising it might still thicken something. Then he touched the door, and the concealment over it vanished completely. The walnut cabinet did not return.

Outside, the storm had moved beyond the hills. Cloud still covered the sky, but the rain was fine and silver. The slope below shone with runoff.

The wizard took a lantern from a hook. With his other hand, he lifted the second teapot from the table.

"You'll spill that," the young man said.

"I once carried a basin of volatile moonfire down four hundred steps while pursued by assassins."

"Did you spill it?"

"Extensively."

The young man held out his hand. The wizard gave him the lantern.

They crossed the threshold.

The safe path was invisible until the wizard walked it. He tested each patch of ground with the end of a plain wooden staff, avoiding loose stone and sudden channels. He used no spell to flatten the hill or stop the rain. When the young man slipped, the wizard caught his elbow; when the wizard misjudged a muddy root, the young man steadied the teapot.

At the foot of the ridge, the trees opened. Beyond them, the familiar path lay pale beneath the clouds. Far off, where it reached the inhabited road, a tiny light moved between the trunks.

"My father's lantern," the young man said.

He raised the one he carried and waved it. After a moment, the distant light waved back.

The wizard stood just within the shadow of the trees. Behind them, the tower no longer looked ruined. Warm windows climbed its round wall, and green leaves pressed against the glass near the top.

"The road is that way," the young man said. "You can walk with me as far as it. If you want."

The wizard looked at the answering lantern, then at the tower. Rain ticked softly on the teapot lid.

"Will they remember me?" he asked.

"No," the young man said. "You'll have to meet them."

The wizard considered this, adjusted his grip on the fresh tea, and took the first step out of hiding.
