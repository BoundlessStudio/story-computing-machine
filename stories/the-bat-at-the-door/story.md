# By Wing and Welcome

The pear had dried inside the medicine dropper as a clear amber plug. I stood at the sink working it loose with a pin while Beatrix sat at the kitchen table and wrote one last line in her bat notebook.

“You don’t know it’s the last,” she said without looking up.

“He flew away a week ago.”

“That’s an observation. It’s not a conclusion.”

At sixteen, my daughter knew how to make a correction sound like testimony. Her notebook lay open beside the ventilated shoe box that had occupied our laundry room for three nights. It held the weights she had taken morning and evening, every water change, and the wing observations the wildlife-center volunteer had requested, followed by twice as many the volunteer had not. Inside the box were the old hand towel, a jam-jar lid, and several dark stains I had decided not to investigate closely. Beatrix had written the date of the bat’s departure on the last page and, below it, *Right wing level in flight. Circled porch light twice. Did not wobble.*

I rinsed the dropper. “The wildlife center said we can throw the box out.”

“They said they might.”

“The laundry counter wants its square foot back.”

That earned me the beginning of a smile. Seven nights earlier, we had watched the little brown bat climb the towel Beatrix had pinned inside the open box, hang for several minutes with his injured right wing folded tight, and then drop into the dusk. She had followed the volunteer’s instructions with maddening care: leather gloves, a closed door, daily photographs of the wound, no handling that could be avoided. The pear mash had been the volunteer’s reluctant improvisation after he refused every approved food and dragged himself toward the slice Beatrix was eating. He had skimmed the grass, lifted over the hedge, and vanished. Beatrix had clicked after him, two soft notes made with her tongue, the sound she had used to coax him to drink. Something above us had clicked back.

Beatrix maintained that this was evidence of a continuing relationship. I maintained that it was evidence of a bat.

Someone knocked on the front door.

Not the quick double tap of a neighbor, or the knuckle-and-bell assault of a delivery driver who had somewhere else to be. Three slow knocks sounded through the house. A pause. Then three more.

I put down the dropper. “Stay here.”

“I can see the door from the stairs.”

“That was not a counteroffer.”

The porch light showed me two people through the narrow pane beside the door. They stood shoulder to shoulder although there was plenty of room. Both wore dark coats buttoned to their throats despite the warm night. The taller one had a long, grave face and black hair combed flat behind ears that came to delicate, unnecessary points. The shorter one’s silver-brown hair was cut close to her head. Her eyes caught the porch light with a reddish shine, then went dark when she turned them away.

I checked the chain before I opened the door four inches.

“Can I help you?”

The tall visitor bowed. It was not deep enough to be theatrical, which somehow made it stranger.

“I am Raskel,” he said. “This is Vesper, my wife and balance. We have come by wing and welcome to the keeper of our son.”

Vesper closed her eyes for half a second.

“We’re Bram’s parents,” she said. “Your daughter saved him.”

Behind me, a floorboard gave a tiny complaint. Beatrix had not stayed in the kitchen.

“I don’t know anyone named Bram.”

“You knew him otherwise,” Raskel said. “He was injured. He came here in a lesser shape.”

“Smaller,” Vesper corrected. “Not lesser.”

Raskel inclined his head to her. “In a smaller shape. Brown-furred, narrow-faced, with a tear through the second joint of his right wing. He was sheltered in a box that smelled of oranges and soap. Your child fed him pear.”

There are sentences a person expects to hear at their front door, and there are sentences that make the chain feel as thin as thread.

I started to close it.

“We mean her no harm,” Vesper said quickly. Her hands remained visible at her sides. She had long fingers, bare of rings, the nails thick and dark as if bruised. “We came to thank her, not frighten you.”

“You came to my house after dark and said a bat was your son.”

“We did argue about the hour,” she said.

“The formal call is made at the rescued one’s hour of waking,” Raskel said.

“Raskel lost the argument and kept the hour.”

He looked genuinely wounded by this account of events. I would have found them funny anywhere but my porch.

“How do you know what was in the box?” I asked.

“Bram told us,” Vesper said.

“A bat told you about my laundry room.”

“Our son did.”

Raskel drew himself straighter. “He told us also that your daughter offered mealworms, which he refused past wisdom, and pear mash, which he accepted past dignity. That she replaced the water twice each night. That she made two notes before bringing the dropper near, so he would not startle and reopen his wing.”

Those were intimate facts. They were not proof. For three nights, the laundry-room window had been cracked to keep the room cool. Anyone willing to stand in the side yard could have watched us through the gap in the blind.

I thought of the stains in the box, of sickness, of teeth. I thought of Beatrix crouched on the floor in leather gloves, whispering, “I know you’re scared. I’m going to be boring now,” before she changed the towel. I had been proud of her steadiness. Now every small scene rearranged itself under the pressure of strangers who claimed to know it.

“What do you want?” I asked.

Raskel placed one hand over his chest. “When a child of our house receives the shelter that preserves him, his parents must come to the sheltering child. If she and her household accept, we become her godparents. We answer her call. We open our house. We stand between her and abandonment as your child stood between ours and death.”

The word *godparents* went through me more sharply than *bat* had.

“No.”

Vesper turned toward him. “I told you to begin with what it doesn’t mean.”

“A promise is diminished if first described by absence.”

“And an evening is lengthened if the door gets shut.”

“You are not becoming anything to my daughter,” I said. “You don’t get authority over her because she helped someone. You don’t get time alone with her. You don’t get to decide you’re family.”

Raskel’s pupils widened until almost no iris showed. Vesper touched two fingers to his sleeve, and he became still.

“We ask,” she said. “We do not decide.”

“He said *must*.”

“We must offer.”

“And if we refuse?”

Raskel answered before she could. “A welcome spurned is returned to the wing that bore it.”

“That sounds like a curse.”

“It means we go home,” Vesper said.

A faint scratching came from above the porch light.

I looked up through the gap. The light fixture was a square of frosted glass under the porch roof. Beyond it, the siding and soffit met in hard shadows. I could see nothing moving.

Vesper had looked up too.

“Who else is here?” I asked.

“No one who will cross your threshold uninvited,” Raskel said.

“That is not an answer.”

“Bram came with us,” Vesper said. “He’s above the door.”

I shut it hard enough to make the chain jump, then turned the deadbolt. Beatrix stood halfway down the stairs with her notebook folded against her chest.

“Back from the door.”

“Is he okay?”

“Beatrix.”

“They know about the mealworms.”

“Which means they know something. It doesn’t mean what they say it means.”

Through the door, Raskel said, “Our son’s wing is knit. His flight remains crooked when he tires, but he is in no danger.”

“He can hear us?” Beatrix asked.

“Apparently everybody can.”

She came down one step. “Ask them what side of the lid the water was on.”

“You are not going out there.”

“You don’t know the answer.”

I did not. I had supervised the gloves, made the calls, cut the air holes, and kept a folded towel under the door so our cat could not investigate. Beatrix had arranged the world inside the box.

I opened the door to the chain again. “Which side was the water on?”

Raskel glanced at Vesper.

“Left, near the corner,” Vesper said. “He knocked grit into it the first night. You moved it to the right on the second, and he knocked it over entirely.”

“Why wouldn’t he drink unless Beatrix clicked?”

“He could not see well in the room after striking his head,” Raskel said. “He knew the sound belonged to the child who did not seize him when he woke.”

From the stairs behind me came two quiet clicks.

The answer arrived above my head.

Two notes, the second higher than the first. Then a rustle no heavier than dry leaves.

Beatrix slipped past my arm before I could stop her. I barred the doorway with my forearm, and she stopped inside the chained door, close enough to see the visitors through the glass.

“Bram?” she called.

Another pair of clicks sounded from the porch roof.

Vesper’s face changed. Until then, she had been watching me with the patience of someone approaching a frightened animal. Now she looked up, and worry stripped the careful plainness from her voice.

“He didn’t tell us how you called him.”

“He came anyway,” Beatrix said.

Raskel lifted his chin toward the darkness. “Bram. Descend and present yourself to the household that preserved you.”

“Don’t order him,” Beatrix said.

At the same moment, I said, “Bring him down where I can see him.”

Beatrix twisted around and looked at me. “You too.”

“I need to know what’s outside my door.”

“He knows what’s inside.” She held up the notebook as if it were evidence in a trial. “You’re both making him prove things with his body. He doesn’t like being watched when he can’t fly right.”

Vesper looked at her husband. “She understood that from three days.”

Raskel lowered his gaze.

Beatrix turned and went quickly to the kitchen. She returned with the old towel from the shoe box. I nearly told her not to touch it with bare hands, then saw that she had already washed it. It smelled faintly of our detergent.

“I’m opening the door,” she said. “Not all the way.”

“Beatrix.”

“You can hold it.”

That was how she offered compromise: a practical job, already assigned.

I kept the chain on and opened the door as far as it allowed. Beatrix folded the towel into a thick strip and laid it on the inner sill.

“You can come to this side if you want,” she called. “Or you can stay up there. I won’t put you in the box.”

She clicked twice.

For several seconds, nothing happened. A car passed at the far end of the street, its tires whispering on pavement. Raskel and Vesper stood apart now, leaving the center of the porch empty.

A small shape dropped from the roof.

It opened its wings less than a foot above Raskel’s head and struck the doorframe with a soft, leathery slap. I caught the door before it bounced against Beatrix. The bat clung to the outside trim, trembling. His right wing sagged a fraction lower than the left, and a pale line crossed the dark membrane near the joint.

I remembered that line raw and red under the laundry-room light.

“Hi,” Beatrix whispered.

The bat crawled sideways onto the towel.

Vesper unbuttoned her coat. “May I come close enough to cover him? The change is private, and the light hurts.”

I wanted the door shut. I wanted Beatrix behind me. I wanted proof to be something clean that could be examined without granting anyone an inch.

“One step,” I said.

Vesper stepped up and spread her coat across the opening, making a dark tent between the door and the porch rail. Raskel turned his back. Under the coat came a rapid series of clicks, a shudder through the towel, and the muffled scrape of something gaining weight. Vesper reached inside her coat lining and passed a bundle beneath the screen.

The change took less than a minute. It did not sound easy.

When Vesper dropped the coat, a thin boy stood barefoot on the porch in gray shorts and a shirt too large for him. He looked eleven or twelve. His hair stuck up in soft brown points, and his face had the pinched, furious dignity of someone discovered after crying. He held his right arm close to his ribs. A healing scar ran from his wrist toward his elbow in the same curve I had seen on the wing.

He looked at Beatrix, then at the dropper still in my hand.

“I hate pear mash,” he said.

Beatrix’s mouth opened.

“I drank it because you wouldn’t stop,” he added. “And the worms were dead.”

“You’re a bat,” she said.

“Sometimes.”

“You could have told me.”

He stared at his bare toes. “I couldn’t change without tearing the wing again. And my mouth wasn’t right for words.”

I unhooked the chain.

“The kitchen,” I said. “All of you. The door stays unlocked, and nobody makes any promises until I understand the nouns.”

Raskel bowed again. “Your caution honors the threshold.”

“My caution is going to make tea.”

We went inward in stages. Beatrix and Bram crossed the entry together, with the towel around his shoulders. Vesper followed them. Raskel waited on the porch until I stepped aside for him, then entered last. I closed the front door but left the deadbolt unturned.

At the table, the visitors looked no more ordinary under the brighter light. Raskel’s ears were definitely pointed. Vesper’s eyes flashed whenever she turned too quickly. Bram seemed mostly like a tired boy until he clicked his tongue at the sink and flinched at the answer.

“Hard room,” he muttered.

The refrigerator compressor started. He clapped both hands over his ears.

“Does it always do that?”

“Several times an hour,” I said.

“Why?”

“It keeps food cold.”

He regarded the refrigerator as if this were an inadequate defense.

I set water to boil. Beatrix opened her notebook to a clean page but did not pick up her pencil.

“If I say no,” she asked, “does anything bad happen to Bram?”

Raskel placed his palms flat on the table. “The shelter already given cannot be ungiven.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

He glanced at Vesper, but she waited for him to try again.

“No,” he said. The word came out stiffly. “Nothing happens to him.”

“Do you get mad at me?”

“I would regret that our houses remained apart.”

“He’d be disappointed,” Vesper said. “I would too. We’d still be grateful. We’d leave you our number in case you changed your mind, unless you told us not to.”

I put five mugs on the counter, although I had not yet agreed they were staying long enough to drink.

“What does godparent mean to you?” I asked.

Raskel began, “By the old obligation of—”

“Tonight,” Vesper said. “In this kitchen.”

He considered the correction. “It means that if Beatrix calls to say she is unsafe or without shelter, we answer. If she needs what we can give, we do not pretend not to hear. We do not replace you. We acquire no custody. We ask before entering your home.”

“And no secret visits,” I said. “No taking her anywhere without asking me. No gifts with obligations attached. No deciding that your customs outrank my rules for my child.”

His mouth tightened at the last condition.

“If a rule endangered her?” he asked.

His objection was immediate and ordinary. My answer came more easily.

“Then you tell me,” I said. “If there’s no time and she’s in danger, you help her. Afterward, you tell me everything.”

Raskel nodded once. “That I can promise.”

“Is it magic?” Beatrix asked.

Vesper smiled. “No.”

“Not even a little?”

“No spell,” Vesper said. “We can break our word, and you can send us away. We would have to live with either.”

I poured the tea. “Beatrix, do you want them to be your godparents?”

She did not answer at once. She looked at Bram. He had lowered his hands from his ears but kept glancing suspiciously at the refrigerator.

“Does saying yes create anything between Bram and me?” she asked.

“No,” Bram said.

Raskel looked pained again, but Bram continued.

“I mean, I’d like to see you again. You’re bossy, but in a useful way. I don’t want you stuck with me because I got hurt.”

Beatrix nodded. “I don’t want your parents assigned to me because I fed you. That would be buying people backward.”

“It is not a purchase,” Raskel said.

“Good. Then I can pick.” She looked from him to Vesper. “If I say yes tonight, can I say no later?”

Raskel’s answer took a long time. Vesper did not rescue him.

“Yes,” he said at last. “A door that cannot close is not a welcome.”

Beatrix glanced up at me. However determined she was to choose for herself, she wanted to know whether I would stand beside her afterward or make the choice a fight between us.

I sat down. “My conditions stay.”

“Mine too,” she said. “And don’t come at night without calling. We have school.”

Vesper extended her hand across the table, palm up, stopping well short of Beatrix. “Agreed.”

Beatrix considered the hand and then touched two fingers to it. “Then yes. You can be my godparents the way we said it here.”

Raskel placed his hand over his chest. This time his bow was only a tilt of the head.

“By wing and welcome,” he said, “we will answer.”

The kettle ticked as it cooled, and the refrigerator started again. Bram groaned.

I put a bowl of fruit on the table. Bram chose a whole pear, which made Beatrix laugh so suddenly that tea slopped over the edge of her mug.

“You said you hated pear.”

“Mash,” he said. “It’s different when it has corners.”

“Pears don’t have corners.”

“This one does from sound.”

He clicked at it, then toward the dark pantry. His face brightened despite himself. “There are crackers in there. Top shelf, behind a metal tin.”

I checked. He was right, although he could not have seen inside the closed pantry.

“It lasts a few minutes after he changes,” Vesper said. “Very useful for finding things in the dark. Miserable in kitchens.”

Bram bit into the pear and winced when the sink rang under a dropped spoon. Beatrix moved the spoon onto a folded dishcloth. She did it without comment.

We arranged for them to come back on Saturday afternoon, after lunch, with a phone call first. Vesper wrote two numbers on a page from Beatrix’s notebook. Raskel added a third and said it reached a neighbor if both of them were flying. I decided not to ask whether that was a figure of speech. One impossible thing at a time seemed like a sound household policy.

When they left, I turned off the entry and porch lights, and Vesper screened Bram with her coat while he changed beside the front door. She carried him outside with the coat buttoned around him. A moment later, he slipped from the collar and went over the hedge on wings that still pulled slightly to the right. Raskel and Vesper walked away beneath him, their faces raised to follow sounds I could not hear.

I locked the door. Then I checked the porch roof, the windows, and both household phone numbers. Raskel answered the first. Vesper answered the second with, “You are correct to test them,” before hanging up.

In the kitchen, Beatrix had written *Saturday, two o’clock* on the calendar. The cleaned dropper lay beside her notebook.

“Can we keep the box until after they visit?” she asked.

I looked at the towel missing from it and the dark little stains in the corner. “Sunday morning. Then it goes.”

“That’s a conclusion,” she said.

I added pears to the grocery list.
