# Outline

## Story

- Proposed reader-facing title: *By Wing and Welcome*.
- Premise and central promise: A week after sixteen-year-old Beatrix nurses an injured bat in a ventilated box and watches it fly away, her unnamed parent opens the door to Raskel and Vesper, a nocturnal, not-quite-human-looking couple who say the bat was their son, Bram. They ask to become Beatrix's godparents under a family custom that answers lifesaving shelter with an offer of shelter in return. The parent keeps them on the porch, suspicious that gratitude is being translated into a claim on a child; the visitors cannot prove themselves without exposing the still-recovering Bram, who is hiding in bat shape above the door. Resolve the claim privately when Bram chooses to come down and change, Beatrix recognizes the patient she cared for, and both households separate care freely given from debt. Beatrix and her parent accept Raskel and Vesper as godparents only after the couple make clear that the role is a chosen promise to answer and shelter, not ownership, secrecy, or authority over her existing parent.
- Intended reader experience: Begin in recognizable parental wariness sharpened by an absurdly formal doorstep request. Let the couple's wrong-footed etiquette, Bram's embarrassment, and Beatrix's practical competence keep the uncanny scene warm without dissolving its unease. The reader should believe the visitors before the narrator is ready to, then feel relief when proof gives way to the harder question of what relationship anyone wants. End with a modestly comic household beginning, not a lore reveal or sentimental adoption.
- Generating force:
  - Mode: Relationship-led threshold negotiation. Each movement changes who is allowed to speak, be seen, or cross: strangers remain on the porch; Beatrix enters the conversation; Bram descends to the sill; the family comes into the kitchen; the offer is restated in terms all five can choose.
  - Focal attachment or contradiction: The narrator loves Beatrix's tenderness and is proud that she noticed and sustained a fragile life, yet treats the same daughter as too young to interpret the relationship her care created. Beatrix wants Bram safe and wants the adults to stop making her kindness either proof of innocence or payment for a bond. Raskel and Vesper want to honor a serious obligation without surrendering their son's privacy or frightening the household they mean to thank.
  - Plot-led wants and useful limit, if applicable: Not primarily plot-led, but the narrator immediately wants verifiable truth and the strangers away from Beatrix, while also wanting not to teach Beatrix that compassion was foolish. Their useful limit is protective overcontrol: they ask every question for Beatrix and initially mistake excluding her from the decision for keeping her safe. Beatrix wants to see that the bat recovered and decide whether these adults may belong in her life; she is observant but cannot evaluate unfamiliar adult custom alone. The visitors want their offer heard and Bram's transformation kept private; their limit is ritual language so solemn that a voluntary promise sounds like an irrevocable claim.
- Counterforce or complication:
  - Source: No antagonist. The pressure comes from incompatible meanings of family obligation, the narrator's reasonable refusal to admit strangers, the couple's reasonable refusal to demonstrate their son's body-changing on a visible porch, and Bram's shame at meeting the child who fed him pear mash from a dropper while he was helpless.
  - Credible inside logic, if a person: Raskel and Vesper protect Bram's safety, their household's secrecy, and a custom meant to prevent a rescued child from leaving another child unsupported. They assume asking formally is respectful and do not understand that “godparents” plus an unexplained night visit sounds like a custody demand. After challenge they translate, answer limits directly, accept Beatrix's conditions, and remain free to withdraw rather than becoming compliant props. Bram hid above the door by choice; his parents will not call him down merely to win an argument.
  - Pressure or change created: The narrator can obtain certainty only by making enough room for Bram to choose visibility, while the visitors can honor their gratitude only by treating Beatrix as a chooser rather than the beneficiary of an automatic debt. Beatrix must risk disappointing both sets of adults to prevent their protection and ceremony from speaking over the two children at the center.
- Narrative design:
  - POV, tense, distance, and limitation: Close first-person past through Beatrix's unnamed parent. The voice is attentive to locks, hands, exits, adult evasions, and every way the porch scene could become unsafe, but less attentive to the small replies Beatrix and the hidden bat exchange. Keep the visitors and children exterior; distinguish Raskel's ceremonial precision, Vesper's corrective plainness, Beatrix's concrete questions, and Bram's mortified brevity.
  - Time shape and compression: One continuous evening, roughly ninety minutes, seven nights after Bram flew from the recovery box. Fold in only short, object-triggered recollections of three days of care—the towel, dropper, pear mash, and the bat's refusal to drink unless Beatrix clicked softly—so the doorstep remains the present-tense pressure even though the narration is past tense.
  - Information and reveal strategy: Establish the rescued bat and the visitors' claim in the opening pages; do not make their identity a late twist. Let intimate facts accumulate without becoming conclusive: they know which wing was hurt, that Bram rejected mealworms, and the exact two-note click Beatrix used. Seed scratching and weight above the porch light before Vesper admits Bram came but is too ashamed to appear. Confirm the claim through his voluntary descent, the same scar and preference for Beatrix's click, and a brief change behind a coat held as a screen. Explain only the custom needed for this choice; leave their origin, numbers, and wider society unknown.
  - Governing movement: Put away the rescue supplies; answer the knock; hear the godparent request; keep the chain fastened; compare the couple's knowledge with Beatrix's notebook; bring Beatrix into the hall despite the narrator's first refusal; hear the familiar answering click above the door; learn Bram is present by choice; stop Raskel from ordering and the narrator from demanding; let Beatrix invite Bram down without inviting anyone farther; shelter his painful change from the street; recognize the healed-but-not-erased injury; move to the kitchen; translate “godparent” from debt into an offer; let Beatrix ask what happens if she says no; accept that nothing happens; let her say yes with her parent beside her; share fruit; arrange an ordinary next visit.
- Speculative surplus: For several minutes after changing, Bram still maps rooms by tiny tongue clicks and hears hard surfaces answer at different pitches. The refrigerator compressor overwhelms him, the sink rings unpleasantly, and he navigates a dark pantry better than any human present. This residual sense supplies material comedy and inconvenience but does not verify honesty, compel trust, or decide the godparent question.
- Structural distinction: A single-household threshold story in three inward steps—porch, doorway, kitchen—without chase, branching puzzle, recurrence, paired body-swap tracks, deadline mechanism, or gathering audience. The reveal occurs behind a coat at the half-open door; the decisive exchange occurs among five people at a kitchen table. No public proof, neighbor conversion, expert confirmation, institution, codified new order, or polished emblem follows.
- Decisive turn, deepening, or recognition: While the narrator demands that Bram come down and Raskel begins a formal command, Beatrix asks both adults to stop. She places the familiar towel on the inner sill, tells Bram he may use it but does not have to enter, and repeats the soft click that got him to drink. He descends, allows Vesper to screen him, and changes into a thin, bruised boy whose injured arm carries the same healing line as the bat's wing. His first words are an embarrassed correction—he hates pear mash; he drank it because Beatrix would not give up. Proof settles the factual claim, but Beatrix then refuses its implied arithmetic: helping him did not buy her another family, and his survival did not buy his parents rights over her. Vesper restates the custom as an offer that can be refused now or later. The narrator finally asks Beatrix what she wants and listens before answering.
- Aftereffect or live uncertainty: Beatrix accepts Raskel and Vesper as godparents in the limited meaning they have now made mutual: adults she may call for shelter or help, with her parent informed and included. The promise has no magical enforcement and gives them no custody or secret access. Bram joins them at the table in human shape, irritated by the refrigerator and determined to restore his dignity by eating whole fruit. The families exchange a contact method and set a daytime visit. The narrator still does not know what the visitors are beyond what this evening showed, whether Bram will remain a friend, or what future help might cost, but no one leaves claimed, indebted, or disbelieved.
- Opening / ending relation: Open with the narrator scrubbing dried pear from the medicine dropper and preparing to return the last bat-care supplies now that Beatrix insists her patient is gone. End after the visitors leave with the narrator adding whole pears—not mash—to the grocery list while Beatrix confirms the next visit time. The repeated food detail lands as practical embarrassment and changed household knowledge, not a declared symbol.

## Beats

1. Seven nights after the injured bat departed, the narrator cleans the last recovery tools while Beatrix records one final observation in her notebook; an after-dark knock interrupts their argument over whether the box can be put away.
2. Raskel and Vesper introduce themselves on the porch, look subtly ill-fitted to ordinary human presentation, identify the bat as their son Bram, and formally ask to become Beatrix's godparents. The narrator keeps the door chained and Beatrix out of sight.
3. The couple answer concrete questions with details from the nursing days, but every correct answer could have been learned by watching. Their solemn account of reciprocal guardianship makes the narrator hear danger where they mean gratitude.
4. Beatrix disobeys the instruction to stay upstairs, tests the visitors with her own observations, and hears the two-note reply from above the porch light. Vesper admits Bram came along in bat shape but does not want to be seen helpless or ordered to perform.
5. As both households' adults begin deciding what Bram must do, Beatrix interrupts, lays the old towel on the sill, and offers him a choice. He comes down. Vesper screens his painful shift, and the resulting boy confirms his identity through body, behavior, and mortified memory rather than spectacle.
6. In the kitchen, Beatrix asks whether refusing the godparent offer would endanger Bram, insult the family, or create a debt. Raskel's ritual answer falters; Vesper answers plainly that refusal changes nothing about the rescue and that the offer can carry no authority Beatrix and her parent do not grant.
7. Beatrix accepts the relationship as an additional source of help, not replacement parents or a secret compact. The narrator consents after naming their continuing place in any contact, and the visitors accept without trying to enlarge the promise.
8. Fruit and tea expose Bram's residual echo-sense and his opinion of pear mash. The families arrange one ordinary daytime visit; after the door closes, the narrator updates the grocery list while Beatrix updates the calendar.
