A sheep bell sounded under the jaw of the mountain. A little later the mountain sounded it back. Sarve had stopped on the Veyr Stair to get her gloves off and at the second stroke she raised her head. The echo had come from where it ought to, across the pass. She had begun to listen for these small instances of the world behaving itself. They were apt to occur at the same time as something worse. For example, she could see the sheep, fifty or so with a smudge of yellow on their backs, and she could see eleven inches of new white sky under their feet.

She could get them to stand in the shelter if she could get the drover to listen. Down at the turn he was leaning on his stick to let two ewes go by him abreast. It was warm enough under the rock for the thaw to go on dripping. She smelled the wool on the stone she was sitting on and rubbed her left palm on it to see if she could feel the grit. She could. They had let her keep the storm hook after the signing. It had stood in the corner of a borrowed room ever since, with some other woman’s bodice hanging over the fork.

Without getting up she said, “Come and have a look, Dannel. Put that away until you’ve looked.”

He came sideways up the step, his toes out like a man passing in front of people at a play. He had a rolled chart pressed under one arm. “I can show you from here if it’s the ground you’re worried about. There’s quite a considerable stretch of it with nobody on it now. I asked for the names of the occupied houses all the way round, and I’ve marked those. There are only seven. There’ll be people from the seven in town by this time. I can’t be sure for another hour.” He knelt, weighed one end of the chart with his knee, and followed her finger out into the pass.

“What’s at the bottom of that?” he said.

The ewe picked up her black foot and set it precisely on the white. The leg went in up to the shoulder. For two strokes of the bell the animal held there, looking gravely over its flank as if somebody had come up and leaned on it. Then it plunged back against the flock. Where the ewe had stood Sarve could see the bottom of a cloud. A line of blue fire passed through the opening and caught the loose end of the drover’s stick. He let it go. It flew straight up along the edge of the mountain until it was out of sight.

The drover spread his arms to turn the flock. The sheep began to mill, and the ones in front, with their heads too low to see over the bodies, pushed up the only clear ground toward the stair. Dannel got to his feet and made a noise as if he meant to shout. Sarve put a hand on his calf. With only the fold of the rock between them and the open sky, she could already feel the slight upward draw of the current against her palm.

“Let them come up to the jaw,” she said. “You can get the man over the stair rail if he needs a hand. How long have you known which houses you’d like me to aim past?”

He looked down at the paper under his boot. There was yellow ink on the far slope of Veyr and enough crosshatching to show all the ground the armies had burned. He drew a thumbnail down the middle of the yellow. “It would go here. There’s a mile of burnt ground before you come anywhere a family might be, and if you can tell me you need two miles, I’ll go round again. The seven houses are at the outside of what I took to be your reach. I used the gunners’ account of Hask. They said you put the whole length of the current down the main street, exactly where they’d told you the guns would be. That’s how I chose this place.”

“I was told they’d be in a field. There were sheets out to dry on the street behind it. Did your account mention those?” Sarve folded her gloves finger by finger and tucked them inside her coat. “Have you been out to the yellow part yourself?”

“I went this morning. There’s nobody there. They’re lighting the bakehouse ovens for the seven families in Veyr town.”

The flue at the Veyr bakehouse came up through a crack in a boulder behind the bread queue and smoked most heavily on the people who got there first. When her sister worked the station on the far side of the ridge, Sarve used to leave a fresh loaf under a stone for her on the long way up. Heth had asked to be let out of the hall to piss on the morning of the executions. She had been too proud to ask for anything else. Afterward Sarve had kept thinking how easy it would have been for somebody to give her that.

“Is this what you voted for, Dannel? I’ve wanted to ask you that since I got out. I had her bread in my bag that morning and you told me I’d been pardoned.”

He sat down beside the chart and put his hand on it like somebody holding down the paper in a game. “When we first came into the hall I had nine places marked. That was enough for the currents to go round, going by what your order had sent us before the war. I wanted a weaver at every place and one guard from each army to watch them. People said their border lay between one place and the next and it was quite impossible to have armed men from the other countries on their ground. Then they said they wouldn’t sign a peace with a weaver on the other side of the border. They’d all got a Hask by then. I spoke again at the end because they needed my vote. I said I would give it if one of you came out alive and if I got to say who. I said your name. It was my vote that made it a peace.”

Sarve watched him rub the edge of the chart with his thumb. He’d said the last sentence as proudly as if he’d carried a woman out of a fire. He looked tired enough to have done it.

“It was your vote that killed Heth,” she said.

He lifted his hand off the chart. “On the first list I sent in, Heth had the station over the ridge. When I took the second to the clerk it only had your name on it. I remember carrying it folded in my hand. I told the man at the door I was getting somebody out.”

The first sheep came close enough for her to hear it breathing. There was a hitch in the sound, as if it expected the air to be taken off it halfway through. She could scarcely keep sitting and listen to it. She got the hook onto her shoulder and took the stair above Dannel, her bare hand on the rock.

* * *

At the last bend of the stair the roof of the mountain ran out. She went another six steps in the open and found the socket with the end of the hook. Centuries of Stormweavers had worn it slant on the side that took the pull. In her first year at Veyr she had put the hook in backward and leaned on it without testing it. Heth had come across the ridge when she heard Sarve yell. By that time Sarve had climbed all the way back up and had words prepared about how it was poor practice to leave your station. She remembered that she had said them. Heth had stood there breathing hard, her sleeves full of blown snow.

Sarve gave the hook a pull. The iron would come up easily enough but it held against a sideways pull, and with a boot behind its fork she ought to stay on the stair. She stood with the other boot against the lip of the top step and looked up. The blue coat was blowing sideways off her back. What she had taken for sunset on the lower stair was amber fire going on steadily in the west. A black swell was coming through it, lit a little way through in blue, and the edge of the white opening ran down in front of the swell from high east to low west. Where it came down to the pass it had taken a length of the old road and the ground the road lay on. Farther up she could see firs slide into the white, upright with their roots and the earth they’d grown in.

What light went through the opening went up. She picked out the blue running close to the rock, the one that had carried the stick. It was rising into the main storm. She could get a hand to it. If she put its leading edge over the ridge where Heth would have taken it, she might move the white edge away from the pass long enough for somebody to get the flock off the slope.

“Get down below the hook, Dannel.” She could hear him coming up. “You’ll want two hands on the stair rail.”

She reached from the hip as she had been taught and closed her right hand. The light came in hot, with a weight to it that drove her heel against the hook. She waited for the first shaking to go out of her arm. On the far side of the ridge Heth had liked to start taking the weight early, before the turn was properly made. When they were on good terms Sarve would let her have it. Now Sarve had time to finish her turn exactly as she liked. She tipped her fist to the left, then back a little to ease it, and the same two movements went over the ridge in light.

The light went down out of sight. Off to her left she saw where the clouds ought to tear around it as Heth brought it onward. She had a blink in which nothing at all happened there, a longer blink than she could have borne six weeks ago, and then the light came up from behind the ridge with both its angles still in it. It swung back toward the open cut in the pass. She hauled it upward with both hands. She could see exactly how much ground she had to cross with it, where the slate roofs began and where they thinned out into the slope. She got it high and the light went over the roofs. What came away from the pass was a broad, flat stone from the face of the step below her. For a second there was earth showing, with little yellow roots in it. The slab tipped into the current and rode up and was gone.

A little way down the stairs Dannel sat with his back against the wall. The ends of his neckcloth had blown forward over his shoulders, and he had both hands around one of the uprights of the stair rail. Sarve called his name twice before he raised his head.

“Go down to the drover,” she said. “There’s room under the jaw if he brings the sheep in behind the rubbing stone. Take anybody you find on the stair in there with you.”

He looked past her at the near edge of the storm. It had climbed, and from where he sat he could see across to the burnt ground, which had gone a gentle black in the light. “Could you take it that little bit farther?” he asked.

It was a good place he’d found. He deserved to be told so and Sarve would have liked to tell him. She could see how to get the blue over the shoulder of the pass and into the old burns. She could do it and be down the stair with him before he was halfway, with the mountainside between her and the part of the sky she could still see failing. There was room for everybody under that great jaw. If she sat deep enough under it she might be able to shut her eyes.

“I need you off these steps,” she said. He went backward for the first few, hands on the wet rock, then got turned round and took to his feet. Sarve waited until his coat had gone under the corner of the jaw. The blue ran on through her right hand, clear of the pass. A long way above it, the white edge was going on cutting. Across the horizon a whole bank of cloud slipped out of sight behind the cut, leaving a little jagged remainder. The blue was the only part of the storm that came this low. If she brought it down across the burnt ground she’d have killed the one thing that might carry her to the rest of it.

It took her a while to get her foot out from behind the hook. She had put it in expecting to live by that piece of iron. The top of her boot had jammed against the fork and the leather was caught in a burr, so that she had to put her weight on it again before she could get free. All the time her hand was in the blue and it drew steadily at her, up toward the edge it kept trying to climb. She planted both feet on the last step and laid her free palm on the light. It held her there high on the rock like a sail sheet gone hard in the wind. There was a star visible to the left of the current. She wanted to establish where the step was relative to the star, in case she needed to reach it again, and found that her sky had moved while she was looking at it. She quit measuring and went out into it with both feet.

For some time she had the same feeling she got falling through a mistake in a stair. Her feet searched below her, one and then the other, and the muscles behind her knees tightened as her body went on preparing for the stone. The weight in her hands changed. When she let it draw past her left shoulder it lifted her until she could bring both knees up. She could lean a little harder and get above the blue and see ahead of it. She had to try this three times before it worked. Far below, a stick tumbled toward the mountain. She could see at a glance past it how the pass narrowed, how the towns lay across the shoulder of the land, how little shifting of the white would take the lot of it.

Bringing the current back to her own hands went against a great many things she had learned. All the teachers she’d had said to pass a current as far from yourself as you could, because your hands had to be clear when it came on again. The blue had brought its bend with it all the way through the first return. If she could put her hand on that bend again, she would have an end to receive. She let the current go on running above the mountains into the dark. She drew its leading edge round after that first turn, and felt the rest of the sky begin to come. It went on coming. The length of blue she rode thickened first with violet and then with a bright hide of fire. The currents that had joined in the storm were traveling with her now. In the west she saw where they had cut open an inlet from sea to sky and the white had spread through the soft green the sea usually kept around land. She put the storm round the white and went on.

The injury to the sky went ahead of her. At first she was afraid that the little black things rising out of it were people. She saw a whole wood come apart in the air. The sound of it reached her so late that it was still following her when the shoulder of the world turned and she could see day again. The light came up red behind her and on the underside of everything. The top of the storm was so near to her now she could see little streams within it going faster than the storm itself. She drew the one she rode inside the great sweep and began to take a sharper turn. The pressure came up through her arms and into the hinges of her jaw. She drew her tongue back because she had started to bite it without knowing.

There was a field of blue ahead in which she could see a first angle rise and a second one after it, exactly the little lift at the end for which Heth used to put up two fingers on the walk home. She had taken a short enough way round to reach it. She could see the place where she must put her left hand and wait until it came in. Her fingers opened of their own accord, with the outer two settling at an angle from the rest. When Heth was first teaching her to take a current, she used to slip her own little finger between those two to show Sarve how wide to keep them. Sarve looked down at their angle, right in the middle of everything. She could almost feel the light pressure of Heth’s finger. Then the blue came sharply toward the place where the fingers hung open. Sarve had to lower her hand until it was level with the other. She was going to touch it herself. The trouble she had once gone to, getting all the way back up the stair before Heth could see what had become of her, had come to nothing. She wanted her sister to see her now, badly enough that she began to say her name into the rush, and had to stop and use the breath.

She took the bend between her left thumb and fingers. The current had come around the whole earth and it came to her like a hooked gate with all the sheep behind it. Holding the bend apart from the length in her right hand was possible for the space of a breath. The other thing she could do, she found, was draw the two hands nearer. The width of the white changed when she did it. She drew the hands nearer still and saw the vast body of the storm close up on either side of her, such deep blue through the two uprights that she could see the life in every thread. She could feel where it would go if she let her hands clap shut. She held them apart by two fingers’ width and rolled both palms outward as hard as she could.

The light met a little way out from her fingertips. The broad storm wheeled after it into the bend and through again, and the front that had been sawing at the sky ran into the storm’s own body. She felt the coat jerk hard against her throat. Its skirts had blown up into the light and it was going round with the storm. She got two fingers under the catch at her neck and tore it loose. For a little while she and the coat were falling at different speeds. She got her hand into it by the collar before it could rise away, then held it against her face as the last of the light went by.

As the current went up there was clear air behind it. Sarve put out a hand into the low tail that had lifted her off the stair. She felt how weak it was now, with the weight going through the upper turn. It went through her fingers a little at a time, and the grate of the rock came up against her heel. She came down with one knee on the wet step and the other leg out in the air. She got that leg up and went down on her hands as far as the hook. She had to lie against the stone for a while before she could get a boot behind it and trust herself to look.

* * *

The light was gone from the cut in the pass. There was bare earth down there now, and the end of the road with enough of its wall still up to show where it had gone. The stone she lay on was cold through the coat. She got up as far as sitting and found a loose pocket caught against her wrist. She could feel the seam in her fingertips. She stayed that way until she began to hear what was going on under the rock.

By first daylight Dannel and the drover had brought the flock out from behind the rubbing stone. They let the ewes have the width of the stair. The one with the yellow rump rang her bell coming round the corner. The answering stroke came plain from the other side of the pass. Sarve pulled the iron out of its socket and sat with it across her legs while the flock came up.

She could see stars in the west and a strip of sun along the east. Between them the storm made its great blue passage, violet underneath where the land was still dark, brightening to fire as it went into morning. She watched for the turn, and saw it a long way off, running high beyond any mountain she knew how to climb. It was going as fast as it had when it was tearing the sky. It passed into itself and came back whole.
