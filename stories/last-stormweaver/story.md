A sheep bell sounded under the jaw of the mountain. A little later the mountain sounded it back. Sarve had stopped on the Veyr Stair to get her gloves off and at the second stroke she raised her head. The echo had come from where it ought to, across the pass. In the six weeks since they let her out of the hall she’d begun to listen for these small instances of the world behaving itself. Down in the pass at dusk there were fifty sheep or so with a smudge of yellow on their backs, and eleven inches of new white sky under their feet.

She could get them to stand in the shelter if she could get the drover to listen. Down at the turn he was leaning on his stick to let two ewes go by him abreast. Meltwater still dripped under the jaw. She smelled the wool on the stone she was sitting on and rubbed her left palm on it to see if she could feel the grit. She could. The storm hook slid across the blue of her coat and she caught it by the fork. They had let her keep it after the signing. It had stood in the corner of a borrowed room ever since, with some other woman’s bodice hanging over the fork.

Dannel, who’d spoken for the Veyr towns at the signing, had followed her up with a chart. He untied it and shook it out against his knees. “If you can put the first current that comes low enough into the ground, we can get people under cover before the next one,” he said. “I’ve got you a place. I had the old burns put on the chart.”

“Come and have a look, Dannel. Put that away until you’ve looked.”

He came sideways up the step, his toes out like a man passing in front of people at a play. “I asked for the names of the occupied houses all the way round, and I’ve marked those. There are only seven. There’ll be people from the seven in town by this time. I can’t be sure for another hour.” He knelt, weighed one end of the chart with his knee, and followed her finger out into the pass. “I’d taken that for snow,” he said.

The ewe picked up her black foot and set it precisely on the white. The leg went in up to the shoulder. For two strokes of the bell the animal held there, looking gravely over its flank as if somebody had come up and leaned on it. Then it plunged back against the flock. Where the ewe had stood Sarve could see the bottom of a cloud. A line of blue fire passed through the opening and caught the loose end of the drover’s stick. He let it go. It flew straight up along the edge of the mountain until it was out of sight.

The drover spread his arms to turn the flock. The sheep began to mill, and the ones in front, with their heads too low to see over the bodies, pushed up the only clear ground toward the stair. Dannel got to his feet and made a noise as if he meant to shout. Sarve put a hand on his calf. With only the fold of the rock between them and the open sky, she could already feel the slight upward draw of the current against her palm.

“Let them come up to the jaw,” she said. “You can give the man a hand when he gets to the steps. How long have you known which houses you’d like me to aim past?”

He looked down at the paper under his boot. There was yellow ink on the far slope of Veyr, where the armies had burned the ground. He drew a thumbnail from the middle of the yellow to the first marked house. “That’s a mile, and if you can tell me you need two miles, I’ll go round again. I used the gunners’ account of Hask. They said you put the whole length of the current down the main street, exactly where they’d told you the guns would be. That’s how I chose this place.”

“I was told they’d be in a field. There were sheets out to dry on the street behind it. Did your account mention those?” Sarve folded her gloves finger by finger and tucked them inside her coat. “I brought it down in the field they sent me to. By the time it stopped running it had gone all the way through the town. I’ll look at your yellow bit when I get up where I can see it.”

“I went this morning. There’s nobody there. They’re lighting the bakehouse ovens for the seven families in Veyr town.”

The flue at the Veyr bakehouse came up through a crack in a boulder behind the bread queue and smoked most heavily on the people who got there first. When her sister worked the station on the far side of the ridge, Sarve used to leave a fresh loaf under a stone for her on the long way up. Heth had asked to be let out of the hall to piss on the morning of the executions. She had been too proud to ask for anything else. The man at the door sent her back to the bench. Afterward Sarve had kept thinking how easy it would have been for somebody to give her that.

“I had Heth’s bread in my bag that morning when you came to tell me I’d been pardoned.” She put her finger on the yellow. “You could have told me what you wanted from me right then.”

He sat down beside the chart and spread his hand over the edge Sarve had touched. “When we first came into the hall I had nine places marked. Nine was the fewest the Stormweavers said they could manage on, going by what you sent us before the war. I wanted a weaver at every place and one guard from each army to watch them. People said their border lay between one place and the next and it was quite impossible to have armed men from the other countries on their ground. Then they said they wouldn’t sign a peace with a weaver on the other side of the border. They’d all got a Hask by then. I spoke again at the end because they needed my vote. I said I would give it if one of you came out alive and if I got to say who. I was thinking of Hask when I said your name. A person who could put a current down one street could put it onto a piece of empty ground. It was my vote that made it a peace.”

Sarve watched him rub the edge of the chart with his thumb. He looked as tired as a man who’d carried a woman out of a fire.

“I’d have liked to hear you make that speech to Heth,” she said. “She’d have let you finish.”

He lifted his hand off the chart. “On the first list I sent in, Heth had the station over the ridge. When I took the second to the clerk it only had your name on it. I remember carrying it folded in my hand. I told the man at the door I was getting somebody out.”

The first sheep came close enough for her to hear it breathing. There was a hitch in the sound, as if it expected the air to be taken off it halfway through. She got the hook onto her shoulder and took the stair above Dannel, her bare hand on the rock.

* * *

At the last bend of the stair the roof of the mountain ran out, and so did the sound of the sheep. Cold got to Sarve’s bare fingers before she’d gone another six steps. She found the socket with the end of the hook. Centuries of Stormweavers had worn it slant on the side that took the pull. In her first year at Veyr she had put the hook in backward and leaned on it without testing it. Heth had come across the ridge when she heard Sarve yell. By that time Sarve had climbed all the way back up and had words prepared about how it was poor practice to leave your station. She remembered that she had said them. Heth had stood there breathing hard, her sleeves full of blown snow.

The hook held when she pulled, and with one boot behind its fork and the other against the lip of the top step she ought to stay on the stair. She looked up. Her coat was blowing sideways off her back. What she had taken for sunset on the lower stair was amber fire in the west. A black swell pushed through it in silence, riding on a sharp blue point. Where the point went, the sky came open in a line of white. It had come all the way down to the pass and taken a length of the old road and the ground the road lay on. The hooked ridge between her station and Heth’s looked as if it had been cut through with a white saw. Firs slid into the opening, upright with their roots and the earth they’d grown in.

What light went through the opening went up. She picked out the blue running close to the rock, the one that had carried the stick. That was the part she could reach. If she could send it over the ridge where Heth would have taken it, the point might follow it away from the pass long enough for somebody to get the flock off the slope.

“Get down below the hook, Dannel.” She could hear him coming up. “You’ll want two hands on the stair rail.”

She reached from the hip as she had been taught and closed her right hand. The light came in hot, with a weight to it that drove her heel against the hook. She waited for the first shaking to go out of her arm. On the far side of the ridge Heth had liked to start taking the weight early, before the turn was properly made. When they were on good terms Sarve would let her have it. Now Sarve had time to finish her turn exactly as she liked. She tipped her fist to the left, then back a little to ease it, and the same two movements went over the ridge in light.

The light went down out of sight. Off to her left she saw where the clouds ought to tear around it as Heth brought it onward. She had a blink in which nothing at all happened there, a longer blink than she could have borne six weeks ago, and then the light came up from behind the ridge with both its angles still in it. It swung back toward the open cut in the pass. She hauled her right fist up until it was level with her shoulder. The point climbed and went west in the shape she’d put into it. The white went out between the two sides of the ridge and they were one ridge again. Just below her, a stone came loose from the face of the step. There was earth showing, with little yellow roots in it. The slab tipped into the rising blue and was gone.

Down the stairs Dannel sat with his arms locked round the stair rail. The ends of his neckcloth had blown forward over his shoulders. Sarve called his name twice before he raised his head.

“Go down to the drover,” she said. “There’s room under the jaw if he brings the sheep in behind the rubbing stone. Take anybody you find on the stair in there with you.”

He looked past her at the near edge of the storm. It had climbed, and from where he sat he could see across to the burnt ground, which had gone a gentle black in the light. He let go of the rail with one hand and pointed at the slope. “The current you’ve got hold of, could you put that there?”

“I could put this one there,” she said. “Now look up past my hand. See how the cut’s still running?” He tipped his head back so far he had to catch the rail again. The first bare slope in front of them went back as far as the yellow had on his chart. She had only to lay the current the length of it and she could be down the stair with him before he was halfway. There was room for everybody under that great jaw. If she sat deep enough under it she might be able to shut her eyes.

“I need you off these steps,” she said. He went backward for the first few, hands on the wet rock, then got turned round and took to his feet. Sarve waited until his coat had gone under the corner of the jaw. The blue ran on through her right hand, clear of the pass. A long way above it, the white edge was going on cutting. Across the horizon a whole bank of cloud slipped out of sight behind the cut, leaving a little jagged remainder. The blue was the only part of the storm that came this low. If she brought it down across the burnt ground she’d have killed the one thing that might carry her to the rest of it.

It took her a while to get her foot out from behind the hook. She had put it in expecting to live by that piece of iron. The top of her boot had jammed against the fork and the leather was caught in a burr, so that she had to put her weight on it again before she could get free. All the time her hand was in the blue and it drew steadily at her. She planted both feet on the last step and laid her free palm on the light. The pull nearly lifted the weight off her boots. There was a star on the left of the current. If she could fix in her head where the star stood above the rock, she’d know where to look for the stair on the way back. By the time she looked up from her boots, her sky had moved. She quit measuring and went out into it with both feet.

She had the same feeling she got falling through a mistake in a stair. Her feet searched below her, one and then the other, and the muscles behind her knees tightened for the stone. The weight in her hands changed. When she let it draw past her left shoulder it lifted her until she could bring both knees up, and on the third try she got above the blue and saw ahead of it. Far below, a stick tumbled toward the mountain. Beyond it the towns lay across the shoulder of the land. An inch of white, seen from where she was, would take the lot of them.

The point was a long way west already, with the two angles she’d made still in it. She rode after it with the blue at her right hip, bearing down until the length in her hand climbed into the currents above it and the violet came through, followed by the whole reach of amber fire that had lit her stair like sunset. If she got back to Veyr first, she could catch the bent point with her left hand and turn it into the length she was holding. All the teachers she’d had would have told her to get rid of what she held before she touched the point. She kept her right hand down hard against her hip.

The bent point was still widening the white. Along a coast of low red cliffs, black things rose out of the opening that for a moment she took for people. She saw one turn end over end and show roots, and then the rest of the wood came apart in the air. By the time she heard the wood break, she’d gone far enough round the world to see the sun again. The coast ended in a long cape and the storm was making a wide turn around it, far out to sea. Sarve leaned left, drawing the current at her hip toward the end of the cape until her feet swung ahead of her and all she knew of where she was going was the weight in her right hand. When she could see again, surf was breaking on rocks just below her. The bent point was still out on the long arc over the sea. She held the shorter course until the sun dropped away and the hooked profile of the Veyr ridge rose out of the dark.

The pass lay below her. Over its white light the point was coming round at her from the left, with exactly the extra lift at the end that Heth used to mimic with two crooked fingers when she wanted to make Sarve laugh on the walk home. Sarve reached to meet it. Her fingers opened of their own accord, with the outer two settling at an angle from the rest. When Heth was first teaching her to take a current, she used to slip her own little finger between those two to show Sarve how wide to keep them. Sarve looked down at the gap. There was nothing there but cold. She put her left hand level with the other. She remembered how the ends of Heth’s snowy sleeves had dripped while she was telling her off at the Stair. Heth had let her finish. She wanted her sister to see her now, badly enough that she began to say her name into the rush, and had to stop and use the breath.

She caught it in her left hand. The current had come around the whole earth and it came to her like a hooked gate with all the sheep behind it. The point was trying to run down into the white. When she pulled it across her body toward the length in her right hand, the opening narrowed below her and a whole bank of wet cloud spilled free. She drew her hands closer, and the pull took hold up both arms into the hinges of her jaw until she tasted blood where she had got her tongue between her teeth. The blue on either side of her was drawing her fists together faster than she could keep them apart. She turned both wrists outward while she still had room, so the two streams went clear of her hands and into the air in front of her.

The light met a little way out from her fingertips. The broad storm wheeled after it into the bend and through again, and the front that had been sawing at the sky ran into the storm’s own body. She felt the coat jerk hard against her throat. Its skirts had blown up into the light and it was going round with the storm. She got two fingers under the catch at her neck and tore it loose. For a little while she and the coat were falling at different speeds. She got her hand into it by the collar before it could rise away, then held it against her face as the last of the light went by.

As the current went up there was clear air behind it. Below her was the Veyr Stair, dark except for a trickle of blue along the outer edge. Sarve put out a hand into it and felt how weak it was now with the weight going through the upper turn. It went through her fingers a little at a time, and the grate of the rock came up against her heel. She came down with one knee on the wet step and the other leg out in the air. She got that leg up and went down on her hands as far as the hook. She had to lie against the stone for a while before she could get a boot behind it and trust herself to look.

* * *

The light was gone from the cut in the pass. There was bare earth down there now, and the end of the road with enough of its wall still up to show where it had gone. The stone was cold through the coat she’d balled up under her chest. She got up as far as sitting and tried to pull a loose pocket off her wrist, only to have her fingers cramp shut on the seam so that she had to pick them open one at a time. The drover clicked his tongue below the jaw. Dannel tried the sound after him.

By first daylight Dannel and the drover had brought the flock out from behind the rubbing stone. Dannel had the chart folded to a wet square in his belt and stood aside to let the ewes have the width of the stair. The one with the yellow rump rang her bell coming round the corner. The answering stroke came plain from the other side of the pass. Sarve pulled the iron out of its socket and sat with it across her legs while the flock came up.

Stars hung in the west and a strip of sun showed along the east. Between them the storm made its blue passage, violet underneath where the land was still dark, brightening to fire as it went into morning. She watched for the turn, and saw it a long way off, running high beyond any mountain she knew how to climb. It was going as fast as it had when it was tearing the sky. It passed into itself and came back whole.
