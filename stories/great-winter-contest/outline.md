# Provisional direction

The Great Winter Contest is the Ice Kingdom's famous snow-beast steeplechase. At dawn, each entrant gets an equal mound of snow and sculpts a mount; by noon, its maker must ride it through a spectacular course above and across the palace lake. Riders supply the magic and movements themselves. They can reshape their mounts by touch, but cannot add snow; reshaping uses the same finite attention as steering and supplying the gait. Rebuilding a loaded knee means stopping or hobbling, and changing an entire animal would require learning unfamiliar handling. The course's marked gates and finish make the winner legible.

Rilka has made exquisite little moving snow rabbits for twenty years. She loves them and resents how thoroughly they have made her the rabbit lady: good for children's presents, never anyone's idea of a champion. She spends money she can ill afford on her first contest entry and a magnificent cape. Her full-sized hare is deliberately beautiful, fast in a straight line, and much less sensible to sit on than anything she has sold. She wants the trophy without having to pretend she entered for fun.

Her chief rival, the reigning champion, rides a superb snow stag. He is competitive and knows the course; he is neither a cheat nor a disguised bully. His habit of opening room for a broad, fast outside sweep should be visible early. Other entrants provide real spectacle and different approaches. The hare's high opening bounds delight the crowd and clear successive ice ridges in fewer strides, putting Rilka ahead. Full-sized landings accumulate strain at one heel. She feels this and can lower the gait, but keeps her advantage a little too long; liking how it looks helps her rationalize the racing judgment. The stag catches her through better pacing and turns.

The strained heel breaks. Rilka must slow, losing places while moving snow from the exquisite ears into the damaged joint. In the recovery, a lowered crouch takes load off the heel and produces an unexpectedly quick glide. She recognises the low, folded dart she uses when little rabbits dodge reaching hands, and tries it for a stretch before the finish. The altered snow distribution changes her balance and the repaired leg's flex; the mount is sounder but unfamiliar. The final terraced descent ends in the last leap. The stag's broad traverses retain its gallop; a direct sequence of drops is shorter but would destroy the old high-bounding hare. Rilka can attempt it with the folded gait, sustaining a new cadence at speed and hoping the stiff repaired leg will gather and release cleanly for the leap. The champion sees her gaining and presses his own line harder. This is a risky sporting choice based on what she has felt, not a discovery of an unmarked shortcut or new magical reserve. A narrow win is the current possibility; the writing may earn a loss instead. Either way, she rides the finish rather than receiving recognition for a rescue or a loophole.

Close with the actual result, her unapologetic reaction to it, and a particular exchange with the rival or someone who has come to watch her. The cheering should still be about the contest. Her small-scale rabbit expertise is extended under pressure, not renounced, and an odd-looking repair is not a moral achievement. Aim for buoyant close third person, tactile winter spectacle, and competitive people who enjoy the same peculiar thing. The exact maneuvers and ending remain open to discovery.
