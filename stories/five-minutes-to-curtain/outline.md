# Five Minutes to Curtain

Provisional direction: a close-third backstage farce, following Werrit, the auburn-haired comic actor in the beloved Lantern Company. He adores playing the hind legs of their dreadful cloth dragon. His human appearance is a shape he deliberately holds; his colleagues have never known he is a dragon. He has spent years sending his parents doctored playbills that make him the heroic lead.

Five minutes before tonight's curtain, his mother puts an enormous gold eye to the dressing-room window. His father is settling into the theater's monster box. Werrit's false final-performance notice, intended to discourage a visit, has brought them to what they think is their last chance to see his triumph. Mother has kept every notice; Father has practiced waiting for the end of his son's great speech before applauding. Werrit calls her Mother, scorches his forged playbill with a panicked breath, and has to confess both his species and his letters. The whole cast unravels at this intimate surprise and the discovery that he has advertised them as his supporting players. Short armored Tib refuses to surrender his spectacular fall; taller Varlo offers Werrit the villain's seven-minute death instead; Bessa, already crowned and laced into violet, will not have her queen's entrance cut. Nalla plays the dragon's front as well as calling cues; she wants to try the new false-sneeze sequence she and Werrit have perfected. Dressing, argument and prop changes overlap. Werrit helps rearrange the opening, greedily enjoying a chance to be magnificent. For his promotion, they stuff the dragon's rear with coats and put it on the wheeled throne base.

The hastily altered opening can succeed: Werrit has a splendid entrance and Nalla can wheel the inert dragon into his sword for the heroic finish. But she begins their familiar sneeze setup and leaves the space for the hindquarters' indignant reply. He can deliver his great line and take the applause, letting their shared piece die under her. Instead he passes Tib the sword, ducks under the cloth in his heroic costume, and answers Nalla's cue. His helmet appears at the dragon's rear. The whole company must complete the old routine with the wrong props and a man in the wrong costume. They earn the rescue through physical craft; Werrit makes his choice before the parental response. Their laughter does not erase the false notices, and he may still want the sword afterward. The exact final exchange remains open for prose to discover, with unfinished family accountability and shared delight both present.
