# A Question for the Sky

The radiator vane should have been black from hinge to tip. Halfway along it, something red snapped and bellied in the station's wake.

Naro locked one boot beneath the service rail and checked the timer inside her visor. She had twelve minutes left in the maintenance window, one sluggish hinge actuator still to grease, and a heat load already climbing through the pipes under her gloves. The red thing had wound a shining filament around the vane's root. Each time it pulled tight, the vane shivered off its commanded angle.

She tagged it as filament fouling. Objects escaped the station: insulation flakes, packing net, once an entire laundry drum whose underdeclared spin had sent it tumbling silver against the dark. Nothing came up from the planet. For eight centuries, every child born in the rings had learned the same three facts about the world below. Its oceans had soured. Its land had burned. Nothing lived beneath the weather.

Naro did not recite the lesson as she edged out along the rail. Doctrine belonged to schoolrooms and the beginning of memorial shifts. A snag was enough; snags became tears if a mechanic let them argue with her.

The thing was cloth.

It had been sewn into a lopsided diamond over two thin wooden spars. Wood itself was rare enough on the station that Naro stared before remembering the heat ticking upward at her wrists. The cloth was pieced from blue, yellow, and red scraps. Some stitches were doubled, some wandered. A knot at the bridle had cinched so hard that the cord had bitten halfway through the wood. White mineral stains ringed three old wet patches. The cut ends of the fibers were soft, not yet crazed by radiation.

Newly made, Naro thought, and then refused the thought its grammar.

She clamped the vane, cut the conductive thread, and folded the cloth against her suit. The hinge settled after two strokes of grease. By the time the sector controller accepted her repair, sweat had gathered at the base of her neck.

Inside the air lock, the cloth gave off a smell she had no category for: wet dust, perhaps, with something green and bitter underneath. Naro laid it beneath the quarantine hood and found writing on the inner blue panel.

The characters were blocky and dark, brushed on by a hand that had pressed harder at the end of each stroke. She knew them. Every mechanic did. They survived on equipment too old or too important to reskin: ABOVE on lift lugs, PERSONNEL on access locks, WATER beside emergency drains. Apprentices learned component nouns and imperative verbs so an eight-hundred-year-old warning could still keep their fingers attached.

Naro read the cloth one label at a time.

PERSON. ABOVE. FALLING WATER. The final mark was not a word she knew, though its hooked shape appeared twice. A query, perhaps. A destination code ran below the sentence in smaller writing: TELU.

She turned the kite over. The back offered only knots, faint smears, and a repair patch cut from fabric printed with rows of tiny leaves.

The accepted report for her shift read FILAMENT DEBRIS REMOVED; NO IMPACT DAMAGE. Naro submitted it before she could invent a more dangerous noun.

Then she rolled the kite beneath her jacket and went to the disposal ring.

Spent supply capsules were lined along the transfer cradle like blunt white teeth. They had brought seals, grain cultures, medicine, bearing paste, and their pressure shells were too fatigued for another ascent. Most now held compacted waste and waited for the burn corridor, where friction would eat the shells before any fragments reached the dead ground. One empty retirement capsule stood open while its mass scanner recalibrated.

Naro tore the unprinted margin from a condemned filter sleeve. With a maintenance marker, she copied two characters she had seen together on ten thousand status plates.

ABOVE OPERATING.

Not person. Operating was measurable.

She sealed the strip inside the capsule's empty document recess. The snag's approach vector was still in her wrist display; the disposal control offered a family of permitted releases through the same band of atmosphere. Naro chose the late edge of the next window. She altered no guidance, added no propellant, and told herself that a strip of filter cloth could not change a landing on a lifeless world.

The cradle closed. The capsule rolled away.

***

Six days later, the radiator sector came under Naro's name again. She had checked the assignment board so often that morning that the system warned her against duplicate requests. The planet filled half the view below the service rail, all white weather and brief blue-black gaps. Naro tried to watch the coolant gauge. She tried not to look along the vane.

A triangle of yellow cloth trembled at its tip.

This kite was smaller. Its bridle thread had fused into a glassy bead where it touched the charged edge. Naro freed it with both hands, careful not to tear the picture sewn to its center.

Someone had drawn a capsule as a white oval with flame streaming behind it. Beneath, the oval lay in a black furrow beside a square roof. The roof's edge was black too. Two small figures tipped buckets over it. At the bottom, the unknown writer had copied an old hazard triangle and filled it with the maintenance word HOT.

Naro carried the kite inside before translating the rest.

NO MORE ANSWER, the message said, IF HOT FALLS HERE.

Beside the words was a map too careful to be a child's idle decoration. It began at the first capsule's black furrow, with an arrow following the direction the shell had skipped. Twelve short marks, each the length of the drawn furrow, stepped along the arrow to a bare circle crosshatched in gray. Three pale boulders made a triangle beside it. The roofs, water, and planted squares lay off the other edge of the cloth. There was no compass mark or absolute coordinate, but the burn scar supplied scale, the logged skip supplied bearing, and the boulders supplied a reference Naro could seek from above. The writer had shown her how to find a safe landing patch without surrendering the settlement's location as a number.

TELU appeared again at the lower edge.

Naro put her palm over the drawn roof. The capsule had not burned away. It had gone down too shallow, shed enough speed to survive, and struck near somebody's shelter still carrying reentry heat. Her answer had arrived as a hazard warning of its own.

She took the kite to the service shop after second shift, when the benches were empty. Discarded supply capsules were permitted to vary widely in mass, but not in descent behavior. An intact one was a hammer. By dawn Naro had stripped a retirement shell of its latch block, pressure liner, and cargo web. She cut a drogue from heat-cured packing membrane and stitched its load tapes through the shell's old restraint points. The work was familiar in pieces and unprecedented as a whole.

She ran the configuration through the disposal model. The drogue opened too early and tore. She shortened the lines. It opened too late. She changed the pilot fold, then loaded the shell with crushed ceramic to mimic the message mass and fired it down the station's test shaft. The membrane snapped full before the marker band and lowered the shell onto the padded arrestor with a force that would bruise a shin, not burn a roof.

Only then did Naro prepare another capsule. She called up the first shell's logged track in the downward maintenance optics and searched backward from its release. The blackened footprint appeared between cloud banks, a narrow dash ending at the roof. Naro aligned it with the recorded skip direction, counted twelve scar-lengths along that bearing, and found three pale points grouped in the triangle Telu had drawn. The crosshatched patch lay beside them, bare even at the optics' poor resolution.

On a square of filter cloth, she copied the bare landing circle and drew the drogue above it. She added a picture of the kite climbing past a storm cloud, then the labels KITE, RISE, HOW. The old script had no grammar Naro could reliably use. Maintenance language assumed an object in front of you and a problem already happening.

She closed the capsule around the message and held it on her workbench until the mass reading stopped changing. This time, when she altered the release time, she used the logged track and Telu's twelve-furrow offset to aim for the empty patch beside the three boulders.

***

The answer came up nine days later on green cloth that had once been a sleeve. Three pictures crossed its panels. In the first, a figure stood in slanting rain, letting out line from a flat spool. In the second, the thread climbed into a black cloud split by white branches. The figure had tied the line to a stake and stepped far back. In the third, the station passed overhead as a long moving spark. The thread curved toward its wake; near the top, a knot opened and the kite flew free.

The pictures explained enough. During the strongest storms, charge climbed the wet, conductive thread. The radiator system cast its own charged wake when it dumped heat. Somewhere between cloud and station, two forces that had never been designed to meet pulled the little sail upward.

The writing underneath corrected Naro's order.

HOW DOES KITE RISE?

Then, in darker strokes:

CAPSULE FELL CORRECT. CAPSULE WAS WARM. ROOF WAS SAFE.

TELU.

Naro read WARM twice. She had known the temperature from the model. The word still loosened something behind her ribs that calculation had held rigid for nine days.

She copied the corrected question into a notebook made from the unused backs of inspection cards. She drew columns for objects, actions, qualities, and the small joining marks she had once dismissed as stains. TELU went in a fifth column headed ROUTE.

After that, waiting entered her work. The return corridor crossed the radiator wake every six or seven days, depending on how the station trimmed its orbit and how far the storm columns wandered. Some crossings brought nothing. On others, Naro found a kite worrying at the vane as if it had been there for years and had merely waited for her to notice.

She never delayed a heat cycle. When the sector needed purge, she purged it. Charge leaking along the maintenance trunks killed the yeast starters in the nearest galley bays, as it always did during heavy dump weeks, and the corridor filled with the warm mineral smell of dense, unleavened rounds. Inner-ring crews traded risen loaves at insulting prices. Naro ate her ration at the service bench while she pared grams from empty capsules and practiced a language built from caution plates.

She drew her meal for the next message: a gray circle scored into six wedges. The reply labeled it MOON. Naro sent back NO, FOOD, with a picture of teeth.

The following kite carried two circles. One hung above a roof with light marks around it. The other sat on a flat stone above a fire, with fingers breaking its edge. Telu had crossed out Naro's word order and written FOOD: FLATBREAD. Naro laughed loudly enough that the empty service shop answered her from its vents.

The exchanges widened by such corrections. Telu drew rain beating seedlings flat and, beside it, seedlings standing again under the sun. Naro answered with the station's water loop: a cup, a drain, a pipe curling around to fill the cup again. Telu sent a roof patched in three different colors and a row of people walking around black scars where old things had fallen from the sky. Naro drew her exterior gloves, each finger thick as a tool handle, and a radiator vane shedding heat in lines no eye could see.

She asked no question about population. She did not request soil, seed, blood, or coordinates. Once she began to draw a map of the planet with a query mark over the land, then blacked it out so thoroughly that the filter cloth stiffened. Telu had given her a safe circle, not an invitation.

The child's impatience came through even before Naro knew to call the writer a child. Corrections slashed through bad words. Questions crowded the hems when the central cloth was full. One red kite arrived with its tail shortened to make room for a complaint: ABOVE ANSWERS SLOW. RAIN DOES NOT WAIT.

Naro replied BELOW SIGNAL RECEIVED, because it was a familiar phrase and because it hid her behind the station's habits. When a drogue landed wide of the gray circle, she wrote CAPSULE CORRECTED. When Telu asked whether there were roofs above the rain, Naro drew the pressure hull and labeled it ENCLOSURE SAFE.

Every response was accurate. None exposed more of her than a status panel would.

In the seventh week, a blue kite came in during a weak dump, barely high enough to catch the vane. Its cloth was soaked through before launch; in vacuum the remaining moisture had bloomed into frost along the seams. Naro tucked it beneath her arm and hurried through the lock while the fibers were still flexible.

At its center, Telu had drawn a person.

The figure was small beside the roof, with knees showing below a square tunic and dark hair sticking out in several directions. One hand held the kite spool. The other pointed to the familiar mark Naro had filed under ROUTE.

TELU.

Above it, copied with painstaking exactness, was a label Naro recognized from every suit locker on the station:

PERSONNEL NAME.

An arrow led from those words to TELU. A second arrow led to an empty box beside a drawing of a suited figure on a radiator rail.

Naro sat on the air-lock floor.

The repeated mark was not a route. It had never named a corridor through the weather or a launch site. It named the hand that sewed crooked stitches and crossed out Naro's grammar. Telu. A person small enough to stand below that roof, stubborn enough to launch questions into a storm.

With the name, the opening sentence changed. Naro took the first red kite from its sealed drawer and spread it beside the blue one. She added the joining marks Telu had taught her, moved the hook from destination to query, and read what had been waiting there all season.

IS THERE A PERSON ABOVE THE RAIN?

Not: Is the machinery operating? Not: Did a signal reach you? Telu had asked for someone and received a chain of system statuses.

On the blue kite, below the empty name box, the child had written the question again. The disposal forecast showed one more safe pass through the corridor before the storms weakened. Naro had a tested drogue already folded and a retired shell cleared for release. She also had an inventory notice in her queue: three capsule liners lighter than their registered retirement masses. The notice was automatic, low priority, and due for reconciliation in eleven days.

She could send another anonymous answer. ABOVE PERSONNEL PRESENT would satisfy the grammar. It would disclose nothing that a population estimate had not already implied.

Instead, Naro wrote until the old letters stopped looking like labels.

PERSON ABOVE. NAME NARO. I HEAR TELU.

I was not printed anywhere in the station's inherited warnings. Naro built it from the person-mark and the near-pointing stroke Telu used in corrections. HEAR came from the alarm instruction inside every pressure shelter: IF ALARM HEARD, REPORT. She could not be certain it meant what she needed. She drew herself beside the radiator vane, one gloved hand raised, and pointed the name field at her chest.

The message weighed four grams. She took out the drawing, shaved its margins, and put it back. She opened the drogue on the bench, inspected every stitch, folded it again, and seated it in the supply capsule.

At release, the discarded shell turned once beyond the cradle, found its blunt end, and vanished toward the white weather.

***

The lift season ended without ceremony. Storm charge fell below the radiator's attraction threshold. The station adjusted its orbit, moving the return corridor eastward across cloud tops that revealed nothing.

Naro completed two ordinary exterior shifts. On the third, she found no cloth and told herself she had expected none. The vane angle held within tolerance. Coolant pressure stayed flat. In the service shop, the inventory notice climbed from low priority to routine review.

Then a final kite reached the station on the ragged edge of a heat dump. It was so small that at first Naro mistook it for a strip of insulation. No wooden cross held it open, only two springy reeds and a diamond of pale fabric. The thread had parted cleanly at the release knot. Whoever had made it had learned exactly how little sail the wake would take.

Naro brought it inside. There was no picture on the first panel, only a sentence in compact, careful writing.

TELU HEARS NARO. NARO IS A PERSON ABOVE THE RAIN.

The answer returned to her, corrected and complete.

On the second panel, Telu had drawn a roof under heavy rain. A jar stood beneath one corner, filling from the eaves. Beside it floated Naro's sealed cup and looping pipe. The new question ran down the narrow remaining space:

HOW DOES NARO DRINK WHERE RAIN DOES NOT FALL INSIDE?

By the next shift the charged season was over. Naro freed the radiator vane, verified its hinge, and closed the same kind of service ticket she had closed on the first day: FILAMENT DEBRIS REMOVED; NO IMPACT DAMAGE. The station kept its schedule, its school lessons, and its course around the clouds.

At her bench, the capsule discrepancy waited in the queue. So did the long months before another capsule could follow the same corridor.

Naro laid a condemned condensation diagram on the scale. It showed vapor cooling against a pipe wall, gathering into drops, and returning to the drinking reservoir. Five grams. Too heavy.

She trimmed away the title block and began translating the arrows.
