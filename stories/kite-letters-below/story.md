# A Question for the Sky

Naro first knew the kite as a three-degree error.

Vane fourteen had stopped following its commanded angle. With the station pouring noon-side heat into the radiator wings, three degrees was enough to wake a yellow fault on her wrist. She hauled herself along the exterior rail, boots clipping and unclipping in practiced sequence, expecting a warped hinge or a clot of frozen lubricant.

Instead, something red tugged at the black vane.

It inflated, collapsed, and inflated again in the thin charged wake behind the station. A bright cord had wrapped twice around the hinge barrel. Each pull worried the vane against its stops.

Naro fixed her tether short and caught the thing before it could slap away. Cloth, patched in three colors. Two wooden splints crossed beneath it. Hand stitches crowded one edge and wandered wide along another, as if whoever held the needle had grown impatient halfway through. A wet-looking stain had dried white around the knots. The cord was finer than any station braid and stiff with fused grit. Her suit timer showed nine minutes before the radiator controller would reclaim the sector.

She cut the cord, tucked the folded cloth through her belt, and cleared the hinge. The actuator took fresh grease and returned to tolerance on the second cycle. Only after the fault winked out did she look down.

Weather hid most of the world. Cloud towers cast blue shadows over one another, so far below that their motion looked slow. For eight hundred years, station schools had taught the same account: the oceans had poisoned, the land had emptied, and the last life beneath the weather had ended before the oldest ring was closed. Debris went down. Nothing came up.

Naro checked the cloth again. Its wooden splints were newly shaved. One still held a pale curl where the knife had run against the grain. She entered the air lock with six minutes of heat margin and filed the fault as conductive filament fouling.

Under the quarantine hood, the kite smelled of damp mineral dust and a sharp green odor the filters erased within seconds. Naro spread its diamond shape over the steel table. On the inner face, black strokes crossed the yellow panel.

They were not station script. They were older.

Every maintenance apprentice learned enough of the inherited language to survive the unmodernized machinery: WATER RETURN beside a drain, PERSONNEL CLEAR on a pressure door, ABOVE on lifting eyes that had outlasted seven sets of owners. The station used the characters as fixed labels. Nobody ordered breakfast in them. Nobody knew why some verbs changed when a small hooked mark followed them.

Naro recognized four pieces on the kite.

PERSON. ABOVE. FALLING WATER. A hooked mark.

Beneath the line appeared a fifth cluster, repeated smaller at the tail: TELU. It might have meant a launch route, a weather class, a maker's mark. She copied it onto an inspection card and spent an hour searching the maintenance archive. No match.

The first line bothered her more. Its nouns could be assembled into a dozen sensible warnings. PERSON ABOVE RAIN. PERSON: ABOVE RAIN. RAIN ABOVE PERSON. The hook could mean danger, direction, or the sort of question no equipment label had reason to ask.

She should have sent the kite to materials analysis. The quarantine hood had already logged an unclassified organic fiber. Yet the hood purged its temporary scan at shift end unless she opened a contamination case. Naro closed the scan, rolled the kite around its splints, and carried it to the locker beneath her workbench.

Then she went to the disposal ring.

Retired supply capsules waited in a cradle there, white shells mottled by old ascent burns. They had brought sealant, grain culture, medicine, and once a box of brass fasteners so overpacked that the receiving crew still cursed its mass allotment. After enough flights, the shells were stripped, filled with refuse, and sent down established burn lanes. The atmosphere normally consumed them above the lifeless surface.

One capsule stood empty while its cargo scale awaited calibration. Its document recess was no larger than Naro's palm.

She cut the clean margin from a condemned filter sleeve and wrote two words in the inherited characters.

ABOVE FUNCTIONING.

It was an answer only if the hook was a question. It was also nothing she could not defend as a disposal test label.

The kite's snag vector passed through a family of permitted release tracks. Naro chose the next track in that family, advanced the capsule's release by forty-three seconds, and sealed the cloth in the document recess. She changed no burn program. She added less than three grams. When the launcher rolled the shell into darkness, she watched until the planet's white glare swallowed it.

Six days later, a yellow kite quivered against vane fourteen, its line wound around the hinge.

Naro saw it before her wrist found any fault. She made herself grease the hinge first. Only when the work order closed did she ease the glassy thread from the vane with her pliers and carry the cloth inside.

The drawing on it needed no translation. A white capsule lay at the end of a black scar. Flame climbed from one edge of a square roof. Two figures threw water. Beside the picture, the writer had copied the old triangular sign for heat danger.

The words came apart slowly under Naro's finger.

HOT IS NOT ANSWER.

DO NOT DROP HERE.

The smaller TELU cluster had been struck twice beneath the warning, hard enough for the pigment to bleed through the cloth.

Naro sat very still. The disposal model had predicted full ablation. Her timing change had given the empty shell a shallower entry, and the shell, relieved of its normal refuse mass, had skipped instead of burning. Her first message had reached a roof hot enough to char it.

The kite carried a second drawing, cramped along the blue hem. The black scar appeared again with an arrow following its long axis. Twelve copies of the scar, end to end, led to a crosshatched circle. Three pale boulders made a triangle beside it. The roofs and planted plots lay in the opposite direction, cut off by the cloth's edge. No compass. No numbers that could become coordinates. Only a place described from the damage Naro had already caused.

At the bottom, one final sentence:

ANSWER CAN FALL THERE. SLOW.

The disposal cradle opened for its next cycle in thirty-two hours. Naro spent eighteen of them making sure nothing like her first answer fell again.

She chose another retired capsule and removed everything not needed to hold its shape: latch motor, inner web, pressure liner. From a heat-cured packing sheet she cut a four-lobed drogue. She stitched its straps through the capsule's old cargo anchors, then loaded the shell with ceramic scraps and fired it down the maintenance drop shaft. The first fold streamed without opening. The second snapped open too hard and tore one strap. On the fourth test, the drogue filled before the red band and lowered the shell into the catch net with enough force to bruise, not kill.

The landing place was the harder problem. Naro pulled the first capsule's recorded path into a downward service optic. Clouds erased the surface for hours at a time. During a break she found a thin black dash and, at its end, a roof with one darkened corner. She aligned the dash with the logged direction of travel. Twelve dash-lengths farther on, three pale points formed an unmistakable triangle around bare ground.

The sender had given her bearing and scale but kept the settlement itself off the map.

Good, Naro thought. She did not want to know more than she had been offered.

She drew the drogue over the safe circle. On the other half of the filter cloth she sketched the kite below a storm cloud and the station above it. Her labels read KITE / RISE / HOW. She copied the order from warning plates because it was the only order she trusted.

Before sealing the capsule, she wrote WARM LANDING EXPECTED, crossed out WARM, and replaced it with SLOW. Heat was her category. Speed was the danger the sender had named.

The release put the shell four seconds late along the original track. Through the service optic, Naro saw it flare, vanish into cloud, then reappear as a pale speck beneath its open drogue. It drifted toward the crosshatched ground beside the three stones. The roofs remained untouched at the edge of the image.

For nine days there was no kite.

Naro slept badly and became severe about other people's tool returns. On the tenth day, during a heavy radiator dump, green cloth fluttered from the service rail itself. Its thread had parted at a release knot before reaching the vane.

Three pictures filled the panels. In the first, a small figure stood in slanting rain and paid out shining line from a spool. In the second, the line climbed into a cloud forked with white marks. The figure had tied it to a stake and moved well away. In the third, the station passed above as a bright stroke. The kite sprang toward the charged wake; the top knot opened, leaving the dangerous length of line behind.

The mechanism was incomplete, but the limits were plain. Only the season's strongest storm columns raised the wet conductive thread high enough. Only a radiator dump drew the released sail farther. Ground and station had made a temporary ladder neither side had intended.

Beneath the pictures, the writer had rewritten Naro's question.

HOW DOES KITE RISE?

The hooked mark was a question after all.

Below the correction came the report Naro had been waiting for.

CAPSULE CAME TO STONES. CAPSULE WAS SLOW. NO ROOF BURNED.

Then, squeezed into the last corner:

WHY ABOVE DOES NOT KNOW KITE?

Naro turned the cloth over as though an easier question might be hidden on the back. She could draw the radiator wake. She could draw a station sealed against weather. She could not explain eight hundred years of certainty with the vocabulary of drain labels and lift warnings.

Her answer was a picture of vane fourteen shedding wavy lines of heat. Beside it she wrote ABOVE LEARNS KITE.

It was the first message she sent in the tested light shell. The capsule floated down beneath its drogue and vanished beside the three stones.

***

The return track crossed the radiator sector five more times while storms walked beneath the station. Naro measured the season by scraps of cloth.

The messages arrived on patched diamonds, narrow triangles, and once a crooked square that spun so violently it scuffed blue paint from the rail. Naro found leaf print, flour paste, soot, and the pressed track of a small thumb. No two knots were alike. Every kite had been made since the last answer.

Naro drew the dense round served during radiator-purge week. Charge leaking through the nearby maintenance trunks spoiled yeast cultures, so crews in that corridor ate flat, mineral-tasting bread while the inner ring sold risen loaves at outrageous barter. The writer labeled Naro's circle MOON and added rays.

Naro returned a drawing of teeth biting it, with FOOD beneath.

The next kite showed two circles: the moon over a wet roof and a flatbread browning on a stone. The writer had added, MOON NOT FOR TEETH. FOOD NOT IN SKY. The second sentence ended in three heavy strokes Naro decided were laughter, though no maintenance archive supported her.

The writer drew seedlings flattened by rain and standing again days later. Naro drew gloved hands changing a valve while the planet filled the visor behind them. The next question asked whether the people above ever got wet. Naro replied with a sealed hull and the phrase WATER MUST STAY INSIDE. A roof patched in five colors and a jar catching a leak came back.

The questions crowded one another. HOW MANY PEOPLE ABOVE? DO YOU HAVE NIGHT? WHY DOES HOT FALL FROM SKY? Naro answered the middle question with a diagram of the station entering shadow. She answered the third with NO MORE HOT. She left the first untouched.

Naro never asked how many people lived below. She did not request a soil sample, a seed, a blood spot, or an absolute position. Once she drew the curve of a continent from an orbital chart and set a question hook over it. Then she remembered the settlement deliberately omitted from the landing map and burned the cloth in the service furnace.

Her messages remained serviceable and bloodless. BELOW MESSAGE RECEIVED. DESCENT CORRECTED. ABOVE WATER RECIRCULATES. The writer replied to whatever fact survived the grammar and ignored whatever did not. On one orange hem came a complaint: ABOVE USES MANY WORDS TO SAY LITTLE.

Naro kept that sentence pinned above her bench.

The capsule work acquired a trace. Three retired capsules had left the cradle lighter than their approved disposal masses. Packing membrane had disappeared from reclamation. An automatic reconciliation notice entered Naro's queue, low priority and patient. She could restore every discrepancy with spare material, but then the next drogue would have no cloth. Instead she acknowledged the notice and gave herself ten days to account for the grams. She told herself the season would end first.

***

The next kite arrived rimed with ice and barely intact. Naro caught it against her suit before a torn panel could peel away. Inside, she found a person drawn beside the patched roof: narrow knees below a short tunic, dark hair in uneven spikes, one hand closed around a spool. The figure reached only halfway up the door beside it. A child, unless the drawing abandoned the careful scale used in every landing map.

An arrow pointed from the figure to the cluster Naro had catalogued as a route marker.

TELU.

Above the arrow, copied from some surviving piece of old equipment on the surface, were the characters PERSONNEL NAME.

Telu was not a corridor through the storms. Telu was the maker who pulled bad stitches tight, scolded hot capsules, and thought Naro's bread was a moon.

A second drawing showed a suited figure on a radiator rail. The box beside that figure was empty. From it, an arrow led to the same label: PERSONNEL NAME.

Naro carried the kite to her bench. She took the first red one from its sealed drawer and spread the two side by side. The joining strokes Telu had corrected through a season of questions now had functions. One placed the person above the rain. One turned the statement toward a listener. The hook did not merely mark uncertainty; it asked someone to answer.

At last she could read the first line in its order.

IS THERE A PERSON ABOVE THE RAIN?

Under the empty name box on the new kite, Telu had written:

ABOVE FUNCTIONING IS NOT PERSON.

WHO HEARS TELU?

The forecast allowed one more safe descent before the storm corridor slipped beyond vane fourteen. Naro had a stripped shell, a tested drogue, and four grams of message allowance. She also had nine days left on the inventory notice. A signed message would not prove the surface alive to anyone above, but if the altered capsules were examined, it could prove who had sent them.

She wrote ABOVE PERSONNEL PRESENT.

The sentence fit neatly. It answered without opening anything.

Naro tore it in half.

On fresh cloth, she copied the person marker. Telu's corrections had shown her how to bend it toward the speaker. For a verb, she borrowed HEAR from an alarm plate, where it meant that a person had received a sound and now owed a response.

PERSON ABOVE. NAME NARO. NARO HEARS TELU.

She drew herself badly: broad shoulders inside the suit, one boot under the rail, vane fourteen at her back. The empty name box became NARO.

The message and drawing weighed six grams. Naro trimmed the border. Five. She scraped pigment from the broadest letters and removed the unused half of the filter cloth. Four.

At the disposal cradle, she ran the drogue inspection twice. The straps were sound. The shell's landing track ended beside the three pale stones. She did not add a gift, an instrument, or a promise. All Telu had requested was an answer.

Naro released the capsule.

It turned blunt end forward, struck atmosphere, and burned briefly against the cloud. Then the drogue flowered. The shell descended out of the optic's sight, slow and on course.

***

The storm charge collapsed two days later. The station trimmed its orbit, and the safe return track began sliding away from the radiator sector.

Naro completed an exterior inspection with no cloth on the vanes. On the next pass, there was nothing again. The reconciliation notice changed from low priority to routine. She opened its form, entered RECLAMATION VARIANCE UNDER REVIEW, and left the explanation blank.

During the last heat dump of the week, a pale scrap flickered past her helmet. Naro lunged. Her glove closed on one reed spar. The little kite spun at the end of her arm while its freed thread floated away behind the station. It was no bigger than her two hands together, made with a single layer of cloth and a tail scarcely wider than string. Telu had pared it to the least mass the dying lift would carry.

Inside, the first panel held no drawing.

TELU HEARS NARO.

NARO IS PERSON ABOVE RAIN.

The second panel showed rain pouring from a roof into an open jar. Beside it, Telu had copied Naro's earlier water loop: cup, drain, pipe, cup. A new question followed the arrows around the panel's edge.

HOW DOES NARO DRINK WHEN RAIN CANNOT FALL INSIDE?

The capsule corridor was already beyond the three stones. No release now could reach them, and the storm charge under vane fourteen had fallen below every useful forecast. Naro closed the service ticket, stowed the little kite with the others, and returned to her bench.

The inventory form waited on one screen. On another lay a condemned condensation diagram from a galley service manual. It showed vapor touching a cold pipe, gathering into drops, and running back toward a reservoir.

Naro put the diagram on the scale. Seven grams.

She took up her knife and began with the margins.
