# Opus review: The Other Twin

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the voices are distinct and most exchanges work, but the bus and concert-fight beats close on the sister's epigrams, the clinician answers the central question with a policy sentence, and the small exchanges fall back on correction-and-topper routines.
- Prose: REVISE — the bodily prose is concrete and strong, but the retrospective narrator sums up the lesson at the end of most scenes.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is delivered and resolved within the length band; only minor wording slips about who controls which part of the body.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree. It missed the epigram closes, the clinician's thesis answer and the brother's self-diagnosis in the recording.

This dialogue is better than the collection norm. The brother's forecasting jargon ("Unpredictability of final traits," "lower visibility, no restricted shoulders") belongs to him, the sister's best lines are short bodily needs and refusals ("It hurts now," "They stare now," "No," "Don't finish it"), and the mother stays brief and never takes sides. The failures come at the pressure points. There the sister gets the author's epigrams and borrows her brother's accounting metaphors (L93, L225–L229), so she wins by out-phrasing him instead of refusing his frame. The clinician answers the story's most exposed question with a policy sentence (L201), and the brother states his own lesson in his recording (L309). Around these, several small exchanges run as volleys of corrections closed by a neat last word, and the narration then explains points the scene has already made. Most fixes are cuts: remove the toppers and summaries, give the sister plain, specific complaints, and let the clinician say no.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Bus-shelter "girls" exchange is a logic trap with no clear motive

- Where: L59–L71, bus shelter just before the fall
- Text:
> “He wasn’t talking to you.”
> “He said girls.”
> “Exactly.”
> She was still looking back at the man when our left foot met the step
- Problem: Punchline Patch with an unclear stance. "He said girls" either means "plural, so he included me," which has a boy who is proud of his stubble claiming a place among "girls" with no reaction, or it is a wry "I noticed." Either way the line exists to hand the sister a debate-winning "Exactly." The reader leaves the exchange not knowing what she wants: to answer the man herself, to object to the misgendering, or to win a point. So the next beat, her "still looking back at the man" that causes the fall, has no clear motive. The intended pattern, that he answers for both of them and her late reply coincides with the misstep, is buried under the riddle.
- Fix: Keep "Morning, girls.", the narrator's "Morning," and "He wasn't talking to you." Cut L67–L69. In their place, put an action that makes her grievance plain and causes the fall: she turns back to answer the man herself while he is already stepping toward the bus. If the story wants his reaction to being called a girl, give it one clause of narration, not a counter-argument. Illustrative only: She turned back and said her own "Morning" to the man, and I was already moving.

### D2 · Major · Aphorism on the bus that the narration then has to decode

- Where: L83–L95, back of the bus after the fall
- Text:
> “The question can get here before your answer once in a while.”
> I did not yet understand that answering for both of us was also a kind of movement.
- Problem: The sister is bruised and embarrassed, yet she closes a four-line back-and-forth with a polished epigram that plays on his "The bus was here." "The question" points at nothing stable: nobody asked anything before the step, and the driver's "You okay?" came after the fall. The reader can decode it only through L95's thesis sentence, which then explains the scene a second time (Philosopher; Ground/referent; narration glossing the line).
- Fix: Keep L85–L91 ("I wasn't trying to trip us." / "I know." / "You moved." / "The bus was here."), which show his habit of blaming outside causes. Replace the L93 line with her second, specific grievance, the "We're fine" he said over her "I'm not," in her plain register, and keep her pulling the jacket closed over the green shirt. Cut the last sentence of L95; "I did not include myself." can stay. Illustrative only: "You told her we were fine."

### D3 · Major · The clinician answers the thesis, not the girl

- Where: L197–L203, first consultation, the sister's question
- Text:
> “If we pause it, I don’t stop being a girl?”
> “The medication would act on your shared body. I’m not treating either of your identities.”
- Problem: This is the story's most exposed question. A frightened fourteen-year-old asks it after a long silence, and the reply is a policy statement addressed to both twins ("either of your identities") that never actually says no. It states the story's correct thesis in clinic language, which is exactly the doctor-delivers-the-thesis default the outline warned against, and it turns the moment into procedure (Philosopher). The body-level fact is already on the page at L187 ("It would not assign one set of effects to my sister and another to me").
- Fix: Keep the question word for word, and keep "The clinician's gaze stayed on her." Replace the spoken answer with a short, direct answer to her alone: a plain no, and at most one specific clause about what the medicine acts on (the hair, voice and chest she has just listed). Do not mention "identities" or add a balancing sentence. The answer must still leave her girlhood untouched by the treatment. Illustrative only: "No. It slows what's changing. You'd still be a girl."

### D4 · Major · Climactic fight: a feed line and a composed thesis from the angry twin

- Where: L223–L231, bedroom before the winter concert
- Text:
> “You’ve spent me again.”
> “What does that mean?”
> “My shirt. My chest. My nine minutes. They all disappear inside your total.”
- Problem: At the emotional peak the sister produces an opaque money metaphor, the brother asks the question that lets her explain, and she answers with a three-part list capped by an aphorism. Mid-fight she suddenly becomes the more fluent debater, arguing in his accounting terms. The outline's plan was for her to refuse his frame, not match it with a speech, and this does the reverse (Punchline Patch setup; uniform fluency under pressure; Philosopher).
- Fix: Cut "You've spent me again.", "What does that mean?" and the sentence "They all disappear inside your total." Let her go straight from his nine-minute plan to a rougher claim that throws his words back at him. "My shirt. My chest. My nine minutes." can stay, broken up or repeated if that helps, because his "There is no version where either of us gets everything" answers a claim of ownership directly. Leave everything from "There is no version" through her "No." unchanged.

### D5 · Major · The brother's recording states his own lesson

- Where: L307–L309, the narrator's recording
- Text:
> I keep saying later will be easier to decide, but I’m the one asking for forecasts while everything keeps happening.
- Problem: The recordings are the story's decisive communication, and the rest of his is specific and good ("I like shaving, which is stupid because I've cut my chin twice"). This sentence is his arc summarized by the person who lived it, spoken to a clinician who needs his wish, not his self-diagnosis. L307's "crossed them out aloud" already shows the change (Philosopher at the decisive exchange).
- Fix: Cut the sentence. If he needs a bridge before "I want a pause too.", add one more specific like or dislike in the same plain list form. Keep "I want a pause too. Not forever. Now."

### D6 · Major · Correction-and-topper routine in the small exchanges

- Where: L121–L129 (specialist appointment), L145–L153 (clinic forms), L319–L323 (February sink); see also L47–L51
- Text:
> “That’s not a kind.”
> “It’s the kind we have Thursday.”
> “You make us sound like a building proposal.”
> “You wrote speed.”
> “It’s one too many.”
- Problem: Together with D1, D2 and D4, these make the story's default rhythm a volley of precise corrections closed by a neat last word (Tennis Match; correction ping-pong). The specialist exchange is inert: the sister asks a real, frightened question and gets a quip, then her correction of the quip, then a topper, and nothing changes. The forms exchange is five equal jabs, and "building proposal" does not fit "final traits." In the final scene, "You've got one." exists only to set up the stock comeback "one too many." Each of these also pushes the sister into her brother's pedantic register ("That's not a kind").
- Fix: Specialist: keep the mother's first answer, which carries information (scans already sent, more needed), and cut L127–L129, or have the mother admit she doesn't know the word either. Forms: keep "Final traits." and "It asks what troubles me.", and cut L149–L153 so the exchange ends on his defensive answer. "Speed" stays on her form and pays off at L267–L271. Final sink: cut "It's one too many." and let her pluck the hair without explaining. Keep the "Bus in six minutes." / "Five," pair at L47–L49, because the ending calls back to it. Once the other toppers are gone, the mother's "Then you'd better not spend one correcting me." can stay as the one family joke.

### D7 · Minor · Two lines that don't fit the action

- Where: L107–L109, first-period morning; L159–L161, first consultation
- Text:
> “Bathroom first,” she said.
> “You can go first when I ask you.”
- Problem: "Bathroom first" is said to twins already standing at the bathroom sink, so it reads as a place to go; the mother means the toilet and the pad. "You can go first when I ask you" is followed at once by her asking the sister first, so the first reading contradicts the very next action (Ground: the line doesn't fit the action).
- Fix: Have the mother name what she actually means (toilet, cleaning up). Give the clinician a plain line about taking turns. Illustrative only: "I'll ask you on your own."

## Prose findings

### P1 · Major · Narration sums up the lesson after the scene has shown it

- Where: L185 (clinic), L273 (bedroom after the fight), L301 (recordings), L313–L315 (after the treatment starts); L95 is covered in D2
- Text:
> it was the first useful thing I said in the room
> Neither picture belonged to her. Neither belonged only to me.
> Privacy was impossible, but authorship was not.
> But the mornings stopped feeling like a race against a body that had started without us.
- Problem: The retrospective narrator keeps closing a scene by naming what it meant, usually as a balanced antithesis. L185 explains the clinician's repeated question after the reader has watched it work. L301 explains the separate recordings before we hear them. L315 announces the ending's mood before the final sink scene shows it. Combined with D2–D5, the story states its point in both dialogue and narration at almost every turn.
- Fix: Cut the second clause of L185 (keep "That answer looked very small beside all the ones I had brought."). Cut "Privacy was impossible, but authorship was not." Cut L315, or replace it with one specific morning detail. In L273, keep the two pictures and cut the closing antithesis or restate it plainly. Keep L33 ("That was how I decided we had been fair.") and L35; they set up irony rather than explain it. Optional: L313 can open directly on its specific list instead of "The treatment was not a clean door shutting."

### P2 · Minor · Staging and clarity slips

- Where: L37–L45 (early-bus morning), L59 (bus shelter), L145 (clinic forms), L207 (choosing concert clothes)
- Text:
> Our mother, kneeling by the front door with one of our shoes between her knees, pulled the laces tight.
> a man in a reflective vest held the gate for us
> She read over my shoulder.
> Neither was formal enough for her and both were too noticeable for me.
- Problem: The early-bus scene jumps from the mirror to the front door with no transition, and it's unclear whether the shoe is on their foot. Bus shelters don't have gates. "Read over my shoulder" assumes two separate bodies in a story that is otherwise exact about the shared one. L207's summary contradicts the choices it follows (he picked charcoal to be less noticeable; she picked red), which muddles the setup of the climactic fight.
- Fix: Add a short location beat before the mother appears and make clear she is lacing the shoe on their foot. Change "gate" to a door, or move the gate somewhere plausible such as the school gate. Replace "over my shoulder" with a sideways look. Cut L207's last sentence, or make it say that both outfits were compromises.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Wording slips about who controls which part of the body

- Where: L101, first-period morning; L331, February sink
- Text:
> My sister braced both hands on the porcelain.
> She held our chin steady while I shaved.
- Problem: The story establishes that the sister runs the left hand and the narrator the right (L21, L215, L221), so she cannot brace "both hands" unless he gives her his, and the text doesn't say he does. The twins have separate faces and chins (L15 "my own chin," L113 "one face each"), so "our chin" is wrong.
- Fix: Change to "We braced both hands", or have her brace the left while he puts the right beside it. Change "our chin" to "my chin".
