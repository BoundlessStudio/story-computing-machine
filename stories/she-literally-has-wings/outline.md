## Story

- Central promise / premise: In a casually fantastical city, an unnamed husband learns during his first-anniversary gathering that the winged woman he married is not the bird demi-human or hybrid he assumed but an angel. The comic impossibility of his mistake opens a sincere marital question: can valuing someone beyond a label excuse never asking what that label means to her?
- Form and tone: First-person past from the husband's perspective; warm contemporary-fantasy romantic comedy with grounded emotional stakes, aimed at 2,500–4,000 words.
- Character want and pressure: The husband wants to preserve the easy, person-first love he and his wife share, but his best friend's disbelief and the wife's hurt force him to admit that incuriosity can also make a partner feel unseen. The wife wants to be loved as a person rather than treated as a miracle dispenser, yet must decide whether safety from other people's projections has become concealment inside her marriage.
- Turn: When party guests overhear that she is an angel and begin pressing her for blessings and impossible favors, the husband finally sees the burden behind her silence. She states her limits for herself; he supports those limits, admits his assumption without turning it into a defense, and asks what she actually wants him to understand.
- Ending intent: They do not erase the communication failure or solve angelhood in one talk. They choose honest curiosity with consent: she will share her history on her terms, and he will ask rather than classify. His anniversary toast celebrates both her extraordinary nature and the personality he married; her unguarded smile provides the tender comic answer to the prompt's claim that she makes a room smile.

## Beats

1. In the couple's unnamed apartment, the husband and his best friend prepare a small first-anniversary gathering while the wife carries chairs, welcomes early guests, and navigates the room with her large folded wings. Her attentive warmth lifts each interaction; the husband reads every smile as the ordinary effect of her kindness.
2. The wife steps into the hall to help a neighbor. Staring after her, the friend delivers the prompt's exchange nearly verbatim: "Bro, how did you not know your wife is an angel? She literally has wings." The husband explains his bird demi-human/hybrid assumption, and when the friend points out that everyone smiles as soon as she enters, he credits the amazing personality that made him marry her.
3. The friend tests that explanation against clues the husband normalized while courting her: immaculate wings unlike any single bird's, light gathering around her when she is moved, strangers confessing troubles to her, and her careful refusal to promise miracles. The husband grows defensive because he hears the teasing as an accusation that his marriage is foolish rather than evidence that he never asked a basic question.
4. The wife returns in time to hear his categorical guess. Her smile vanishes. In a private kitchen conversation, she identifies herself plainly as an angel and asks why he was certain enough to assume but never curious enough to ask. He initially says her nature never changed how he loved her, then realizes that this answers his intention rather than her hurt.
5. She explains that people who learn the label often stop seeing a woman: they solicit blessings, confess without permission, demand proof, or expect flawless goodness. He was the first person who cared about her preferences and boundaries before her capabilities, so she let his mistaken assumption stand; she admits that relief does not make her silence fair within their marriage. Both own a failure without collapsing it into equal blame or instant absolution.
6. The gathering interrupts before they can finish. The friend has not spread the news, but one guest overhears the word "angel" and recognition ripples. Lighthearted requests for luck turn into a desperate plea concerning an ill relative. The wife freezes under the familiar expectation, wings drawn tight, demonstrating why the label carried pressure rather than supplying an action spectacle.
7. The wife says what she can and cannot offer: she is neither omnipotent nor a wish-granter, and any warmth people feel around her cannot manufacture happiness or erase grief. The husband waits for her lead, then ends the growing queue, protects the celebration from further demands, and redirects the guests toward practical care for the person who asked. His action proves he can know what she is without treating her as a function.
8. In an honest anniversary toast, the husband apologizes for mistaking nonjudgment for understanding. He says he married her for her personality and wants to know the rest of her as she chooses to share it. She accepts the direction rather than granting a frictionless pardon, and invites him to begin with one real question after the guests leave.
9. Alone amid the dishes, he asks instead of guessing. She gives one modest, personal answer about her wings or the room-warming gift, saving wider origins and cosmology for later. Her genuine smile draws an answering smile from him; the best friend calls a final amused "finally" from the hallway, landing the comedy without undoing the couple's earned intimacy.
