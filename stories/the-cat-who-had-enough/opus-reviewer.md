# Opus review: Nine Lives of Excuses

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the lecture's central accusation contradicts itself, the complaint-trigger rule is applied inconsistently, and both the snap and the turning point play as composed legal set pieces.
- Prose: PASS — concrete and controlled; only a "not X but Y" habit, a closing gloss, and a muddled near-signing beat.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the promise is delivered (inherited advice cat, long lecture, "waste", "source of your own problems") and all money and times reconcile; one invoice thread blurs and there is one policy nit.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree — it missed the self-contradiction at L55 and the inconsistent trigger rule.

The dialogue problem sits in Mizzle's scenes; the rest of the cast mostly works. Her voice is a real choice (formal, no contractions, evidence gathered from cat height) and should stay, but the prompt's "snap" arrives as an orderly case file, its key paragraph (L55) contradicts itself, and the sparring after it runs on a trigger rule that the narration enforces with ad hoc labels the story itself undercuts. At the turn (L107–L109) narrator and cat trade mirror-built anaphora, so the exchange that sends the narrator to the sister reads as a formal ruling. The storefront scene is the strongest dialogue in the story: the narrator's hedge breaks on "Yes or no?", the sister talks in figures and deadlines and lets actions carry the hurt, and the text-message ending is earned. The edit pass should make the lecture coherent and audibly frustrated, make the rule predictable, loosen the two set pieces, and otherwise leave the sister scene nearly untouched.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt (rewrite section controls; craft profile prospective-2026-08-21): the cat is inherited (keep "since I inherited her", L13) and advises when complained to; one day she snaps into a long, frustrated lecture that calls the narrator a waste and names them the source of their own problems. The lecture must stay long and must keep both charges. Target length 2,500–4,000 words; the prose is about 3,080 now, so lecture revisions should be roughly length-neutral.
- The trigger rule as stated at L23 is the story's fixed mechanic: a sincere complaint, spoken directly to her, buys one reply of any length; questions, praise and fake grievances get silence. Repairs should conform to that rule, not add new categories.

The story's modern-like setting is unpinned, which is consistent; do not add real-world places, brands, or dates that would put a talking cat in our time. Mizzle's origin is deliberately unexplained; do not add lore. No names besides Mizzle; no collision found (the other speaking cat in the collection, Murrant, is distinct).

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Blocking · Lecture's core accusation contradicts itself

- Where: L55, the lecture's "You collect advice" paragraph
- Text:
> You have asked me whether to sign this guarantee four times without complaining, because you know questions do not compel me.
> hoping I will answer the question you are too proud to ask and too frightened to answer.
- Problem: Ground/referent and internal contradiction. "The question" can only point back to "whether to sign", which Mizzle has just said the narrator asked four times, so "too proud to ask" contradicts the sentence before it. The paragraph also gives two incompatible motives: the narrator asks in a form they know won't be answered (to avoid an answer), then complains in order to get one. At the moment Mizzle names the narrator's mechanism, which is the prompt's "source of your own problems" charge, neither the narrator nor the reader can tell what exactly she is accusing them of.
- Fix: Make it one causal chain: questions got silence, so this morning the narrator disguised the same question as a complaint about the sister to force an answer that would then be hers to own. Drop "too proud to ask… too frightened to answer"; if a second charge is wanted, name the thing the narrator has actually not said (that they no longer want the bakery) in plain words. Illustrative only: "Four times you asked me whether to sign, and four times I said nothing, because a question does not open my mouth. So this morning you complained about your sister instead, to make me answer, so the answer would be mine."

### D2 · Major · Complaint rule applied inconsistently after the lecture

- Where: L67–L81 and L107–L109, kitchen after the lecture
- Text:
> "That was vicious," I said.
> Mizzle's whiskers angled forward, but a statement was not a complaint. She said nothing.
> "I do not decide. You do. That is why your voice changes when you mean it."
> Silence. Another statement.
- Problem: L23 defines the trigger as a sincere complaint spoken directly to her. "That was vicious" is sincere (the narrator is gripping the mug hard enough to ache) and addressed to her, yet the narration rules it a "statement". L81 then makes sincerity of voice the test, and L107, three flat declaratives addressed to her ("You don't know…"), does get an answer. The reader cannot predict when Mizzle may speak, and that mechanic drives the whole scene. The narration also referees almost every silence ("Another statement." / "Question. Silence." / "I had given her an order, not a complaint."), so the sparring reads as a rules procedure.
- Fix: Use only the L23 criteria. Simplest: have the narrator say "That was vicious" to the mug or the table instead of to her, so her silence follows from "spoken directly to her"; or let her answer it in one short line. Remove the ad hoc "statement" category at L69 and L77 and let those silences stand without a label. Keep at most two labels (the L97 question and the L113 order both fit L23 and are brisk).

### D3 · Major · The snap is delivered as a composed case file

- Where: L41–L61, Mizzle's lecture
- Text:
> I know the order. I slept on the first packet for three days.
> If the advice works, you were clever to ask. If it fails, you were misled.
> Your sister's messages became sharper because you ignored the soft ones.
> it will be because you used the safe days without answering and made everyone ask again.
- Problem: The prompt asks for a cat that "snaps" into a "long frustrated lecture". The content is strong and cat-specific, but the structure is a prosecutor's brief: chronological exhibits ("I know the order"), balanced summations, a thesis paragraph, a verdict paragraph, a caveat, and a closing line that restates L57's "used the safe days". The frustration is stated ("Then you ask why I sound impatient") but never heard: nothing in the rhythm slips, repeats or boils over. Mizzle ends up in the collection's composed legal register at the one moment the prompt asks her to lose composure.
- Fix: Keep the length, the no-contraction formality and every concrete exhibit. Let her own grievance (the dish, being dependent on the narrator, hearing the same complaint for ten months) break into the chronology early, instead of waiting as a tidy late paragraph. Let one run of exhibits speed up or repeat out of exasperation. Optionally, let the narrator try to cut in once and be talked over. Cut "I know the order", the L55 If/If/If triplet, and one of the two "safe days" lines, and end on something hotter than a summary. Do not replace the cut summations with new aphorisms.

### D4 · Major · "You don't know / I know" ledger at the turn

- Where: L107–L109, kitchen, after the confession
- Text:
> "You don't know what my sister can afford. You don't know why I stopped wanting it. You don't know whether I'll regret it."
> "No. I know what I saw and what you told me.
> I do not know her accounts. I do not know whether the shop will succeed. I do not know what you will want next year. I know she does not know what you want today."
- Problem: Tennis Match / ruling, with Identical Voices at the turn. The narrator's triplet and Mizzle's eight-sentence reply are mirror-built anaphora, so the exchange that sends the narrator to the sister plays as a formal knowledge audit. The narrator hedges, rationalizes and gets petty everywhere else; here they suddenly match Mizzle's parallel cadence. Mizzle answers a point nobody raised ("whether the shop will succeed"), skips the one the narrator did raise ("why I stopped wanting it"), and closes on a neat last word.
- Fix: Let the narrator protest in their own mode (one objection, an exception, a hypothetical, or a sentence that trails off) instead of a triplet. Cut Mizzle's reply to what she needs to say: one or two things she saw, and that the sister does not know what the narrator wants. Drop the opening "No." and the "I know / I do not know" symmetry; her limited knowledge can survive in one plain sentence.

### D5 · Minor · Referent and staging slips in Mizzle's lines

- Where: L41, L43, L47, L59, L61 (lecture); L73 (her reply about the pipe)
- Text:
> lost the key in a bowl
> Six weeks ago you brought home those keys
> call the tenant adviser
> When your sister found the adviser
> And yes, you are a waste.
> do not stand over a paper you requested
> and the paper under my body.
- Problem: "The key in a bowl" and "those keys" point at shop keys the reader has not seen (the key bowl first appears at L115), and a corner has no key, so the opening figure only half-lands. "The tenant adviser" is named as already known, yet the sister then has to find one. "And yes" answers a question nobody asked. The narrator did not request the guarantee; the landlord did. At L73 the guarantee is no longer under her, because she stepped off it at L39.
- Fix: Plant the key bowl and shop keys before the lecture (near the L27 sugar-bowl beat), or keep the corner figure self-contained. Change "those keys" to "the shop keys" and "the tenant adviser" to "a tenant adviser". Drop "And yes," or give it a trigger (a flinch, an attempted interruption from D3). Replace "a paper you requested" with something the narrator did set in motion (the shop they talked the sister into), and "under my body" with "I was lying on".

### D6 · Minor · "No, you know now." correction at the decisive exchange

- Where: L171–L175, the storefront
- Text:
> "I know."
> "No, you know now. Three weeks ago, you let me keep spending."
- Problem: This is the only house-register word correction in an otherwise strong scene, and it exists to land a last word. As phrased it also disputes the narrator's confession at L157 ("I knew enough then to tell you"), so the narrator could hear it as being called a liar, which is not her charge; her real charge is the second sentence.
- Fix: Cut "No, you know now." and let "Three weeks ago, you let me keep spending." answer "I know." directly, or have her not acknowledge "I know" at all.

### D7 · Minor · Narration explains the sister's short answers

- Where: L179–L183 and L205–L209, the storefront
- Text:
> I had prepared answers for anger and for forgiveness. She gave me neither.
> That answer did not require interpretation.
- Problem: Both lines explain a reply that has already landed ("All right." / "No."), taking the inference away from the reader at the scene's two hardest beats and giving the narrator the last word.
- Fix: Cut L209. Cut L183 or keep only its first sentence; the phone call that follows already shows what she gave instead.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · "Not X but Y" reversals as a defining habit

- Where: L13, L31, L99, L129, L185 (narration)
- Text:
> This was not sabotage.
> felt not fear but absence. Nothing. No warm leap at the room becoming ours. Only the wish to go home.
> not softened exactly, but narrowed to dates and square footage
- Problem: The reversal is the narration's default way of defining things (also L31 "but not in that accusatory way" and L99 "not because she thought… but because…"). At L129, the story's key private admission, it is compounded by a fragment triplet.
- Fix: Keep L129's "not fear but absence" and merge the three fragments after it into one sentence; state the others positively.

### P2 · Minor · Closing gloss after the last line of dialogue

- Where: L227–L237, ending
- Text:
> Not a complaint. Not quite.
- Problem: The ledger (L223) and "I did not complain to her." (L227) already show the change. The final fragment pair explains the last spoken line and adds a small closing emblem; the forearm image and the left-handed mouse carry the ending without it.
- Fix: Cut L235. Keep L223's ledger, which answers the "waste" charge through the narrator's own action, and keep L227.

### P3 · Minor · Near-signing sequence does not track

- Where: L119–L125, apartment after Mizzle leaves
- Text:
> It would prove Mizzle wrong about me wasting advice.
> I tore the signed corner off rather than pretending the mark had been accidental. I printed a new page.
- Problem: Mizzle refused to advise the narrator about signing, so signing cannot disprove "wasting advice"; the intended point is proving her wrong about the narrator. Tearing the mark off is not clearly the honest alternative to "pretending the mark had been accidental", and the reader has to guess that the fresh page is for handing back a clean, unsigned guarantee.
- Fix: Point L119 at what she actually charged (that the narrator wastes things, or only waits). Give L125 a plain reason (the narrator doesn't want a half-signed page in the sister's hands) and say why they print the fresh copy.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Invoice threads blur

- Where: L53, L75, L215, L229
- Text:
> The invoice got paid.
> if the overdue design invoice clears this week
> the client whose invoice had been late because I had sent it late
- Problem: There are at least two invoices (the one found in drafts, now paid, and an overdue one the repayment plan depends on) and a menu client whose layouts are late. Readers cannot tell whether the "overdue design invoice" belongs to that same client, whose work is not yet delivered; if it does, the Friday schedule looks impossible.
- Fix: At L215 or L229, say in a few words whose invoice is overdue (a different client, or a job already delivered).

### C2 · Minor · Incidental plumbing texture (policy)

- Where: L63 and L155; story-bearing uses at L53, L71–L73, L223
- Text:
> Somewhere in the building, a pipe knocked twice.
> no refrigerator or pipe
- Problem: The style guide's "No default water infrastructure" entry (CANON) counts passing references. The leaking sink pipe is domestic plumbing that carries weight (Mizzle's evidence, the narrator's rebuttal, the ledger), so it is arguably outside the rule and should stay. The knocking building pipe is ambience only, and L155 echoes it only to recall it.
- Fix: Optional in an authorized revision: replace the L63 knock with another building sound and adjust L155. Leave the sink leak alone.
