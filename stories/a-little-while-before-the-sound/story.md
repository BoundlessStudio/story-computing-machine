# A Little While Before the Sound

## 1

This morning Mieke’s audition is still on my phone. I’ve watched the first twenty seconds quite a few times. She comes over the blue line with her eyes down, the way she comes up a driveway when she’s sure the dog next door has got loose, and then she lifts her head and by God there she is. You should try getting a look like that from my sister when there’s only you in the room. Yesterday I watched it and told her I could see the green exit sign reflected in the boards.

I’d let us in after closing at Hale Arena. I had my work keys in my hand when I told her she could have another twenty minutes. I like telling Mieke what I can do with those keys. She took her skate guards off and gave them to me to hold while she tightened a lace. My sister had talked to a woman from the ice show three days before and waited until I had a pink skate guard in each hand to tell me about it.

“She asked what part I wanted,” Mieke said. “I told her I liked the cat. It turns out there are four people in that cat. When it jumps, there’s a whole person in the tail who has to know about it.”

“Show her you have a good back edge,” I said. “She can do whatever she likes with your tail once she gives you a job.”

The emergency signal went off in my jacket. It was a nasty sound in a little car when you were trying to get home. In an empty rink it got all the way up into the rafters. I put the guards in my pocket. It seemed to me we’d only just had a whole week of those alerts, though what I remembered was lying in bed going over the name of a town I’d never been to and trying to work out which side of the border it was on.

“An Amber alert,” I told her. “I’ll look when we’re done.”

## 2

Jory marked her place at the players’ bench with white tape. From here the phone would get Mieke’s skates and just miss the ad for the dentist who had put braces on both of them. She put the phone back in her jacket and worked the tape down with her thumbnail. The rubber mat under her shoes still smelled of the lemon stuff the cleaners sluiced it with on Sunday nights. Through the gate she could hear Mieke’s blades. Even a person who hadn’t been dragged round Hale Arena all her life could tell how fast she was going by the way the hiss changed at the end of the long side.

Halfway through the run Jory put up a hand, palm out, which they’d agreed in the dressing room meant stop. Every sentence you called out from the bench came apart under the fans. Mieke swung in from the centre line and brought a spray of shavings up against the boards.

“If you give me till the end, there’s a reason I come back toward the benches,” she said. “The woman in the black dress in the show video does this beautiful thing where you’re sure she’s headed into the wing, and she comes back in off that foot so fast the man behind her is already looking the wrong way. I wanted to see if you’d look the wrong way.”

“She’s got a follow spot,” Jory said. “Every time you get that close to me, your skates go out of the frame.”

The emergency alert sounded in the pocket of her jacket. The empty arena gave it back to her. Amber, she thought. She had heard it on two consecutive nights last month, when a boy had gone missing up by the border. Mieke leaned an elbow on the top of the boards and looked down at the pocket. Jory kept both hands on the bench till the sound stopped.

“Leave it,” she said. “I can put you at the window end. There’s still enough light if we start now.”
