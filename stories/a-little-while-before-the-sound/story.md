# A Little While Before the Sound

This morning Mieke’s audition is still on my phone. I’ve watched the first twenty seconds quite a few times. She comes over the blue line with her eyes down, the way she comes up a driveway when she’s sure the dog next door has got loose, and then she lifts her head and by God there she is. You should try getting a look like that from my sister when there’s only you in the room. Yesterday I watched it and told her I could see the green exit sign reflected in the boards.

We’d waited for the last public skate to finish. On Sundays in Raincross it was possible to have Hale Arena all to yourself by four, provided you knew the woman at the desk. I had my work keys in my hand when I told Mieke she could have another twenty minutes. I like telling Mieke what I can do with those keys. We’d already got one full run on the phone, and as soon as I’d shown her the sign she’d said I could film from somewhere else. She left her bag and her own phone in the dressing room with her street shoes.

When we went back out by the bench, she took her skate guards off and gave them to me to hold while she tightened a lace. There was black grit from the rubber mats stuck in the grooves. My sister had talked to a woman from the ice show three days before and she waited until I had a pink skate guard in each hand to tell me about it.

“She asked what part I wanted,” Mieke said. “I told her I liked the cat. It turns out there are four people in that cat. When it jumps, there’s a whole person in the tail who has to know about it.”

“Show her you have a good back edge,” I said. “She can do whatever she likes with your tail once she gives you a job.”

Mieke took the guards back and tapped them together over the mat. “I asked her what the first month on the road was like. She said at the beginning you tell everybody where you’re from, and by the time you’ve said Raincross six or seven times they all think they know it. They want to know how your dad’s doing. She made it sound fairly easy to go somewhere you don’t know anybody.”

There were people in town who still asked me how Mieke was doing when she was on the ice right in front of them. I tried to picture her in a hotel breakfast room with a woman I had never seen, explaining how Dad called the river a creek till the spring he lost his lawnmower in it, and the woman being glad she’d asked. I kept seeing Mieke on her first day at school with the hem of her skirt caught up under her backpack. I couldn’t have told you what she’d worn to school the day before she left it.

She set the guards on the bench and went out to try the last part for me. I stayed there on the mat that smelled of the lemon stuff the cleaners sluiced it with after public skate. Behind me was the concrete passage to the dressing rooms. It came out here halfway down the long side. When the little teams came running for an entrance you could hear a dozen pairs of skates before you saw the first helmet come round the bend. Mieke had always been able to take that corner in skates without putting a hand on the wall. To get out among the people, she used the public gate at the street end, under the windows. Dad had come to meet her there with her guards in his coat pocket when she was too small to carry them herself.

She went out backwards from the bench, skated the length of the ice and turned. A push on the outside edge sent her back the way she’d come. Her arms were still reaching toward the street end when her feet took her away from it. I put up a hand, palm out. We’d worked out in the dressing room that meant stop. Every sentence you called out from the bench came apart under the fans.

Mieke swung in and stopped in a spray of shavings. “Did you see what I did with my arms? There’s a woman in the show who does this beautiful thing where you’re sure she’s headed into the wing, and she comes back in off that foot so fast the man behind her is already looking the wrong way. I wanted to see if you’d look the wrong way.”

“I was looking at your feet.”

“You always look at my feet first. I was trying to get one thing in there for the people who watch the whole person.” She dug a shaving out of a lace hook with her fingernail. “My feet can make their own video.”

The emergency signal went off in my jacket. It was a nasty sound in a little car when you were trying to get home. In an empty rink it got all the way up into the rafters. Mieke put an elbow on top of the boards and looked down at my pocket. It seemed to me we’d only just had a whole week of those alerts, though what I remembered was lying in bed going over the name of a town I’d never been to and trying to work out which side of the border it was on.

“An Amber alert,” I told her. “I’ll look when we’re done.”

Mieke swept the shavings away from the gate with the side of her blade. I could see the box of light the windows made on the ice behind her, wide enough for her to turn in. The sun was low enough to catch her face the whole time she came out of the turn if she did it at the right angle. I waited till her eyes followed mine.

“Let’s use the window end,” I said. “We can park you there till I get my angle and then you can do the part with your arms.”

“I can do it from here if you take a step sideways.”

“Try it for me once with the light on you. I’ve got your first run on the phone if you want to send that.”

She put the toe of one skate in, ready to push, the way she used to do when I gave her two errands and she wanted me to say which one I was actually going to make her do. Then she set off for the windows. It took her three strokes to get there. She wanted to find a line she could do twice without looking down for a mark, so I watched while she worked it out. From the end of the bench I could measure her progress by the mullions. The first time two of their shadows went across her face. The next time I asked her to go deeper into the light and she came back with both palms open to show me she’d found it. I put up a finger for one more. Her skates made two new curves beside the faceoff dot before I was satisfied. By then I’d spent so long with one hand in my pocket that I could feel the raised edge of the phone case through my glove.

The light came all the way across the rink. I saw Mieke with her face lit from the street, and her shadow lay across the blue line with the shadows of the window bars. The panes were white to the tops of their frames. For that little while I could see the chips along the yellow kickplate on the far side, where pucks had carried off the paint. Then I could see the windows again, with the dark backs of the houses across the road.

I looked up. The lamps in the roof were steady. The fans were going on as they had been all afternoon. It was possible to hear a blade turn on the ice, and I heard Mieke’s. She’d put a hand above her eyes and started for the public gate. I thought the closer she got the better she’d be able to tell what had happened out there. I had my head turned in the same direction. Then I remembered the way the light had come in, hard enough to put the window bars halfway across the ice, and the noise my phone had made a few minutes before when Mieke had looked to me to tell her what it was.

I took out the phone. There on the screen was the warning I’d left sitting there. It had the name of our district in the first line. An incoming meteor was expected to break up over us. People were to get indoors and away from windows. A damaging blast wave could follow the flash.

When I came to that last sentence I went back and read it again. The windows were still there. All that time we’d been turning Mieke around under them, somebody up the street might have been getting his children away from the glass. I had the thought that there was still a way to make what I’d said to her come out all right. The mistake would end up being the funny part of the story, if I could get her off there before the sound came.

I put the phone back into my pocket. “Mieke. Back here.” I could hear the second word coming back to me from high up as if somebody had tried to do it in my voice with their mouth full. She turned her head, then kept going. I could see her lifting her chin to look through the glass above the boards. The public gate was right by the place where we’d got the light on her face.

I took a step toward the passage behind the bench. Three more and I could get round the concrete turn. Out on the ice Mieke was letting her last stroke carry her. I turned and went through the bench gate. The sole of my shoe skated out on its heel. I put it down again on the whole of the foot, as if I’d got something balanced across my palms. Six paces diagonally out from the bench I could see the side of Mieke’s face. I put one hand high, palm out. I thought if I shouted again she’d keep looking around for the sound, so I held the hand there, which left me with the other arm to steady myself as the foot I’d put down went on drifting.

She saw me. She drew her feet together and came round so fast the free skate passed her other leg cleanly in front of the knee. I’d seen her get a mark for that turn as a child. Now she had both arms out and the point of one shoulder forward and I could see she was going to come past me close enough for me to speak without shouting.

“The light was a meteor,” I said. “The blast can still come. Go through the bench.”

For a second she was looking straight into my face. “Stay where you are till I get past you.” She gave me room, using an edge I could hear cut in. I turned too quickly. I managed to get one arm down before my knee hit the ice. It went through the denim like a blow in exactly the place I used to have a pad when I took the beginner classes. I got back up with my hands on the ice. When I looked up, Mieke was already at the bench. She put both blades sideways to stop in time for the latch and the switch of her hips brought her through the gate and onto the mat.

“Give me your sleeve when you get here,” she called.

She held the gate out of the way with her shoulder. I came the last two steps with my hand stretched toward her and when she took me by the sleeve I could pick my head up and see the passage. There was an old crease in the mat where its edge wanted to come up. The toe of my shoe slid over it and landed on rubber. I could have kissed the floor. Mieke sent me ahead through the first part of the passage and clattered along behind. At the turn I put an arm out, felt the concrete at my fingertips and stopped there. She came round the bend and took a seat on the floor right up against my leg. I sat.

For a little while we could hear the fans from the other room. I used to get children to sit there and calm down before we went out for their first test. I would put my back against the concrete, just like that, and get them to set their hands on their knees. There was no other place in Hale Arena where a child stood such a good chance of hearing you. I had always thought I could get the right amount of sound in here.

“The meteor warning was the sound I told you was Amber,” I said. “I hadn’t looked at it till I saw the flash.”

“Let me see.”

I took the phone out again and put it on the floor between us. Mieke leaned over it and moved a finger back to the top of the message.

“Four fourteen,” she said. “I’d just got that ice out of my lace. I was going to tell you I liked the first take. I’d done the part with my arms exactly the way I wanted and it bothered me having to do it again.”

I was about to tell her how many people in Raincross must have got the same sound I did. Mieke’s finger rested on the numbers. There was a strip of pink above her boot where her tights had slipped down, and if I’d read the message when I got it we’d have had time to take the skates off in here.

“You held your arms out till you were coming straight back at me,” I said. “We could’ve sent that one. I had room to move down the bench if I wanted another.”

“Does it say how long we have after the light?” she said.

“It only gives the time it came through. I’d like to be able to tell you.”

She reached over me and put the phone down against the wall. I’d been holding my head so far forward that I could see my knee shaking. Mieke had her hands flat on the floor. She took one off and put it against the back of my neck, so that I could let my head down.

I felt the sound in the shoe I had flat on the floor. It came up hard enough for my teeth to touch. Then there was a much longer noise from the rink, starting at the street end and coming on in a succession of breaks so fast I couldn’t have said where one stopped. The air went cold round the corner. I shut my eyes against the grit. It tasted of chalk and I could feel it behind my ears. There were little knocks out there which I thought were the last of the panes falling. Under those, something was still going across the ice. It had the light, steady sound of somebody shaking sand out of a shoe.

We could hear a car horn out in the street. I waited for it to quit and it went on. The fans came back into my hearing one by one, though for all I know they’d never stopped. Mieke got up and took hold of the inside wall. At the mouth of the passage the cold got in under my collar. Beside me one of her blades clicked against the concrete and she stopped. There was enough light coming through the tall empty frames for me to see the two lines she’d made on the final turn. They ran side by side to the faceoff dot and disappeared under the glass.
