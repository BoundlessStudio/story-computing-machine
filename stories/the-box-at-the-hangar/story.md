There were fourteen steps at Mr. Auden's house. Dena had carried an upright up all fourteen the winter he moved there. At the turn she'd had to wait for him to take the ferns off the landing, and he'd gone round thanking each person in turn as if he'd been given the piano instead of buying it.

This time he said there were five steps and asked if she'd come down on the price.

She put in a hundred and eighty and took it out again. The queue moved, and Bess caught her at the elbow before she walked into a stanchion. One of the roof lights in the old maintenance hangar at North Sill Airport let in a thread of rain. It broke on the concrete beside her boot and ran along the yellow stripe where they'd once towed planes in. At the end of the stripe stood the Box. There was something so ordinary about its height that you could imagine the person who'd have to tilt his head to get inside. Garth might have to.

“Dena. That woman over by the guard.”

The woman had just come out of the Box. She was pinching at the cuff of her coat, which was brown with a print of green leaves. The leaf came up between her fingers with a stem on it. She let go. It stayed upright. Bess got an arm through the rail to point, and Dena pulled her back by the belt. The guard had already told somebody twice to keep their arms on this side.

“If I get that,” Bess said, “I'd like it to work on the drawing of an orange, so I'd have an orange to eat while I tried the drawing of a horse.”

Dena got as far as thinking where a horse would fit, with the shoe rack out of the hall, before the phone buzzed. Mr. Auden had a price from the other mover. It was forty less than the number Dena had just taken out. The man said he could send the piano straight from the room it was in to the room where he wanted it. Mr. Auden was giving Dena first refusal because she'd done his Friday deliveries for eight years. This was the first one he'd sent her in a month.

“Which of you is going?” asked the man ahead. His sister had been holding his coat for him on the far side of the rail until he asked for it back. He had one arm in it now.

“All three,” Dena said. It came out readily, like the price when you'd already seen the stairs.

Garth put his hand out. “You'll want to leave the side door alone till you get the van home. I parked facing downhill and it sticks that way.”

He had put the garage key on the ring as well. It was the flat brass one Bess had marked with a drop of nail varnish years ago, when they'd got locked out with an order in the back and had to tell a woman the piano was going to be late to her wedding. Dena curled two fingers around the ring and tried to pass it back. Garth spread both hands on the railing and put his weight on them.

“If you have to ring my sister,” he said, “will you do it yourself? She knows I came. She's been ringing me up to tell me all the things that need carrying downstairs at hers. She's got a whole trunk of clothes for the charity shop all of a sudden.”

“You'll be taking that trunk down yourself,” Dena said. She put the keys away. He had held them long enough for the brass to be warm through the lining of her pocket.

At the next bend there was a row of heaters, and there the wet coats gave off a smell of old wool. The woman from the Box waited by the side exit for somebody outside to hurry in and see her cuff. Bess watched until there were too many people in between.

“There's a room above the bakery on Wood Lane,” Bess said. “Did I tell you I went to see it? It's got an alcove where they used to keep the flour, and you could put a piano in there and still open the door. You should see the little yellow squares on the floor where the flour bins were.”

“Who wants a piano up there?”

“I want a piano up there.”

The roof panels had gone the green they went when it was late enough to turn on the lamps. Dena saw the whole room, with flour in the cracks and no one in it. She would have liked to ask why Bess had found a place for the piano before saying anything about where she'd sleep. But Garth had started laughing somewhere down in his chest, hard enough that he had to break off when he tried to say what at.

“The first thing I'd do if I got flying is take a look in my sister's window,” he said. “I traded a radio for my first van and it was her radio in my room, see. She says I could stay away six months and she'd miss the radio more. I'll go up to the fifth floor when she's fixing supper and put my feet on her sill. I'll ask her just how much she's got that wants bringing down.”

***

The second bend of the queue brought them back near the entrance, with three dozen people between them and the place they'd started. A man was coming in with a boy asleep against his shoulder. The guard held the door until the man's bag was clear, then reached for a numbered slip. Anybody could get one for the asking. There was one Box in the world, and the thing you paid for, if you came a long way, was getting here.

On the back of every slip were the two things that could happen once the door closed. An even chance of burning to death, an even chance of coming out with a power. Whatever power you got was chance again. Dena had taken three numbers because she could think of almost anything they could get between them and see a use for it. On the morning she made the booking she'd looked up the odds of all three people dying and come downstairs feeling better. She hadn't put down anybody's name while she was doing the sums.

Garth took off his cap and let Bess push his hair flat with her hand. He went when his number was called. There was a low gate and beyond it a stretch of floor kept clear all the way to the Box. From much farther back, Dena had thought the Box looked as if you could tip it and slide something under it. Now she could see how the sides went straight down into the concrete. The old paint of the tow stripe went right up to the shut iron door.

An attendant met Garth out there and pulled the door open. Garth put his cap back on to go in, then had to lower his head after all. He stepped over the raised lip and turned so he was facing out. The attendant asked if he wanted the door shut. Garth called “Go on,” with his hand flat against the inside wall. It was how he spoke to someone who had come into a narrow space to help him.

The door shut. A white line showed under it, thin as the edge of a sheet of paper. She heard Garth make the first sound of a word, then his boot came down hard enough to hear through the door. For a little while Dena waited for the rest of what he'd been about to say. When the line went out there was a smell of scorched wool, and she could still hear the man who had asked which of them was going. He was pulling in air through his teeth, more and more quietly as he got his breath back.

Two people came across with a stretcher. They pulled the door open and went in. Dena had probably seen them take a sheet out of the folded end of the stretcher, because afterward she knew how it was that Garth was covered. She had been looking at the door, though, where he had stood with his hand out. Something caught for a second on the raised lip under the stretcher. Dena remembered that Garth had left one cuff turned up from work the day before. An attendant pulled the sheet farther over his shoulder before she could see what had caught.

Dena took out his keys. She was very near the low gate. There was a stretch of perhaps forty feet between her and him, and then the attendants turned for the service exit. The guard put his hand across the latch of the gate when she took a step along it. By the time she understood what she'd wanted, there was the knock of a wheel over the door track and both attendants were on the other side. She had wanted Garth to be able to sit up and ask her what she'd done with the van, because she could show him the keys and tell him she hadn't even moved it. She had got nearly as far as holding them out to him.

Bess came up alongside her and took her arm. “Come on,” she said, so low Dena felt the word through how her sister was gripping her before she understood it. “We can get out the way we came in.”

They went a little way together. People flattened themselves to let them through. There was a woman pouring something from a thermos into its lid. Some of it had spilled over her fingers, and she took a step sideways with one hand out, trying to keep the hot lid from coming up against the sisters as they passed. At the rail a guard pointed out the hinged section they could leave by once he'd opened it. Dena stopped there. Behind them one of the attendants was setting a fan in front of the open Box. She felt such relief at being farther from it that for a minute she thought they'd both already left.

“We can get a van from Cotton's on Monday,” she said. “We'll ring about Auden's to fill the afternoon and go after the one at Orchard Music in the morning. For the price of a day's hire we can do two if we have them lined up right. We've got a set of straps at home. I can go back to the yard for the blocks when his sister's had time to put things in order.”

Bess dropped her arm. “Garth's sister hasn't heard yet.”

Dena held on to the rail for a minute. “He told me to call. I was thinking they'd want to tell her themselves.”

“They'll be able to tell her what happened to him. You're going to have to tell her what he said to you.”

Dena had the keys in her fist and could feel the little peg for the garage sticking out against one finger. She lowered her hand. If she had to tell Garth's sister anything, she wanted to be able to tell her they'd brought the van home too, so there'd be no call from the airport in a week's time about getting it out of its space. She pictured walking it out of the lot with one hand on the back of the passenger seat, as Garth did when he couldn't see for the rain. She was looking at a rail in a hangar and all the things she'd imagined doing were things she could do. She could get Bess as far as the van today.

“You can have Monday afternoon if you need it,” Dena said. “You can go see the room again. I'm sure they'll let you back in if you want to have another look.”

“I don't need another look. I've asked if she'll hold it till the first.” Bess put two fingers in her pocket and brought out the end of her slip. “I put a chair in it in my head as soon as I saw the place. A chair you could put all the way back against the wall without somebody having to get up and open a cupboard. I'll have to get the money yet. You asked me if I wanted to come. I said I did, and then all the way here you talked about jobs we could do after. You were trying to take another one while we were in line. I've been looking up at the other end of this room all afternoon, Dena.”

“Come home with me, Bess.” Dena wanted to make a good case. If there was time, she could have told Bess where they could get a van for a week and how much work they'd have to put in to make it pay. “I'll drive it. There'll be some money. I'll answer the phone for the two of us and you can decide what you'll do when we get through the front door. If I stay in the line till they call my number I'll walk out there just because it's what I said I'd do. Look what I said to Garth about that trunk. I was standing right here telling him he'd be home to take it down. I would give you every penny Auden ever paid me to get you to come back to the van.”

Bess breathed out quite steadily. “I'd take you up on the lift. And I'd take you up on the money. I'd still want what I wanted when you booked it with me. Even if the work comes back, I've seen a woman touch her coat and get a real green leaf off it. I could have sat in that room at Wood Lane doing that all afternoon. I think if we get back to the van I'll be pulling out my phone to see when I can get another number.”

“On the way here I kept wanting you to say you were looking forward to it.” What Dena could hear in her own voice was how badly she'd wanted Bess to say so. Then she would have been able to spend the drive asking Bess to tell her how good it would be. “I had the three of us coming out and having the usual argument about who was in the middle.”

Beyond the rail they opened the gate. People who'd been whispering began to talk again. A woman read Dena's number out once, and then again. The man with the coat he'd put back on looked from Dena to Bess, and she remembered having given him three people to watch for. Dena loosened her hand on the keys and said she was giving up her turn. The guard swung the hinged section aside and she went across to the companion side. She had thought she'd want the other people to watch Bess follow. She found she couldn't look away from her sister even to see if they were looking.

Bess put her hand on the top of the rail. “He'd have gone to that window first. I was thinking she might have been fixing supper already. He could have knocked on it and given her a terrible fright.”

The track the stretcher had gone over was still visible from where they stood. Bess stood looking at it till Dena had time to let go of the breath she had been holding.

“Will you wait here while I go?” Bess said. “If they have to take me that way, will you stay till they tell you where they're taking me?”

Dena got close enough to put her hands over Bess's. She thought Bess's hands would be cold and they were. At work they were always warm by the time one of them reached for the other's hand, because there was so much to do first. She could feel in each finger the small uneasiness of somebody leaving their hand where it was.

“I'll wait for you right here,” she said. “If they take you through there I'll wait till they come and tell me.”

Bess took her slip up to the gate and held it for the guard to see. She walked the clear length of floor alone. From the new place at the railing Dena saw her in profile. She tilted forward at the entrance, with her knee up to get across the lip, and then put it down again on the concrete outside. She looked at the attendant and said something Dena couldn't hear. He waited while Bess tucked her hair inside her collar and stepped over. When she turned she was close enough to the threshold that she had to lift her chin to let the door pass her. Before he swung it shut she told him to do it, in a clear voice Dena could hear all the way to where she stood.

Dena looked where the white had been. She had time to hear the engine from the next bay decelerate, a sound like an appliance turned down in a house when somebody comes in and wants to speak. She ought to have told Bess that she'd be along behind wherever they took her, even if she had to wait outside another door after this one.

The attendant pulled the door open. Bess walked out. She put one hand to her mouth and held it there. At the return gate the guard held a paper cup out to her. Dena let out a noise she thought her sister had made and went as far along the rail as she could. Bess had turned at the sound and brushed the cup as she turned. The guard reached for it too late. It fell halfway to the floor and there it hung, crooked and light, dripping water while Bess stared.

***

A week later there was room for the ramps all along one wall of the hired van. Garth had kept a crate in that space. They'd pushed it under gates that swung shut and propped toolboxes on it, and once, in their first year on their own, Dena had stood on it to see whether Bess was coming back down a street they'd got lost in. When she'd taken Garth's keys back to his sister she'd meant to ask if they could have the crate. His sister had opened the garage with the little brass key and said she supposed she'd have to sell the van, and Dena put her hands in her pockets.

She'd told Garth's sister about the window. His sister wanted to know whether those were the last words he'd said and Dena had told her about his cap and how he had asked them to shut the door. Later Bess said she should have told her what he said about the trunk. His sister would have liked to hear that he'd let himself be persuaded to think about carrying it down.

Dena took a block up to the landing at Orchard Music. There were seven steps from there to the alley door, with a fresh mark in the paint where somebody had brought something too wide up them. She could smell the new wood of the stairs beneath the grit people had carried in from the alley. The man from the shop had left the lid of the little upright open for the two of them. He wanted it brought to a woman who was going to come home from work at four to receive it. Bess played four bars of something Dena could remember her playing in her socks in a house they'd emptied for a move. Then she closed the lid.

“Are you taking that one in Pine Street with me on Thursday?” Dena said. She had put the question off until they were both there.

“The place at the freight yard wants me in Thursday morning,” Bess said. “I told them I'm free for three hours. Now they want to know where to ring me the rest of the week.”

Bess had put a saucer in her coat pocket on the morning after the airport, taken it out to the yard and dropped it over and over onto the flagstones until she could stop it when she meant to. She'd learned she could steady one thing as long as she kept her eyes on it. The freight yard had a load they said was worth more than the whole street where she wanted to live. They'd offered her a day's rate to come and see what she could do with it. Dena tried to picture the man on the phone with a hand out, offering her a chance to look at that much money, and found she'd agree to a lot of things before she asked what he expected her to hold up.

“Do they mean they'll pay you to stay near the phone?” she said.

“They said I could get more hours if I turned out to be easy to reach.” Bess picked up her end of the board and set it down a little closer to the piano. “You know what I'd like? To have a number I could give them that went to an empty room. They could ring it and listen to every board in the floor. I'd like that just as much as I want them to call and offer me the work.”

Dena got the quilt over the piano and watched Bess tuck it clear of the little castors. “You can ask them for the three hours and make them pay you separately if they want more. That's three hours of work you're talking about. The other hours they're asking for belong to you while they decide.”

“Do I get to decide about Thursday afternoon at Pine Street, then?”

“I put us both down for that one in my book before I even quoted it,” Dena said. “I can ring another mover. Will you do it with me if we can finish when you want?”

“Put me down for the afternoon. I want to come with you. I'd like to finish by six. The woman at the bakery heard me trying to whistle that tune and she's going to show me what comes after the part I know.”

They got the piano onto its board and brought it to the first step. There was a time when Dena would call out on a tight stair and get Garth answering from the alley door before Bess could tell her she was ready. The first six steps went slowly. Dena could hear somebody in the shop playing a tinny scale as if words were being learned for the first time behind the wall.

At the last step Dena felt the board slide in her hands. The piano had got its weight moving before she could shift her foot. Bess said her name sharply. There the piano stopped, with its quilt hanging over Dena's end and a corner of the board a fist's width off the floor. The quilt swung once against a brass pedal and settled. There was no feeling through either of Dena's arms. She pulled her fingers out and the space stayed. She'd known what Bess could do and still it took her a long time to put her hands down.

A phone rang in Bess's coat pocket. From above the piano came a deep breath that petered out before it was finished. Dena went round to the side and saw Bess looking down at the piano over the open stair, with one hand still the way she'd held it on the board when it started to slide.

“That'll be the freight yard,” Bess said. “Leave it, will you? I'll call them when I'm finished.”

“I'll put a block in,” Dena said. She had left one clump of felt-covered wood by the door. To set it where she wanted she had to get on one knee beside the board and put her hand in close to its underside. She had been taught to do anything but that with a piano on a flight of stairs, and even knowing it was Bess that held the weight she had to make herself look while she did it. The ringing stopped.

“Tell me when you've got it,” Bess said.

Dena got hold of her end where she could bear it and looked again at the space between the board and the block. The distance was half what she remembered between the cup and the hangar floor. She could sense her sister waiting on the other side of the piano. “Put it on the block,” she said. “Then come down and take the other handle.”

Bess let the weight come back. Dena felt it run through both arms into the floor. It was there to be lifted again, and she waited till she had Bess's foot against hers and could hear her getting the breath to do it.
