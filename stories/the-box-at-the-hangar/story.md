Mr. Auden had somehow mislaid nine of his steps. *Five steps to the street, Dena. Could you come down a bit?* It was nearly four, and the queue in the old maintenance hangar at North Sill Airport had stopped under a leak. Dena edged in between Garth and her sister Bess to keep the rain off her phone. The piano Mr. Auden wanted out of his front room was the one they'd brought up all fourteen steps the winter he moved. Halfway up she'd had to wait for him to take the ferns off the landing, and he'd gone round thanking each person in turn as if he'd been given the piano instead of buying it.

Dena typed in one hundred and eighty, then deleted it. The queue moved, and Bess caught her at the elbow before she walked into a stanchion. Rain broke on the concrete beside her boot and ran along the yellow stripe where they'd once towed planes in. At the end of the stripe stood the Box. Its grey iron door came up about as high as one of their long piano boards stood on end. Garth might have to duck.

“Dena,” Bess said. “That woman over by the guard.”

The woman had just come out of the Box. She was pinching at the cuff of her coat, which was brown with a print of green leaves. One of the leaves came up between her fingers with a stem on it. She let go. It stayed upright. Bess got an arm through the rail to point, and Dena pulled her back by the belt. The guard had already told somebody twice to keep their arms on this side.

“If I get that,” Bess said, “I'd like it to work on the drawing of an orange, so I'd have an orange to eat while I tried the drawing of a horse.”

Dena got as far as thinking where a horse would fit, with the shoe rack out of the hall, before the phone buzzed. *My new mover can send it straight out of the house with the doors shut, right into the music room at Firth Road. He'll do it for a hundred and forty.* Forty less than Dena had nearly asked. That was what they set aside for Garth's van before any of them put a hand on a piano. She looked down the stripe at the Box. Whatever it had given Auden's new mover, he could do that job without a van. Auden had rung her with pianos to move every Friday for eight years. It was good of him to remember her number after a month, even if it was only to ask her to match a price.

The man in front of Dena took his coat from a woman on the far side of the rail. He worked one arm into it. “Which of you is going?”

“All three,” Dena said. It came out readily, like the price when you'd already seen the stairs.

Garth held out the key ring. “You'll want to leave the side door alone till you get the van home. I parked facing downhill and it sticks that way.”

He had put the garage key on the ring as well. It was the flat brass one Bess had marked with a drop of nail varnish years ago, when they'd got locked out with an order in the back and had to tell a woman the piano was going to be late to her wedding. Dena curled two fingers around the ring and tried to pass it back. Garth spread both hands on the railing and put his weight on them.

“If you have to ring my sister,” he said, “will you do it yourself? She knows I came. She's been ringing me up to tell me all the things that need carrying downstairs at hers. She's got a whole trunk of clothes for the charity shop all of a sudden.”

“You'll be taking that trunk down yourself,” Dena said. She put the keys away. He had held them long enough for the brass to be warm through the lining of her pocket.

The wet coats had begun to steam by the heaters at the next bend, and the place smelled of old wool. The woman from the Box waited by the side exit for somebody outside to hurry in and see her cuff. Bess watched until there were too many people in between.

“There's a room above the bakery on Wood Lane,” Bess said. “Did I tell you I went to see it? It's got an alcove where they used to keep the flour, and you could put a piano in there and still open the door. You should see the little yellow squares on the floor where the flour bins were.”

“Who wants a piano up there?”

“It's for my own piano, when I've got one,” Bess said. “I could leave the lid up all the time in there.”

The roof panels were darkening to green when the lamps came on. Dena saw the whole room, with flour in the cracks and no one in it. She would have liked to ask why Bess had found a place for the piano before saying anything about where she'd sleep. Garth was looking up at the roof and the crossbeams running below it.

“The first thing I'd do if I got flying is take a look in my sister's window,” he said. “I was short for the first van, so I took a perfectly good radio off my windowsill and let the man have it for the difference. It was my sister's radio, see. She says I could stay away six months and she'd miss the radio more. I'll go up to the fifth floor when she's fixing supper and put my feet on her sill. I'll ask her just how much she's got that wants bringing down.”

***

The second bend of the queue brought them back near the entrance, with three dozen people between them and the place they'd started. A man was coming in with a boy asleep against his shoulder. The guard held the door until the man's bag was clear, and cold air went under Dena's cuffs. At the table by the doors another guard found the man's booking and handed him a numbered slip. Anyone could book a turn and it cost nothing. North Sill had the only Box in the world.

On the back of every slip were the two things that could happen once the door closed. An even chance of burning to death, an even chance of coming out with a power. Whatever power you got was chance again. Dena had taken three numbers because she thought even one new thing any of them could do might be enough to bring the work back. She'd waited four months for an afternoon when they could get three turns together. On the morning she made the booking she'd looked up the odds of all three people dying and come downstairs feeling better. She hadn't put down anybody's name while she was doing the sums.

Garth took off his cap and let Bess push his hair flat with her hand. He went when his number was called. A low gate opened onto a stretch of floor kept clear all the way to the Box. From much farther back, Dena had thought the Box looked as if you could tip it and slide something under it. Now she could see how the sides went straight down into the concrete. The old paint of the tow stripe went right up to the shut iron door.

An attendant met Garth out there and pulled the door open. Garth put his cap back on to go in, then had to lower his head after all. He stepped over the raised lip and turned so he was facing out. The attendant asked if he wanted the door shut. Garth called “Go on,” with his hand flat against the inside wall. He'd say that on a landing, with a piano between them, when he'd got his own end settled and Dena could let hers down.

The door shut. A line of white showed where the iron met the lip, a gap Dena couldn't have got a piano strap through. She heard Garth make the first sound of a word, then his boot came down hard enough to hear through the door. Bess caught Dena's coat at the elbow. Dena was looking to see if the attendant had heard him too, but he had taken a step back and was looking at the light under the door. For a little while Dena waited for the rest of what he'd been about to say. When the line went out she smelled scorched wool, and she could still hear the man who had asked which of them was going. He was pulling in air through his teeth, more and more quietly as he got his breath back.

Two people came across with a stretcher. They pulled the door open and went in. Dena had probably seen them take a sheet out of the folded end of the stretcher, because afterward she knew how it was that Garth was covered. She had been looking at the door, though, where he had stood with his hand out. Something caught on the raised lip under the stretcher. Dena remembered that Garth had left one cuff turned up from work the day before. An attendant pulled the sheet farther over his shoulder before she could see what had caught.

Dena took out his keys. She was very near the low gate. Perhaps forty feet of open floor lay between her and Garth. Then the attendants turned for the service exit. The guard put his hand across the latch of the gate when she took a step along it. By the time she understood what she'd wanted, there was the knock of a wheel over the door track and both attendants were on the other side. She had wanted Garth to be able to sit up and ask her what she'd done with the van, because she could show him the keys and tell him she hadn't even moved it. She had got nearly as far as holding them out to him.

Bess slid a hand under Dena's arm. “Come on,” she said almost under her breath, and gave an upward tug, the way she did when Dena had missed a step. “We can get out the way we came in.”

They went a few paces back to the bend in the rail. People flattened themselves to let them through. A woman was pouring something from a thermos into its lid. Some of it had spilled over her fingers, and she took a step sideways with one hand out, trying to keep the hot lid from coming up against the sisters as they passed. At the bend a guard showed them where a hinged section opened sideways to the people waiting with coats and bags. Bess brought Dena right up to it. Beyond the low gate an attendant was setting a fan in front of the open Box. Dena felt such relief at being farther from it that she thought they'd both already left.

“We can get a van from Cotton's on Monday,” she said. “We'll ring about Auden's to fill the afternoon and go after the one at Orchard Music in the morning. For the price of a day's hire we can do two if we have them lined up right. We've got a set of straps at home. I can go back to the yard for the blocks when his sister's had time to put things in order.”

Bess dropped her arm. “Garth's sister hasn't heard yet.”

Dena kept her eyes on the line of bolts holding the rail to the floor. “He told me to call. I was thinking they'd want to tell her themselves.”

“They'll be able to tell her what happened to him. You're going to have to tell her what he said to you.”

Dena had the keys in her fist and could feel the little peg for the garage sticking out against one finger. If she had to tell Garth's sister anything, she wanted to be able to tell her they'd brought the van home too, so there'd be no call from the airport in a week's time about getting it out of its space. She pictured backing out of the lot with one hand on the passenger seat, looking out through the square Garth had wiped clear on the rear window. His keys knocked against her house key as she put them back in her pocket.

“You can have Monday afternoon if you need it,” Dena said. “You can go see the room again. I'm sure they'll let you back in if you want to have another look.”

“I don't need another look. I've asked if she'll hold it till the first.” Bess put two fingers in her pocket and brought out the end of her slip. “I put a chair in it in my head as soon as I saw the place. I could put it all the way back against the wall and sit there as long as I liked. At home I have to get up and shift the chair every time somebody wants to open the cupboard. I'll have to get the money yet. You asked me if I wanted to come. I said I did, and then all the way here you talked about jobs we could do after. You were trying to take another one while we were in line. I've been looking at the other end of this room all afternoon, Dena.”

“Come home with me, Bess. I'll drive. I'll take what Auden's offering. If we can get the piano down for what he's paying, it'll be a start. I'll answer the phone and you can decide what you'll do when we get through the front door. Look what I said to Garth about that trunk. He'd put the keys in my hand and I told him he'd be home to take it down. I can't tell you you're coming out, Bess. I'll give you every penny Auden ever paid me if you'll come back to the van.”

Bess pressed flat a corner of her slip. “I'd take you up on the lift. And I'd take you up on the money. Even if all the work came back I'd still want a turn. I've seen a green leaf come out of a woman's coat. Think of growing a whole row of them out of the wallpaper in that room. I'd do it all afternoon, Dena. I think if we get back to the van I'll be pulling out my phone to see when I can get another number.”

“On the way here I kept waiting for you to say we'd all three be coming home,” Dena said. “I could see us getting back in the van. You saying it was my turn to sit in the middle.”

At the head of the queue the attendant swung open the low gate and read Dena's number. People who'd been whispering began to talk again. The attendant read the number a second time. The man who'd put his coat back on looked from Dena to Bess, and she remembered having given him three people to watch for. She left Bess at the bend and stepped up to the gate with her slip. Just beyond the attendant's shoes was the yellow stripe.

If she came out strong enough to take an upright down by herself, she could put a price back in the message to Auden and mean it. She could get on with the work while Bess went to see about her room. Dena looked down and saw that one of her boots was across the stripe already. The fan at the other end had blown the smell of scorched wool back up the line. When she thought of saying “Go on” to the man at the door, it came to her in Garth's voice.

“I'm giving up my turn,” Dena said. She backed up to where Bess stood with the guard. “Will you let me out here?”

The guard swung the hinged section aside. Bess gathered her coat close to her to give Dena room. There was space for two of them to get through before it swung back. Dena went across to where people had been waiting with their coats and bags, and when she turned, Bess was still on the other side of the rail.

Bess put her hand on the top of the rail. “He'd have gone to that window first. I was thinking she might have been fixing supper already. He could have knocked on it and given her a terrible fright.”

Bess's eyes went to the track the stretcher had gone over. Dena rubbed a drop of rain off the rail with her thumb and waited for her to look back.

“Will you wait here while I go?” Bess said. “If they have to take me that way, will you stay till they tell you where they're taking me?”

Dena put her hands over Bess's on the rail. She thought Bess's fingers would be cold and they were. At work they were always warm by the time one sister reached for the other's hand, because there was so much to do first. Bess moved one finger under her palm, then held it there.

“I'll wait for you right here,” Dena said. “I've got the van keys. If they have to take you out that way, I'll find out where they're going and I'll come along behind.”

Bess took her slip up to the gate and held it for the guard to see. She walked the clear length of floor alone. From the new place at the railing Dena saw her in profile. She tilted forward at the entrance, with her knee up to get across the lip, and then put her foot down again on the concrete outside. She looked at the attendant and said something Dena couldn't hear. He waited while Bess tucked her hair inside her collar and stepped over. She took another step inside and turned to look at him.

“Close it, please,” she said, clearly enough for Dena to hear.

The door shut. Dena watched the gap where the white had shown, gripping the rail on either side of where Bess's fingers had been. Someone else leant against the rail and she felt it give a little. She had time to hear the engine from the next bay ease off, a sound like an appliance turned down in a house when somebody comes in and wants to speak.

The attendant pulled the door open. Bess walked out. She put one hand to her mouth and held it there. At the return gate the guard held a paper cup out to her. Dena went as far along the rail as she could. “Bess!” Bess turned toward her and knocked the cup out of the guard's hand with her sleeve. The guard reached for it too late. It fell halfway to the floor and there it hung, crooked and light, dripping water while Bess stared.

***

A week later, at a quarter to three, Dena pulled the hired van into the alley behind Orchard Music. Mr. Auden's piano had reached Firth Road before she'd got back to him with a price. She could lay the ramps flat along one wall of the hired van. In Garth's van that space had belonged to the crate. They'd pushed the crate under gates that swung shut and propped toolboxes on it, and once, in the first year the three of them had worked together, Dena had stood on it to see whether Bess was coming back down a street they'd got lost in.

She'd rung Garth's sister Enid from the airport on the night he died. Two days after the call she went round with his keys, thinking she might ask if they could keep the crate. Enid opened the garage with the little brass key. She held the ring by the key with the spot of nail varnish, letting the rest of them hang down and knock together.

“When you rang, you said he'd been talking about my window. Was that the last thing he said?”

“He put his cap back on to go in. Then he had to duck. The man at the door asked if he wanted it shut and Garth said, ‘Go on.’ I could hear him right back where I was.”

“Did he call for anybody when the door was shut?”

“He started to say something.” Dena remembered wanting to hear the rest of the word quite as much as she'd wanted the door to open. “I couldn't make out what.”

Enid closed her hand on the keys. “I kept telling him I'd put that trunk out for him. There were only three cardigans in it. I've put them back now.” She looked into the garage. “I suppose I'll have to sell the van.”

Dena went close enough to the rear window to see the upper edge of the crate through the square Garth had wiped clear. She put her hands in her pockets.

At Orchard Music Dena took a block up the seven steps from the alley door. Something too wide had left a fresh mark in the paint. She could smell the new wood of the stairs beneath the grit people had carried in from the alley. The man from the shop had left the lid of the little upright open for the two of them. He wanted it brought to a woman who was going to come home from work at four to receive it. Bess played the first four bars of “The Rakes of Mallow,” which Dena could remember her playing in her socks in a house they'd emptied for a move. Then she closed the lid.

“Are you coming with me to Pine Street on Thursday?” Dena said.

“I've given the freight yard three hours in the morning. They're asking when they can ring me the rest of the week.”

Bess had put a saucer in her coat pocket the morning after the airport and gone out the back door to drop it over and over onto the flagstones. Once she could stop it when she meant to, she found she could hold it as long as she kept her eyes on it. The freight yard was unloading plate glass on Thursday. They'd offered her a day's pay to see if she could hold a whole pallet steady in the air.

Dena shook out the quilt. “A whole day's pay. How much of a day do they think they're getting?”

“They said I could get more hours if I turned out to be easy to reach.” Bess picked up her end of the board and set it down a little closer to the piano. “I'd like a number I could give them that went to an empty room. They could ring it and listen to every board in the floor. I'd like that just as much as I want them to call and offer me the work.”

Dena laid the quilt over the piano. Bess tucked it clear of the little castors. “How much of Thursday have you put me down for?”

“I put us both down for that one in my book before I even quoted it,” Dena said. “I can ring another mover. Will you do it with me if we can finish when you want?”

“Put me down for the afternoon. I want to come with you. I'd like to finish by six. The woman at the bakery heard me trying to whistle that tune and she's going to show me what comes after the part I know.”

They got the piano onto its board and brought it to the first step. On a stair like this Garth used to wait at the alley door, where he could see Dena's feet when she couldn't. The first six steps went slowly. Dena could hear somebody in the shop behind the wall getting as far as the same note in a scale and starting over.

At the last step Dena felt the board slide in her hands. The piano had got its weight moving before she could shift her foot. Bess said her name sharply. There the piano stopped, with its quilt hanging over Dena's end and a corner of the board a fist's width off the floor. The quilt swung once against a brass pedal and settled. Dena's arms felt as empty as if she'd already put the board down. She pulled her fingers out and the space stayed. She'd known what Bess could do and still it took her a long time to put her hands down.

A phone rang in Bess's coat pocket. Dena heard her take a deep breath above the piano. From the side Dena could see Bess looking down over the open stair, one hand still the way she'd held it on the board when it started to slide.

“That'll be the freight yard,” Bess said. “Leave it, will you? I'll call them when I'm finished.”

“I'll put a block in,” Dena said. She had left a second felt-covered block by the door. To set it where she wanted she had to get on one knee beside the board and put her hand in close to its underside. She had been taught to do anything but that with a piano on a flight of stairs, and even knowing it was Bess that held the weight she had to make herself look while she did it. The ringing stopped.

“Tell me when you've got it,” Bess said.

Dena got hold of her end where she could bear it and looked again at the space between the board and the block. The distance was half what she remembered between the cup and the hangar floor. Bess's heel scraped the step above her. “Put it on the block,” Dena said. “Then come down and take the other handle.”

Bess let the weight come back. Dena felt it run through both arms into the floor. It was there to be lifted again, and she waited till she had Bess's foot against hers and could hear her getting the breath to do it.
