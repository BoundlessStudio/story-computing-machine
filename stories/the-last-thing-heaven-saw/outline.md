## Provisional direction

Approximately 4,000–5,000 words, close third person centered on the woman, Ilessa, with enough distance to preserve her terrible composure. The supplied original informs her silver-white hair, pale face, crimson cloth, and dark clothing. Her blindfold is a restraint she chooses, not a divine shackle. Her uncovered gaze unmakes what it falls upon; it makes no exception for innocence. She can avert or close her eyes, but cannot look safely upon the world she has spent a millennium preserving.

The conflict is between her chosen restraint and a newly enthroned High God, Namar, who believes her concern for the world makes her governable. Emperor Darovan, a formidable conqueror whose victories helped establish Namar's supremacy, is a petitioner and compromised witness. Their relationship is political confrontation, not dependence or care. Ilessa wants to end Heaven's attempt to turn her mercy into a means of ruling her and everyone beneath it.

1. **The imperial petition.** Darovan comes to her unbarred house, sends his armed retinue back, and kneels with his conquered crowns laid beside his own. Namar demands her eyes as his coronation tribute; refusal will bring his fire upon the capital. Darovan offers armies and begs permission to resist. Ilessa refuses his proposed war and his assumption that he may negotiate her surrender. She determines where the encounter will happen and orders him to clear the imperial avenue before noon. The emperor obeys a woman who holds no office over him.

2. **Her arrival governs the capital.** She walks its avenue without an escort. Older gods abandon their shrines and withdraw their manifestations rather than cross her path; their priests cannot obtain the answers Darovan demands. Namar remains above the city with a suspended crown of fire and offers a road of sunlight to his high court, where she is to submit. When he sends down a gigantic burning hand to take her, she tilts her face skyward and lifts one edge of the cloth. The hand and the heavenly battalion behind it come apart. She closes her eye before her gaze can descend to the city. This is an act she chooses and a warning the High God cannot dismiss.

3. **She enters the threat.** Namar holds his fire over the capital and renews the offer of surrender. She ascends his road blindfolded, deliberately taking the confrontation above the mortal world. The elder gods desert the high court as she approaches. Namar mistakes her refusal to look down for obedience and closes the road behind her. In his throne hall she gives him one opportunity to withdraw his fire and relinquish his claim upon her. He commands her to kneel and remove the cloth with her face lowered toward the city: his intended proof that her restraint belongs to him.

4. **Heaven sees her answer.** She remains standing. With the world below and behind her line of sight, she turns toward Namar and opens both eyes. The confrontation is sustained through his attempts to flee, strike, and summon the other gods; she follows his movement through the high court, ending its armies, defenses, throne, and finally the god himself. His living hold upon the fire over the capital ends with him. The ordinary sun remains. Heaven here is this pantheon's sky palace, not the universal afterlife. Her victory comes from bringing her terrible power to bear on the authority that tried to own her, not from a mortal discovering how to operate her.

5. **The consequence belongs to her.** She covers her eyes again by choice. The surviving gods bear her down at her command rather than risk making her seek them with her gaze. Darovan waits kneeling in the imperial avenue; she passes him without accepting his throne or absolving his conquests. The ending leaves an empire whose ruler has seen what his patron could not survive, older gods surrendering the sky above her road, and the woman still freely blindfolded beneath the empty place where Heaven stood. Her mercy preserves the world; it gives no one authority over her.
