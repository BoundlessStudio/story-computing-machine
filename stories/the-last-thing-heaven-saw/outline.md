# Promised experience

The explicit promise is the kingdom's most beautiful woman, who has worn a crimson blindfold for a thousand years, is neither prisoner nor queen, commands the deference of emperors, and is avoided by gods because her uncovered eyes threaten the world. The remake direction explicitly requires keeping her “terrifying presence and authority at the center.” I infer a pleasure of mythic dread, grandeur, and discovery: learning what such a woman chooses to do, and why even immense powers must reckon with her. The user's rejection rules out allowing that stature to become scenery for a modest act of assistance. No particular relationship, origin, antagonist, viewpoint, or ending was requested; the following are proposed choices.

## Provisional direction

Approximately 4,000–5,000 words, close third person centered on the woman, Ilessa, with enough distance to preserve her terrible composure. The supplied original informs her silver-white hair, pale face, crimson cloth, and dark clothing. Her uncovered gaze unmakes what she looks upon, including anything directly behind it; she can control its direction and duration but cannot exempt an innocent thing. The cloth is her chosen safeguard. She remembers why she first tied it, and has kept that choice through the deaths of the people who asked it of her. Her motive is affection for the living world, not obedience to their successors.

Namar, a newly enthroned High God, has gained real victories over rival gods through his own force and Emperor Darovan's conquests. He mistakes Ilessa's thousand years of restraint, lack of worship, and refusal of a throne for evidence that her terrible reputation is an obsolete deterrent. Darovan knows better. Their conflict becomes an attempted seizure followed by her pursuit and judgment. It is not a contest between equal powers: the developing question is what she will permit, and what a god does when he finally believes the warning.

1. **The imperial petition.** Darovan comes to her unbarred house, dismisses his armed retinue, and kneels with his conquered crowns beside his own. Namar demands her eyes as his coronation tribute and has sent his war manifestation to collect them. Darovan offers armies. Ilessa refuses his proposed war and his assumption that he may negotiate her surrender. She orders him to clear the imperial avenue and the ancient pilgrimage stair into Heaven. His obedience is immediate. He recognizes the kingdom's most beautiful face from his dynasty's founder portrait; by touch she recognizes that founder's crown and remembers his voice. Beauty and the thousand years receive a scene, rather than remaining labels.

2. **The failed seizure changes the god's conduct.** She walks the avenue without an escort. Older gods abandon their shrines rather than cross her path. Namar sends a gigantic burning hand down from his distant mountain court to seize her. She tilts her face skyward and uncovers a sliver of one eye; the hand comes apart against the sky. She covers it again before turning toward the city. The destroyed hand is a remote manifestation, not Namar's living body: killing it cannot kill its hidden maker. He now believes the danger. He withdraws his remaining force and closes Heaven's gates instead of repeating his demand. She tells him that he may come down and relinquish his claim, or she will come to him. He retreats without answering.

3. **She chooses pursuit.** She will not leave a god who has ordered her seizure to try again from safety. She ascends the existing pilgrimage stair; no enemy supplies a convenient road. Heaven is this pantheon's physical mountain palace, not the universal afterlife. Its upper approach faces the high court with the inhabited valley behind and below, giving her a direction in which to look without exposing the world. From the avenue she could destroy a hand against the sky, but could neither see the living god beyond the mountain's rim nor look through its inhabited lower slopes. As she climbs, older gods leave their own high halls rather than receive Namar or carry him away. Their avoidance changes his available choices.

4. **Concealment, escape, judgment.** Namar closes the gates to conceal his movement toward the high causeway behind his court. She opens one eye toward the gates and the empty sky behind them, ending the barrier and exposing the attempted escape. She destroys the causeway before he reaches it. He calls the gods who once knelt to him; none will cross her gaze to convey him elsewhere. These turns remove concealment, then flight, then borrowed force. She gives him a final choice: come out without his crown and renounce his claim, or defend it. He chooses a last concentrated attack, preferring the chance of overpowering her to surrender. She remains on the outer threshold, opens both eyes toward him, and ends Namar and his high court. The ordinary sun remains. No hostage spell requires a convenient release, and no mortal discovers how to operate her.

5. **The consequence belongs to her.** She covers her eyes again. The surviving gods bear her down at her command; this is obedience, not rescue, since the pilgrimage stair remains behind her. Darovan waits kneeling in the imperial avenue. She passes without accepting his throne or absolving his conquests. The ending leaves the conquered gods refusing a new High God's crown, an emperor who has seen what his patron could not survive, and the woman still freely blindfolded beneath the empty place where Heaven stood. Her restraint preserves the world; it gives no one authority over her.
