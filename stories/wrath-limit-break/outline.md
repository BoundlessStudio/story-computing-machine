# What Wrath Leaves

Provisional direction, 2026-10-08. Close third through Raithe, an adult berserker; length and final shape remain open. The supplied original guides her long dark hair, muscular build, torn black fighting clothes, buckles and chains, red eyes, and steaming, furious transformation. Violence may be explicit when it gives the fight consequence rather than decoration.

Raithe comes to the mountain battlefield of the local war god Vask carrying her adult brother Orrel's boots, newly resoled. She has imagined their walk home so often that she has failed to ask whether he still wants it. Vask keeps bringing his fallen soldiers back into living bodies. Orrel has fought and died through years that his sister experienced only as absence. He asks for the returning to end because he sees no way to leave, but his active objective is freedom for himself and others, not death as proof that she respects him. Give the siblings a particular, ungentle affection and a remembered disagreement that remains recognizable under everything the battlefield has done to them.

Orrel is a litter bearer between the battlefield and Vask's restoration hall. He has prepared a cart of living deserters made to look like bodies awaiting restoration and a rear service route. He needs Raithe to occupy Vask while he gets the cart away. Raithe first tries to take only him without unleashing her Limit Break. He refuses that rescue before Wrath begins: these are his people, and the home she keeps offering belongs to an earlier version of him. Vask blocks their departure because his army holds a pass above inhabited country. His protection is materially real; his appetite for unquestioning soldiers and perpetual command is also real. He offers Raithe her brother whole and restored if she joins him. Neither his offer nor her original plan answers Orrel's wish.

When Vask tries to force Orrel back into service, Raithe deliberately passes her last bodily limit. Wrath is a fierce physical transformation: red eyes, heat steaming from her mouth, failing flesh driven past its possible strength. It does not automatically make her wise or discriminate on her behalf. Its unprecedented effect is discovered in action and a failed attempt at divine healing: injuries made directly by her bodily force cannot be undone. Blood can clot and flesh can scar, but severed tissue cannot be regrown and lost function cannot be divinely restored. The gods have known damaged bodies; they have never had to keep the damage. Her own injuries sustained within Wrath are equally irreversible. No universal magic or afterlife is erased; this is the bounded effect of her living power on what she actually injures.

The fight must deliver the prompt's scale. Vask meets her with the immense, embodied force of his authority; Raithe tears through it, and the first divine wound makes him improvise, bargain, and fear. At most one additional god intervenes, and only if that intervention materially changes the battle or escape. Raithe seizes and throws Vask away from the cart's road. This purposeful act exceeds what she understands: once committed, she cannot stop it, even on realizing how much of the bastion his thrown body will destroy. The collapse wrecks part of Orrel's route and cart. People die; others survive because Orrel changes the route and uses the wreck as cover. Keep space, sequence, and consequences intelligible through the destruction.

Orrel is pinned during the collapse. Raithe's attempt to pull him free, directed by Orrel, damages his leg with her own grip: the strength committed to the rescue outruns what his body can bear. The loss is permanent because she directly makes the injury, not because Wrath's effect travels invisibly through all the masonry. He lives and is not required to absolve her. Maiming Vask does not end his command or his assault: he uses his remaining strength to close the escape road on the deserters. Raithe kills him to stop that immediate attack, with permanent bodily loss of her own. His protective barrier falls; enemy shots start reaching the pass. The released soldiers must choose to cover each other's retreat, and the inhabited country below remains endangered. Exhausted Raithe has no further divine solution to offer.

The possible ending is a living, unsettled sibling bond. Orrel chooses life once he has an actual alternative to forced restoration. He can accept or alter the boots she brought without becoming either her restored childhood brother or the corpse that completes her lesson. Let the final interaction arise from their particular affection and anger, without a speech of forgiveness or a declaration of the theme. Protect the irreversible power, his consequential choices, her frightening bodily action, and the human difficulty that survives the victory; the prose may still discover a better ending.
