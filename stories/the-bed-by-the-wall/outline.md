# The Bed by the Wall

Provisional direction, not a contract for the prose.

The narrator's sixteen-year-old daughter Carys asks her to borrow a long orchard ladder and make up a bed for a girl who may arrive before morning. Her mother assumes a friend is in trouble. Then Carys produces the letters: twelve years ago fairies took the human child and left Carys, made in her exact likeness, in her place. Carys discovered the other girl three years ago through a visiting fairy's message pouch and began exchanging letters. The stolen girl calls herself Briony now. She has grown at the same rate, with the same face, but she is no longer the four-year-old her mother last saw.

The fairy household is leaving its summer dwelling tonight. Packing removes a great chest that has kept Briony from getting her whole body to a narrow high window. Over months she has reached above the chest to loosen the window's catch and learned when the upper rooms are unattended. Now she can climb through while the household loads the coaches; from outside it is too high to reach without the orchard ladder. This is the first opening the girls have found that does not require passing the guarded doors. Carys knows the dwelling's location from following the messenger. She needs her mother to help carry and steady the ladder. She has concealed the truth for three years through fear, and chosen this moment partly because the emergency gives her mother little time to consider rejecting her. This calculation is not her only motive, but it cannot be tidied away into perfect ethical foresight. The mother reacts to the lost years with a withdrawal Carys feels.

The extraction is short, physical, and frightening rather than a fairy court debate. The mother sees her daughter's face looking down from an unfamiliar height. A wooden case of bird drawings Briony has made from the high window falls from her arm back into the room while she is getting out. She is already on the unstable ladder when she tries to turn back; someone is coming upstairs, and neither helper can release the ladder to retrieve the case. She has a life and work the rescue will not recover. The girls recognize each other from years of writing but are awkward in person. The mother brings both home; this is the beginning of the central consequence, not the triumphant solution.

A story-specific limit is planted in the letters and the preflight argument: the household travels on a road that is visible only until moonset on its moving night. Its members can pursue now, but anyone who misses that road will be stranded in the human country for a year. Briony has witnessed previous departures and believes they will not risk that for her; this belief wounds her mother even as it supplies their chance. At home the mother bolts the doors, draws the shutters, and listens until moonset. This buys the first night, not eternal safety: next year's return remains a danger they will have to face. Carys has remained through twelve previous departures; her fear of being displaced concerns her mother and home, not an arbitrary new bodily expiration.

Most of the story takes place during the first night. The mother has put the spare bed beside the wall because Carys sleeps that way. She prepares food and offers habits of comfort that belong to Carys, then treats Briony's different tastes and skills as damage that needs correcting. Briony wants to keep her own name and wash by herself. She already knows from the letters that their father died; now she asks something only their mother can tell her, such as whether he asked for her at the end. The mother deliberately stops Carys answering because she thinks she owes Briony the answer herself, and wants to be the one who gives her something, denying Carys's real claim as the daughter who was present. A possible exchange: mother says he thought Briony was holding his hand; Carys says, accurately, that she was. Let the actual dialogue find its way rather than engineer it to deliver those lines. The truth that Briony was not known to be missing, and therefore was never mourned, must land. Neither girl should be uniformly generous or perfectly articulate. Carys has a defensible attachment to her place in this house; Briony resents being watched and dislikes something the two correspondents had thought they shared.

The mother reaches reflexively for Carys when both girls are distressed, and Briony sees the preference. The repair cannot be a speech claiming equal love, and Briony need not accept the offered repair tonight. The bed is a material problem rather than a promised lesson. One possible ending leaves the mother outside the girls' negotiation of their sleeping space, hearing them move the bed without her and also hearing a disagreement or compromise that the move does not solve. She may be asked for a small thing and do it without claiming an intimacy it does not grant. She can lose the first trust she expected to receive without either daughter becoming the price of the other. First-person mother voice, spare but specific, perhaps 2,500–3,500 words. The lost years and the girls' future relationship remain real.
