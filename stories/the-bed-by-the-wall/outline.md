# The Bed by the Wall

Provisional direction, not a contract for the prose.

The narrator's sixteen-year-old daughter Carys asks her to borrow a long orchard ladder and make up a bed for a girl who may arrive before morning. Her mother assumes a friend is in trouble. Then Carys produces the letters: twelve years ago fairies took the human child and left Carys, made in her exact likeness, in her place. Carys discovered the other girl three years ago through a visiting fairy's message pouch and began exchanging letters. The stolen girl calls herself Briony now. She has grown at the same rate, with the same face, but she is no longer the four-year-old her mother last saw.

The fairy household is leaving its summer dwelling tonight. Briony has found a narrow opening above its storeroom and plans to climb out while the household loads the coaches; from outside it is too high to reach without the orchard ladder. This is the first opening the girls have found that does not require passing the guarded doors. Carys knows the dwelling's location from following the messenger. She needs her mother to help carry and steady the ladder, and also because bringing Briony home without telling their mother would reproduce the first deception. The mother must move before she has made emotional sense of the news. Her initial language of rescue frightens Carys, who does not know what will happen to her afterward.

The extraction is short, physical, and frightening rather than a fairy court debate. The mother sees her daughter's face looking down from an unfamiliar height. Briony must abandon something she values to fit through the opening, and the approaching household forces the other two to hold the ladder rather than go back for it. The girls recognize each other from years of writing but are awkward in person. The mother brings both home; this is the beginning of the central consequence, not the triumphant solution.

Most of the story takes place during the first night. The mother has put the spare bed beside the wall because Carys sleeps that way. She prepares food and offers habits of comfort that belong to Carys, then catches herself treating Briony's different tastes and skills as damage that needs correcting. Briony wants to keep her own name, wash by herself, and know what became of the father she remembers. Carys, who has lived through his death, hears her mother recount it to a stranger with her face. Neither girl should be uniformly generous or perfectly articulate: Carys is possessive of a household object or ritual she has earned; Briony resents being watched and dislikes something the two correspondents had thought they shared.

The mother reaches reflexively for Carys when both girls are distressed, and Briony sees the preference. The repair cannot be a speech claiming equal love. Mother and returned daughter must survive an uncomfortable exchange in which the mother admits she does not know how Briony wants to be approached. A possible last action: the mother helps move Briony's bed away from the wall when she finally asks rather than interpreting her restlessness. Carys can help or remain turned away; the ending should allow that sore place. First-person mother voice, spare but specific, perhaps 2,500–3,500 words. The ending concerns a first small act of accurate care, with the lost years and the girls' future relationship still real.
