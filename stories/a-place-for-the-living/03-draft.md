# A Place for the Living

The key at Greer’s throat had been quiet for the whole drive. On the front steps, her thumb began tapping it against her coat button.

Imani pretended not to notice. She had brought a cake, clean shoes, and enough composure for one evening. Asking why her girlfriend’s family heirloom had developed a nervous tic seemed greedy.

Greer caught the brass key in her fist. It was old enough that its teeth had softened into brass hills. “Last chance to fake a flat tire.”

“With your mother watching us from behind the curtain?”

The curtain in the left-hand window snapped shut.

Greer winced. Imani kissed the corner of her mouth, which made her smile despite herself.

“No surprises,” Greer said. “I promise.”

Imani knew the promise had qualifications. Greer had explained kept-place suppers on their third date, after Imani had asked why every photograph of the Morrow dining room showed one chair turned toward an empty setting. The dead could visit, sometimes, if invited. They did not roam the halls, possess the cutlery, or appear in mirrors while a guest was flossing. Greer’s assurances on that point had been suspiciously specific.

“No surprises,” Imani agreed, and held up the cake tin. “Unless your family has strong feelings about ginger.”

Davina Morrow opened the door before they knocked. She was smaller than Imani had imagined and warmer in every sense: soft brown sweater, flour on one sleeve, hands that closed firmly around Imani’s cold fingers.

“Imani Crowe. At last. Come in before the rain decides to come sideways.”

The house smelled of onions browned in butter and wet stone cooling after a fire. Umbrellas crowded a brass stand. Framed portraits climbed the stair wall, but so did grocery lists, postcards, and a photograph of Greer at twelve with one eyebrow missing.

“Experiment,” Greer said when Imani paused at it.

“Necromancy?”

“Hair bleach.”

Davina laughed. “More dangerous in untrained hands.”

Greer’s father took the cake tin with the solemn care of someone accepting a peace treaty. “Homemade?”

“My mother’s recipe.”

“Then I’m hiding the last piece before anyone else knows there is one.”

He meant it kindly. The word *mother* did not catch in the room or summon a hush. Imani let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

Dinner began pleasantly enough that she was embarrassed by how thoroughly she had rehearsed disaster.

Greer’s father had made a mushroom pie whose crust shattered at the touch of a fork. Davina remembered that Imani disliked sweet wine and had found something dry. Greer told the eyebrow story properly, with gestures, and Imani laughed hard enough to snort into her glass. When Davina asked about Imani’s work assessing old houses, her questions were interested rather than forensic. Had she ever found a staircase resting on nothing? Yes. Had the owner insisted it was historical? Also yes.

“A house can be old or it can be badly maintained,” Imani said. “Age is not a repair strategy.”

Greer’s father lifted his glass. “I’ve been saying that about my knees.”

Even Davina’s correction when Imani reached to shift a spoon was gentle.

“A guest never rearranges a Morrow place setting,” she said, nudging it back into line. “Old household rule.”

“Noted.”

There were five settings.

Imani had assumed the extra belonged to a late arrival, although the fifth plate was wrapped in black linen and the chair before it stood a handspan farther from the table. An ivory place card lay facedown beside a black-handled butter knife. Greer had not looked directly at either object since sitting down.

Davina waited until the pie plates were cleared. Then she unfolded the linen.

The plate beneath was thin white porcelain painted with a border of green moths. The room seemed to contract around it.

“We have a family guest,” Davina said.

The key struck Greer’s glass.

Imani looked at her. “A surprise?”

“Not exactly.” Greer’s fingers closed around the key again. “It wasn’t certain she would accept.”

Which was not an answer, but Davina had turned over the card. In narrow blue handwriting it read *Aunt Zephra*.

Beside the familiar name, the butter knife looked worn nearly silver at the tip.

“It was hers,” Davina said. “She used it every Sunday for forty years. In our practice, the familiar name directs the invitation. The object helps it reach the person intended. Nothing grander than that.”

“And she can say no?” Imani asked.

“Of course,” Davina said.

Greer met Imani’s eyes. Regret was already there, asking to be mistaken for apology.

Davina placed both palms flat on the table. “Aunt Zephra Morrow, you are remembered here. Your place is kept, if you choose it.”

For three breaths, nothing happened.

On the fourth, the candles leaned toward the fifth setting. Davina’s shoulders tightened. A smear like condensation formed above the chair, gathered color, and became the suggestion of a woman sitting very straight.

She arrived in details. A dark collar. Long brown hands. Silver hair rolled at the nape of her neck. Her face took longest, as if distance resisted expression. Then her mouth sharpened into amusement.

“You’ve put me in the severe dress again,” she said.

Her voice had a soft double edge, one sound in the room and another at the bottom of Imani’s hearing.

Davina smiled with naked relief. “It’s how you present.”

“It’s how you remember.” The woman looked down at herself. “Fortunately, death has cured me of vanity. Mostly.”

Greer laughed, and for a moment she was younger than the photograph on the stairs. “Hello, Aunt Zephra.”

“There you are.” Aunt Zephra reached across the narrow boundary of her setting. Greer offered two fingers. The dead woman touched them, and Greer inhaled as though she had put her hand into cold water. “Still wearing that solemn little key.”

“Still.”

Aunt Zephra Morrow had been dead for seventy-three years. She was not transparent, exactly. The wallpaper showed through her only when Imani looked away from her face. Her chair creaked beneath movements that were entirely her own, though the substance that made those movements possible came from Davina’s rigid hands and slowly paling lips.

Aunt Zephra turned to Imani. “And you must be the structural realist.”

“I didn’t realize I’d been briefed.”

“Davina sends news with every invitation. Otherwise I’d spend my one visit between remembrances asking who had died, which is dreary even by our standards.”

“Do you—” Imani stopped. Every possible ending to the question felt rude.

“Remember being dead? Yes. Remember being alive? Also yes. I have not been remade from Davina’s recollections, if that is what your eyebrows are asking. I am myself there, and I am myself during this visit, and I expect to remain myself when I return.”

Davina’s breath whitened briefly above the table. “Twenty minutes is comfortable. We should not waste them explaining what she already understands.”

“I like her eyebrows,” Aunt Zephra said. “They ask permission before trespassing.”

That won Imani despite her anger. So did the way Aunt Zephra squeezed Greer’s fingers once before letting go, as if contact mattered too much to prolong carelessly.

The cake came out. Greer’s father cut Aunt Zephra a slender slice and set it inside the ring of her cutlery. Her fork moved in her translucent hand. Davina supplied the impossible contact; Aunt Zephra supplied the eagerness.

She tasted one bite and shut her eyes.

“Ginger,” she said. “And pear. Oh, that is unfairly good.”

“My mother’s,” Imani said.

“Then I am glad she made something that traveled farther than either of you expected.”

Imani looked down before gratitude showed too plainly. When she looked up, Aunt Zephra was studying her, not as an examiner but as another woman who understood what a recipe could carry.

“You’re a Morrow by marriage?” Imani asked.

“By invitation, not by blood. The distinction used to matter in a kinder way.” Aunt Zephra pointed her fork at the green moths on the plate. “I painted those. Badly.”

“You established the supper,” Davina said.

“Helped. A kept place was hospitality. The living offered a chair; the dead decided whether to sit. We argued, ate what we could, and went away when the guest was tired.”

She looked toward the two dessert plates Greer’s father had cleared and placed facedown in a stack on the sideboard. Then she took another bite of cake.

For several minutes, it was wonderful.

Aunt Zephra wanted to know whether buildings still hid pipes inside walls as if ashamed of them. She told Imani about a kitchen ceiling that had collapsed into a funeral supper because three generations had agreed not to inspect a stain. Davina supplied one dry correction, Greer supplied another, and Aunt Zephra dismissed both on the grounds that neither had been born.

Imani could almost forgive the ambush. Almost.

Then Davina said, “Tell her about the house survey.”

Greer’s smile faltered. “There isn’t much to tell.”

“Imani examines old houses before people buy them,” Davina told Aunt Zephra. “Useful experience, if she and Greer live here.”

The last word landed with less noise than a fork. It did more damage.

Imani turned to Greer. “Here?”

“Someday,” Greer said. “Maybe. It was only a conversation.”

“A conversation with whom?”

Greer rubbed the key between finger and thumb.

Davina answered. “With me. This house passes with the table.”

Aunt Zephra set down her fork. “Does it.”

“The person who keeps the place must live here,” Davina continued. Her voice remained hospitable; only its purpose had changed. “Greer has known that all her life.”

Imani had known Greer wanted a house with deep windowsills. She knew Greer hated moving, loved burnt toast, and had once spent six months deciding whether to buy a blue sofa. They had discussed children in the cautious hypothetical. Marriage in the laughing hypothetical. They had not discussed taking responsibility for a dining room where the dead arrived by appointment.

“You told me your mother might want to meet me,” Imani said quietly.

“She does,” Greer said.

“You did not mention the property evaluation.”

“It isn’t like that.”

Davina’s expression tightened with something closer to hurt than offense. “I would not hand this table to someone who thought our family was an entertaining eccentricity.”

“I don’t.”

“Then you understand that wonder is the smallest part of the work.”

Aunt Zephra said, “Davina.”

But Davina had the momentum of someone opening a door she had held shut for years.

“There are records to keep. Invitations to prepare. People who say they respect the practice until remembrance falls on a work night, or the room will not warm, or grief is tedious instead of picturesque. I will not leave Greer alone with that.”

Her fear was real. Imani could see its grooves in the house: the polished table, the indexed place cards on the cabinet, the butter knife kept safe across seventy-three years. Davina had spent her adult life preserving doors that opened only briefly. Love had not made her less controlling. Control had not made the love false.

Imani looked at Greer. “Did you tell her we talked about marriage?”

Greer went still.

Davina said, “You have been together eighteen months.”

“That was not my question.”

“Yes,” Greer said. “I told her it might be where we were going.”

Heat climbed Imani’s throat. “You told your mother before you told me?”

“I was trying to work out how to tell you all of it.”

“By arranging a dead relative to ask?”

“I didn’t arrange this.”

“You knew.”

Greer’s hand fell from the key. “I knew it was possible.”

There it was: *no surprises*, trimmed small enough to crawl through.

Aunt Zephra leaned toward Imani. She could not move beyond the reach of her place, and the effort made the edge of her sleeve fray into mist. “For whatever it is worth, I accepted an invitation to dinner. I was not told I would be interviewing anyone.”

Davina’s white breath came faster. “I asked you to meet the woman Greer may marry.”

“Meeting is not measuring.”

“You measured me.”

“I asked whether you loved this family when you took the table. You said yes. I was too sentimental to ask whether you loved yourself outside it.”

Davina flinched. Greer’s father reached toward her, stopped, then folded his hands. His earlier warmth had not vanished. It had simply proved unequal to conflict.

Davina turned back to Imani. “Would you marry Greer?”

“That is a question Greer should ask me.”

“Do you want children?”

“Also not yours.”

“Would you move here?”

Imani looked around at the moth plate, the rain-dark window, the sideboard stacked with generations of intimate obligation. “I would have wanted to see it before deciding.”

“You are seeing it.”

“As an audition.”

Greer said, “That’s enough,” so softly that it functioned as a request Davina could deny.

“No,” Davina said. Her fingers trembled against the cloth. “Not if Greer expects to take that key. I need to know whether I am preserving this for someone who will abandon it.”

“I have not asked Greer to abandon anything.”

“You may, once you understand the cost.”

The room was warm, but Davina’s skin had gone gray around her mouth. Frost feathered the bowl of her wineglass where her wrist touched it.

Greer noticed. “You’re past twenty minutes.”

“I am fine.”

“Close the place.”

Davina looked at Aunt Zephra. “You have not answered.”

“Because I do not give warranties on human beings.”

“You gave me your judgment before.”

“And you have been punishing everyone with it ever since.”

Aunt Zephra glanced again at the sideboard. The cleared dessert plates lay face-down, harmless and finished. Then she flexed her hands as if feeling had become difficult.

“Davina,” she said, and all humor had left the doubled voice. “Close my place, please.”

Davina’s eyes shone. “One answer. Is she fit for this family?”

“I asked to leave.”

“You may not come again until after the next remembrance.”

“I know where I am going.”

Davina’s hands pressed harder into the table. The candle flames bent almost flat. She could hold the visit because she was alive and willing to spend her own heat on it. Aunt Zephra could object, but she could not cast herself free.

Greer stood halfway, the key swinging loose. “Please.”

Davina looked at her daughter, exhausted and pleading. “Sit down.”

Greer did.

The small surrender hurt Imani more than the hidden house, perhaps because she understood it. Greer had spent her life learning which battles could be survived by becoming quiet. Imani had spent hers learning to smile during inspections where owners called cracks decorative. Composure was useful. It was not courage.

She pushed her chair back.

Davina’s head snapped toward her. “Do not touch that setting. A guest who breaks a kept place rejects the table.”

“Imani,” Greer said.

That one word held fear for Aunt Zephra, fear of Davina, fear that Imani would walk out and make every concealed consequence real.

Imani stepped to the fifth chair. Up close, Aunt Zephra’s outline shivered. She looked tired, furious, and entirely present.

“I want to hear it from you,” Imani said. “Do you want to go?”

“Yes.”

Davina rose too quickly. The room tilted through her; Greer’s father caught her elbow.

“This is the only visit she can make this year,” Davina said. “If you turn that plate, it is over. Dinner, her visit, and any claim you imagine you have here.”

Imani’s fingers hovered over the porcelain. She thought of the cake made from a dead woman’s recipe, offered freely. She thought of Greer discussing marriage in this room while giving Imani only hypotheticals. She thought of how easy it would be to sit down and call endurance love.

“I don’t want a claim,” she said. “I want a choice.”

She turned the plate face-down.

Porcelain struck wood with a flat domestic knock.

The working broke.

Cold rushed outward. The candles straightened. Aunt Zephra thinned from the edges inward, silver hair and severe dress loosening into bright condensation. Davina’s hands left the table; color began returning to them almost at once.

For one heartbeat, Aunt Zephra’s face remained clear.

“No verdict,” she said. “A kept place welcomes a guest. It does not keep one.”

Her gaze moved to Greer.

“Make room for the living while you have them.”

Then the chair was empty.

No one spoke. Rain ticked at the window. On the overturned plate, the green moths were hidden against the table.

Davina sat down carefully. Grief altered her face more than anger could. “Do you understand what you ended?”

“Yes,” Imani said. Her voice shook now that courage had become consequence. “She did too.”

“You had no right.”

“Neither did you.”

Greer stood. This time, she did not stop halfway.

Davina looked at her. “If she rejects the table’s rules, she cannot share its care. You cannot inherit this house and make the practice answer to whoever you bring home.”

“Then I won’t inherit it.”

The words frightened Greer as she said them. Imani saw the pulse jumping at her throat, just above the key.

Davina stared. “You do not mean that.”

Greer pulled the chain over her head. “If keeping the table means ambushing the woman I love and refusing a guest who asks to leave, I don’t want to keep it.”

She laid the key beside Aunt Zephra’s butter knife.

It made almost no sound. Still, Greer’s father closed his eyes.

Davina’s hands curled in her lap. “Without that key, you do not prepare invitations after me. You do not choose the remembrances. If someone else keeps the table, you come when they invite you, like any other relative.”

Greer looked at the empty chair. Her face crumpled, then steadied. “I understand.”

Imani believed that she did. Greer was giving up not an antique but a future: this house, the authority to call beloved dead, the certainty of her place in a lineage. The choice did not repair what she had hidden. It cost enough to be real anyway.

“I should have told Imani before tonight,” Greer said. “About the interview. The house. The key. All of it.”

Davina looked older with her heat returning. “I was trying to protect what your family entrusted to me.”

“Aunt Zephra was our family too.”

“I know.”

Davina said it so quietly that Imani almost missed it.

* * *

Outside, the rain had thinned to a cold mist. Imani stood under the porch eave with her coat unbuttoned and did not take Greer’s offered hand.

Greer let it fall.

“No surprises,” Imani said.

“I lied by making the promise smaller in my head.” Greer swallowed. “I knew she might be invited. I knew my mother meant to judge you. I knew taking the key meant living here someday, and I talked to her about marriage because talking to you felt like I could lose something.”

“So you let me find out at the table.”

“Yes. I’m sorry. Not for failing to manage tonight. For deceiving you.”

The clean shape of the apology hurt. Imani loved her. That remained true, annoyingly untouched by wisdom.

“Leaving the key doesn’t make us fine.”

“I know.”

“I don’t know whether we’re fine.”

Greer nodded once. “I know that too.”

The front door opened. Davina held out the cake tin. It was washed, dried, and noticeably lighter.

“He hid two pieces,” she said. “I overruled him.”

Imani accepted it.

Davina kept one hand on the door. “If you come again, no extra place will be set without asking you first.”

It was not an apology. It was not forgiveness either. It was one boundary, offered late.

“Thank you,” Imani said.

Davina glanced toward Greer, then closed the door.

At the car, Greer waited without reaching for her.

“Breakfast,” Imani said. “Saturday. Somewhere aggressively ordinary.”

Hope moved across Greer’s face, cautious enough not to offend. “Living-only?”

“Living-only. We talk about the house. Marriage. Children. The table. Everything you tried to save by not saying it.”

“And after that?”

“After that, we decide whether we can make a place for each other.”

Imani got into the car with the cake tin on her knees. Greer took the driver’s seat, keyless now, and for a while they listened to the rain.
