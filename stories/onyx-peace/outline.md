# Provisional direction, revised after outline discussion

Onyx comes to the coast where her people once lived, after a century of war, because she wants to learn to swim there now. Her stone body will carry her along the bottom but cannot float. She commissions a local boatmaker to help her feel the surface hold her. The gold seam on her shoulder is famous as the mark of a victory, and the boatmaker initially treats her as the woman who saved the harbor. Onyx still wears the splendid armor from the reference image, though she is trying to decide what she would wear if nobody needed to recognize her.

The boatmaker's sister is listed as missing from a prison ship wrecked in that victory. She asks Onyx to walk down to the ship and bring up proof of who was aboard. Onyx knows the wreck is reachable by her body; she also knows what the gold seam actually records. She saw prisoners on the vessel and ordered it sunk anyway to stop an enemy advance. Retrieving an object cannot settle that choice. The boatmaker wants an answer about her sister, not a lesson in Onyx's exhaustion. Onyx must tell her exactly what she knew and sign a first-person account that can be taken to the families. This costs her the boatmaker's budding trust in a visible scene, even if the boatmaker finishes the flotation aid she promised to make. Neither the account nor the aid is forgiveness.

The ending may leave the wreck untouched and the response to Onyx's account unresolved. Onyx returns to the water for the first difficult moments of floating, using help she asked for rather than following another command. Her people's choice to become stone rather than kneel remains a courageous refusal of conquest; Onyx's problem is the century of war that taught her to confuse freely accepting help with surrender. Swimming must remain a pleasure she wants in the present, not a cure or a tidy recovery of who she was before stone. The prose can find a better route or ending.
