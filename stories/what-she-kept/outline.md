# Provisional direction: The Last Thing Heaven Saw

The request explicitly gives us a thousand-year-old, extraordinarily beautiful woman, a crimson blindfold, freedom from both captivity and queenship, kneeling emperors, evasive gods, and eyes whose opening endangers the world. I infer a promised pleasure of mythic dread becoming a consequential revelation, with an intimate view of the person exercising that restraint. The scale is imperial and divine. The central relationship I propose is between the woman and an aging emperor she has known since he was a child: she has watched nothing of his life, yet knows him through its changing voice. The payoff should be an actual use of terrifying power, a mortal act that matters beside it, and survival with an irreversible mark. These are editorial choices, not added user requirements.

Close third person through **Ivara**, approximately 3,500–4,500 words. The prose can be stately and sensory without withholding ordinary directness. Her silver-white hair gathered high, pale face, dark gown, and broad crimson blindfold with trailing ends follow the supplied original. Her beauty is real, not a glamour or the hidden explanation for her power. Everyone knows her face; she has seen none of the people she has come to love during the last thousand years.

Ivara's uncovered gaze brings things she sees to their ends: living things die, stone becomes dust, a god can finally cease. The ending spreads outward from the first thing she sees into the rest of her vision. She cannot select the innocent out of it. While the gaze is active, her body goes rigid, including her eyelids; she cannot close them or lift a cloth. The first time, her mother came from behind and covered her face with a crimson sash. The mother survived and later aged normally. The valley was left a pale, lifeless hollow. Ivara can remember faces from before her power appeared, but has seen no living face since. She replaces the blindfold's worn fabric and always keeps its first color. The cloth has no agency; it blocks her living power. For a thousand years she has worn it voluntarily and traveled the inhabited kingdom. Emperors kneel in recognition that their conquests endure only because she withholds her gaze. Gods keep their actual bodies away from her.

- **The petition.** Emperor **Daret**, whom she remembers as an inquisitive boy, kneels before her in a palace shaking under distant thunder. He asks her to end the war god **Orvek**. Daret has broken with the god's demand for recurring wars and human tribute, after long participating in them. Orvek is breaking his former empire rather than allowing that refusal to stand. He still has worshippers and real power elsewhere. Ivara refuses to look toward a battlefield or populated city. Establish the old submission rite here: on the ridge altar above the city, a hooded crown-bearer hands the crown to the kneeling ruler; Orvek receives it directly while setting his own hand on the ruler's neck. Daret has performed the rite before. It is Orvek's cherished form of visible dominion and the explicit surrender he has demanded again, not a universal divine mechanism.
- **The world she has kept.** She goes among the people leaving the city, hearing the scale of the flight and recognizing the emperor's childhood questions in a frightened child. Her attachment is to this changing, unfinished world. Daret remembers his granddaughter's face; Ivara has only voices and touch for those she has come to love. She may want to see his face without being tempted to kill him to do it. The earlier valley and her mother's covering hand become a concrete warning through memory and the present landscape. Her reason to risk the opening is the people already being killed by the god; agreement does not absolve Daret of the wars he fought.
- **Choosing the ground.** Ivara proposes using the demanded rite. Orvek's scouts report her leaving by the western road among the refugees, where his storm continues to keep its distance. Beyond their view she turns onto the old quarry path and rejoins Daret on the ridge; he guides her over the last rough ground. She takes the customary hooded crown-bearer's place. The altar is visible on the city's skyline to the army and the rear of the refugee column behind them. In front lies her dead valley, then the far wooded slopes, where the road curves around the hollow and the leading refugees are already climbing. Orvek comes to take the emperor's crown and submission into his own hands before those witnesses. His knowledge of her departure, the familiar rite, and the expected covered attendant make this vulnerability credible; his personal appetite brings him close. The trap does not depend on an oath making him mindless.
- **The opening.** Daret already has the second opaque cloth. As Orvek reaches for his crown and neck, Ivara pulls hood and blindfold clear in one movement and opens her eyes. There is no interval in which she poses revealed while he could simply strike her. He recognizes her and tries to rise away, but the ending has caught his living body. Ivara goes rigid. Daret moves behind her shoulder with the replacement cloth raised, outside her sight, and must judge when to cover her. The immense god and his storm break apart across the sky. As his body dissolves, the living slopes and their road become visible beyond him; the ending also spreads toward the edges of Ivara's vision. Daret's courage and timing carry the tension. No extra revenge temptation is needed. The altar and the ridge's crown are destroyed, permanently breaking the city's familiar skyline. Preserve the full grandeur and the terrifying possibility of more.
- **After.** The god is gone. The surviving army and refugees can see the broken skyline and the empty air where he stood. Daret publicly credits Ivara's choice; no claim that propaganda has become impossible is necessary. She has briefly seen the being who died, not the people she saved. The gods' subsequent absence can be felt without settling an entire pantheon's future. When Daret kneels again, she recognizes the movement by his failing knees and offers him her hand to rise; establish that sound earlier. He guides her down the changed mountain toward the actual people below, whose frightened silence begins breaking into voices. End with their living sound rather than an explanation of the theme.

The principal risks remain the credibility of Orvek's bodily approach and the external covering. Establish the rite, his knowledge of her departure, the first closer, and her bodily rigidity before the climax. One intelligible experience should carry the covering rule; the story need not disprove every possible apparatus. The route remains provisional if prose or discussion finds a stronger cause or ending.
