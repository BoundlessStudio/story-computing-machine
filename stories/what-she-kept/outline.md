# Provisional direction: The Last Thing Heaven Saw

The request explicitly gives us a thousand-year-old, extraordinarily beautiful woman, a crimson blindfold, freedom from both captivity and queenship, kneeling emperors, evasive gods, and eyes whose opening endangers the world. I infer a promised pleasure of mythic dread becoming a consequential revelation, with an intimate view of the person exercising that restraint. The scale is imperial and divine. The central relationship I propose is between the woman and an aging emperor she has known since he was a child: she has watched nothing of his life, yet knows him through its changing voice. The payoff should be an actual use of terrifying power, a mortal act that matters beside it, and survival with an irreversible mark. These are editorial choices, not added user requirements.

Close third person through **Ivara**, approximately 3,500–4,500 words. The prose can be stately and sensory without withholding ordinary directness. Her silver-white hair, pale face, dark gown, and broad crimson blindfold follow the supplied original. Her beauty is real, not a glamour or the hidden explanation for her power.

Ivara's uncovered gaze brings things she sees to their ends: living things die, stone becomes dust, a god can finally cease. Its field spreads as her eyes take in more of the world. She cannot select the innocent out of it. Once the light and the power catch, she cannot reliably close her own eyes; someone must cover them from outside. The cloth has no agency. It simply blocks her living power. A brief opening a thousand years earlier destroyed the valley where she lived, leaving a pale, lifeless hollow. Since then she has kept the blindfold voluntarily and traveled the inhabited kingdom. Emperors kneel in recognition that their conquests endure only because she continues to withhold her gaze. Gods keep their actual bodies away from her.

- **The petition.** Emperor **Daret**, whom she remembers as an inquisitive boy, kneels before her in a palace shaking under distant thunder. He asks her to end the war god **Orvek**. Daret has broken with the god's demand for recurring wars and human tribute. Orvek is now breaking his former empire rather than allowing that refusal to stand. The god still has worshippers and real power elsewhere; destroying a temple or issuing a decree will not dispose of him. Ivara refuses to look toward a battlefield or populated city. Daret has brought this request because armies have failed, not because his crown grants authority over her.
- **The world she has kept.** She goes among the people leaving the city, hearing the scale of the flight and recognizing the emperor's childhood questions in a frightened child. Her attachment is to this changing, unfinished world, not an abstract obligation imposed by the gods. Daret can remember the face of his granddaughter; Ivara cannot remember the faces of any of the people she has come to love. A god-killing gaze has never helped her keep one mortal person alive forever. The earlier valley is a concrete warning, revealed through memory and the present landscape rather than a lecture about the power.
- **Choosing the ground.** Ivara proposes the confrontation herself. Orvek attacks from within a moving crown of storm, keeping the crowded lowlands between his body and her gaze. They climb beyond that cover to the bare ridge above her dead valley, while the army protects the refugees' retreat. Daret lets the god believe he has come alone to submit. Orvek wants the living emperor's public capitulation, then his death: a mortal refusal must not become an example to the kingdoms still worshipping him. Ivara waits hooded beside Daret as an apparent attendant. The god is formidable rather than omniscient; he has avoided her known road, and recognizes this chosen meeting too late.
- **The opening.** Ivara reveals her face and gives Daret a second opaque cloth. He must cover her eyes when the god is destroyed, before her gaze widens into the world beyond him. They have no guarantee about the moment, and Daret has every reason to want an extra instant of annihilation for the enemy who devastated his people. Orvek tries to flee upward. Ivara opens her eyes. His immense living body, storm, weapons, and authority cannot save him from an ending. Let the destruction have actual sky-filling scale, with the ridge and empty valley paying a permanent cost. Daret, sheltered below her field of sight, puts the cloth over her eyes at the crucial instant. His part is an action requiring courage and restraint, not a speech that persuades a god.
- **After.** The god is gone. The scar can be seen from the surviving city; his worshippers must live with his defeat, and the emperor cannot sell the victory as proof that he commands Ivara. She has briefly seen the being who died, not the people she saved. Daret takes her hand to guide her down the changed mountain. When he kneels again, she recognizes the movement by his failing knees and offers him her hand to rise. The sound rising from the living lowlands, rather than an explanation of the theme, closes the story.

The principal risk is that dependence on another person could feel like a contrived limit placed on an otherwise convenient weapon. The draft must establish the uncontrolled opening and the first valley's destruction before relying on them, make Orvek's choice to approach credible, and give the climactic act enough uncertainty and consequence to earn its scale. The route remains provisional if prose or discussion finds a stronger cause or ending.
