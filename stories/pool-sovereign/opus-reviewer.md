This story is an exception to the collection's usual dialogue problem. Madelon
and Hewlin do not trade legalistic corrections. She talks like a queen who
has spent her life asking questions and being answered ("Please stay where you
are", "May I hold on to this?", "I shall need some clothes"). He answers in
modern, hesitant sentences, apologises, brings up his dad and his aunt, and
refuses things flatly ("I don't want to speak"). The story notices this
imbalance itself (L139) and then lets it shift. The mourners each remember a
different small encounter (a fruit cake, a glove, a grievance about a theatre)
and sound like different people. They do not act as a chorus explaining the
queen. The final exchange is action, not a statement of theme. The edit pass
should change very little. Make the one pretext exchange at L167–L173 legible
in the moment, keep "I knew it" for the climax, and leave the rhythm of the
rest alone.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · Pretext at the decision beat decodes only through narration

- Where: L167–L175, third evening on the bench, just before "I'd like to go"
- Text:
> “Is it one of these three?” I held up the program. “They've only printed the first lines.”
> He looked. “The second one. They've put down the proper title.”
> I knew the title. I had known it all along. I simply wanted to keep talking about the evening, close enough to the question that he might ask it for me.
> All this time I had been thinking of my teacher and the closed music book, and I knew it.
- Problem: Ground/referent, plus narration doing the decoding. Madelon's claim that only first lines are printed is contradicted by Hewlin's reply about "the proper title". Until the narration explains, a reader cannot tell whether the claim is a pretext or a continuity slip. She has read this sheet for two evenings, and the text never shows that the question is a feint. Then two soft referents fall in one sentence at the story's hinge: "the evening" reads as the evening they are in (the scene opened "On the third evening"), not the memorial, and "the question" only resolves once she speaks at L175. Finally, "I had known it all along" uses up the phrasing of the climax at L249 ("and I knew it") on a trivial fact.
- Fix: Keep the feint, since her pride stopping her from asking outright is in character, but make it visible in the moment. Either cut "They've only printed the first lines", or show her already knowing (her thumb on the title, or Hewlin noticing that she has had the sheet for days). Replace "the evening" and "the question" with concrete nouns (the memorial; his inviting her). Drop "I had known it all along" so the knowing-without-knowing idea belongs to L249 alone. Illustrative only: “Is it one of these three?” I asked, with my thumb already under the second title.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Idiom collision in the snow sentence

- Where: L125, first evening, after "It was easier to write that than to arrange a fortnight."
- Text:
> Said aloud, it would have sounded like a poor excuse for something I had loved.
- Problem: "A poor excuse for X" is an idiom meaning a poor example of X, so the sentence first reads as "an inferior version of something I loved". It can also be read as "a poor excuse for leaving something I loved". The intended meaning (the snow would sound like a flimsy excuse, though she truly loved it) comes through only on a second pass, at a moment of quiet confession.
- Fix: Recast so that "excuse for" does not trigger the idiom and the thing she loved is clearly the snow and the pool. Illustrative only: I could not have said it aloud without the snow sounding like an excuse.

### P2 · Minor · Closing sentences that explain what the paragraph already showed

- Where: L79 (morning after the recognition) and L235 (the theatre grievance at the memorial)
- Text:
> There had been good afternoons. I had not needed to forget them to be happy here.
> I remembered the minister's carpet. Explaining would not have given her another play.
- Problem: In each paragraph the concrete images already make the point: the peacocks and the carpet set against the paper snow. The final sentence then states the lesson. L235 is the clearer case, because it turns a lovely held-back moment into a neat moral.
- Fix: Cut "Explaining would not have given her another play." and end on the carpet. At L79, consider cutting the second sentence or making it more specific to her, so it does not read as the story's thesis. Leave L139 ("as though he were still standing outside a door") and L221 ("I hoped I had") as they are. Those are her realisations, not glosses.

### P3 · Minor · Mixed British and American spelling

- Where: throughout; e.g. L89, L99, L229, L245
- Text:
> The paper was a program for the local gathering.
> “You practised a lot.”
> When they closed the little theatre, she should have said something.
> In the second verse she welcomed a traveler; in the third she hid his boots beneath the bed.
- Problem: "practised" and "theatre" are British, while "program" (9 occurrences, L89–L259) and "traveler" are American. The vocabulary (fortnight, pavement, bathing costume, cardigan, plectrum) is British, so the American forms stand out.
- Fix: Choose one system and apply it throughout. Given the vocabulary, "programme" and "traveller" are the smaller change. The -ize forms (organizing, organizer, apologized) are acceptable Oxford spelling if kept consistent.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Arrival at the hall is out of order, and the flowers move

- Where: L199–L209, arrival at the hall above the bed shop
- Text:
> Then I was annoyed because my chair rocked whenever I shifted my weight, and for several minutes I thought chiefly about that.
> “Well, sit anywhere. There's tea already, if you'd rather not wait.”
> an infant slept through the whole observance in a basket on the table intended for flowers
> When everyone had settled, she stood by the flowers and read the official account.
- Problem: Madelon has been sitting on a rocking chair "for several minutes" before the organizer greets them and tells them to "sit anywhere". The basket is on "the table intended for flowers", yet the organizer then stands "by the flowers" without our being told where they ended up. Both are small staging slips that make the reader re-picture the room.
- Fix: Move the L201 paragraph ("I had expected more…") to after the organizer's welcome (after L207), or have the organizer come to them once they are seated and drop "sit anywhere". Give the flowers a place in half a clause (on the floor, on a chair), or have her stand "by the table". Keep "I had time to wish I had taken tea" (L243), which depends on the tea offer.
