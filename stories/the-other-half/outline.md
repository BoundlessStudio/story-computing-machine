# The Other Half

Provisional direction: a contained aftermath, close third person through Dara Vale, seventeen, about 2,500–3,000 words. Dara and Kit Danner have been together long enough for private jokes and familiar touches, but their earlier disagreements have always dissolved into one of those. This one cannot.

Dara is Racket. She can send destructive vibrations into things she touches; she must choose their direction and cannot call back a released blow. Her tabloid reputation has some truth behind it. Alongside the rescues, she has wrecked things out of anger, including something she had no heroic excuse to wreck. Kit, Hush, normally holds an object or a person's voluntary movement still within sight. That requires sustained attention and is temporary. His quiet, patient sweetness is real.

Their nasty recurring enemy, Switchjack, coerces other people's bodies. They have captured him before, and he has escaped and hurt people again. Tonight Kit has killed him while Dara was getting the last captives away. The killing is definitive and non-graphic. It was a choice, not an involuntary accident or a secret last-second necessity. Further temporary restraint and another attempt at secure custody were possible; neither teen can promise that custody would finally work.

After the emergency responders arrive, Kit takes Dara to the shopping-centre photo booth where they had meant to end their interrupted date. The booth has been their private place to look ridiculous together without having the pictures sold. He makes some small, ordinary provision for her as usual. She has understood that Switchjack is dead but not yet that Kit deliberately killed him. His attempt to return them to familiar gentleness brings that fact into the open.

What Dara wants first is an explanation that will put her boyfriend back where he was. Kit wants her to understand that there will be no next victim and, especially, no next time Switchjack makes Dara's body do something she has refused. He believes he has ended a danger they have been asking other people to keep surviving. Dara's opposition to killing is complete. Her involuntary relief at being free of this enemy makes that opposition more painful, not weaker. She also has to hear that her own reckless damage has frightened Kit; he has his own memory of being unable to stop her, and her no-killing rule does not cancel what she owes him for that.

The argument moves through failed comfort, wounded interruption, particulars of their shared history, and the discovery that love has concealed rather than resolved this difference. Neither gives a polished ethical address. Kit's tenderness survives beside his choice, and he does not immediately adopt Dara's position to keep her. Dara remains angry and can hurt him with words without secretly wanting the enemy alive only in theory.

Possible ending: an ordinary touch becomes unavailable between them. They cannot finish the date or decide their whole relationship tonight. One offers or accepts a small act of care without treating it as forgiveness. The photo strip, if it appears, records a real change rather than supplying a lesson. Prose may find a more immediate stopping point.
