# The Other Half

Provisional direction: a contained aftermath, close third person through Dara Vale, seventeen, about 2,500–3,000 words. Dara and Kit Danner have been together long enough for private jokes and familiar touches, but their earlier disagreements have always dissolved into one of those. This one cannot.

Dara is Racket. She can send destructive vibrations into things she touches; she must choose their direction and cannot call back a released blow. Her tabloid reputation has some truth behind it. After being barred from a school excursion for absences, some heroic and some her own fault, she smashed a trophy case. Kit tried to still her wrist too late; her blow had already left her, and he needed his eyebrow glued. Kit, Hush, ordinarily holds an object or a person's bodily movements still within sight while leaving the person alive. This works on self-directed or coerced movement, requires sustained attention, and is temporary. His quiet, patient sweetness is real. Both teens already know each other's power can kill: they heard the same training warning. They have never discussed when either might choose to do so.

Their nasty recurring enemy, Switchjack, coerces other people's bodies. They have captured him before, and he has escaped and hurt people again. Tonight he made Dara begin a blow toward people she was protecting. Kit stopped her hand; the interruption let Dara get the people clear. Kit then killed Switchjack by choosing a complete stillness beyond ordinary restraint, described without anatomy or a usable technique. It was a choice, not an involuntary accident or a secret last-second necessity. Maintaining the temporary hold until the capture team arrived was possible; neither teen can promise that custody would finally work. Fresh fear, anger, and the conviction that there must not be a next escape drive Kit's choice.

Responders have confirmed the death and asked the teens to wait in the evacuated shopping-centre food court for statements and parents. Kit takes Dara into the nearby photo booth, their familiar date-ending hideout, and partly closes its curtain. Here they could look ridiculous together without having the pictures sold. He makes some small, ordinary provision for her as usual. The medics have his name and know his power caused the death; an officer is coming for his full account, which he intends to give. Establish that early: he seeks Dara's understanding, not an alibi. Dara knows the enemy is dead but assumes an accident. When she asks if the hold slipped, Kit says it did not.

What Dara wants first is an explanation that will put her boyfriend back where he was. When that fails, she asks him to promise he will never kill again, clumsily offering to stop wrecking things herself. Kit does not trust her promise and will not make one he disbelieves. His real hurt over the trophy case becomes an unfair reply; the narrative will not equate the harms or let Dara's refusal cancel what she owes for her own act. He asks her to admit she is glad Switchjack is dead. Her involuntary relief makes her complete opposition to killing more painful, not weaker. She is glad Kit stopped her hand; she did not authorize him to make killing the answer to what happened to her.

The argument moves through failed comfort and wounded interruption. Neither gives a polished ethical address. Kit's tenderness survives beside his choice; Dara's anger does not conceal a wish that someone else would kill for her. Love has concealed rather than resolved their difference. Dara refuses their familiar plan for him to take her home and contacts her mother herself.

Possible ending: one earlier attempted comfort becomes unavailable, or becomes costly to accept. They cannot finish the date or decide their whole relationship tonight. A small act of care must answer a particular demand or refusal rather than merely illustrate estrangement. The photo strip is expendable. Prose may find a more immediate stopping point.
