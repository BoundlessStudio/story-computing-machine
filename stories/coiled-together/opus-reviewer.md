# Opus review: Coiled Together

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — two Ground failures (the two-whistle exchange, and the cousin quoting words they never heard), plus a queen–narrator "keep" thread told in rulings and contract terms.
- Prose: PASS — concrete and controlled; only minor phrasing slips and a closing paragraph that restates the verdict.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the wake, the claim, "keep" and the remembered plea are all delivered within the length band; there are minor object and route-name slips. The cousin's knowledge leak is filed as D2.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree. It missed the whistle and knowledge failures, the misfired first tap signal, and the queen's clerk-like register.

The rescue sequence (L145–L341) is the story's dialogue at its best. The rescuers argue over load and speed, the cousin talks over the carrier, the attendant reports in triage fragments, and the fissure eats the queen's warnings so the humans hear only a hiss. The failures sit on either side of that sequence. Before it, the exchanges that set up the premise (the plea, the whistle, the stone taps, the "keep" offer) are either unclear about what happened or play as rulings from a queen who talks like a clerk. After it, the cousin quotes words they could not have heard, the carrier recites the story's evidence rule, and the last queen–narrator exchange is a list of contract terms. The edit should fix the Ground failures first. Then it should give "keep" one consistent meaning between the queen and the narrator and let a little of it into their speech, without turning the queen into a seducer and without losing the terms that prove the narrator is free.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Blocking · Two whistles, no recoverable referent

- Where: L59 and L85–L93, first chamber scene, right after the low-air explanation
- Text:
> I remembered the storm whistle at the cave mouth, three falling notes that meant shelter or danger depending on which roadwarden had carved it.
> "I went down when I heard your whistle."
> "That was your whistle."
> "No. Yours."
> My brass storm whistle hung from the snapped loop of my belt.
- Problem: Ground/referent and Ground/knowledge. The story calls two different objects a "storm whistle": a carved one at the cave mouth, which a roadwarden made and whose notes mean shelter or danger (L59), and the narrator's own brass one (L93). When the queen says she heard "your whistle", the narrator's "That was your whistle" has no visible basis. He has just remembered the mouth whistle as a roadwarden's, not the serpentfolk's, and the lure suspicion that would explain the accusation is never voiced. The reader cannot tell which whistle either speaker means. "No. Yours." settles it by flat correction. The evidence the narrator then finds (a scale caught in the snapped belt loop) proves she grabbed his belt, not who blew which whistle. This is the beat that explains how the rescue began and why the serpentfolk warnings failed, which is the basis of the later job offer.
- Fix: First decide what happened. For example: the narrator followed the mouth whistle's notes in, taking them for "shelter", then blew his own brass whistle when the air turned, and she came down to the brass one. Give the two whistles distinct names in L59 and in the dialogue. Let the narrator's accusation carry its reason (the lure stories), so that the queen answers that reason and says what she actually heard. Present the scale in the belt loop as evidence of her grip, not of the whistle. Illustrative only: "Somebody whistled me in off the road. Was that you?"

### D2 · Blocking · Cousin knows words spoken out of their hearing

- Where: L361–L373, upper mouth after the escape, the cousin's accusation
- Text:
> "You heard her say she wanted to keep them."
> "I heard half the sounds in that throat."
> My cousin looked at me. "You're not taking this offer."
- Problem: Ground/knowledge. The queen said "I want to keep you" and made the six-day offer at L123–L135, in the chamber, while the cousin and the carrier were at the far end of the signal fissure. The story then shows that her words do not survive the fissure (L141, "Her consonants tore away"; L173). Nobody repeats either line to the cousin on the page. Even so, the cousin quotes "keep" and knows there is an "offer". The two claims also clash. If the cousin heard enough to know it was an offer of paid work, then calling it "keeping" is deliberately misleading, and nothing marks it that way. The carrier's reply half-concedes that they heard it, which compounds the leak instead of catching it.
- Fix: Give the cousin an on-page source. The smallest version: at the mouth, before L361, the narrator mentions the offer in one line (for instance in answer to "We take you as soon as that drops"), and the cousin seizes on the word. Then rewrite L369 so the cousin cites that source instead of what the carrier heard, and adjust L371 to match (see D9 and D10). If you instead show at L125 that the words carried up the fissure, the cousin knows it was a paid offer, and the "keep" charge has to read as deliberate twisting. Illustrative only: "You said she wants to keep you. Her word."

### D3 · Major · The first two-tap "stone" signal misfires unnoticed

- Where: L95–L103, first chamber scene, the rescuers' first contact
- Text:
> Somewhere above, stone rang twice. The attendant answered by striking the floor twice with the wooden handle of a spoon.
> "Falling-stone warning," the queen said. "Two taps.
> The tapping came again, followed by a voice made thin by water and distance.
- Problem: Ground/staging. "The tapping came again, followed by" the cousin's voice tells the reader that the first two raps came from the human rescuers, who cannot know the serpentfolk drill. Yet the attendant answers them as a stone warning, the queen explains them as one, and nobody takes cover or notices the mismatch. Either the queen's reading is wrong and the scene ignores it, or a real warning goes out and nobody acts on it. The later payoffs (L265, L323, L337–L341) depend on the code being exact, and this first use muddies it.
- Fix: Make the mismatch legible and put it to use. The attendant can still answer by drill and the queen can still give the code. When the voice follows, let the queen or the attendant register in one short line that humans were knocking, not warning. That also quietly seeds the job offer about warnings humans misread. Alternatively, change the number of raps so no code is triggered. Keep "Nothing else", because the narrator's raised knife still needs that reassurance.

### D4 · Major · Opening claim settled by a ruling that opens a gap

- Where: L43–L57, the first exchange after the release (the prompt's "you begged me" beat)
- Text:
> "Then how convenient for you."
> "That request governed the climb," she said. "You asked for release now. I released you."
- Problem: This is the prompt's central exchange. The narrator's "how convenient" is a good, human jab. The queen answers it with a ruling on how far a request reaches. "Governed" is legalistic, and taken literally the line opens a hole: if the plea covered only the climb, why was she still coiled tight around them on the ledge, and why did she tighten when they struggled (L19)? Nobody asks the question the prompt plants. The reply sounds like the collection's clause-and-ruling register, not like a queen who has just bled for a stranger and been accused.
- Fix: Keep the wake beats and "how convenient". Replace "governed" with plain speech that also accounts for the hold after the climb. Choose the reason to fit what "keep" is meant to mean (the drop on the left, their thrashing, their cold, or her own reluctance to let go). Let her insistence carry some feeling: impatience, pride or hurt. Or let the narrator ask outright why she was still holding them, and have her answer or evade in a way the reader can read. Illustrative only: "You were thrashing a pace from that drop. I held on until you told me not to."

### D5 · Major · "Then leave them" at the crossing has two opposite readings

- Where: L275–L283, the crossing, with the narrator dizzy in the cut
- Text:
> "I can brace the break or carry you. Not both."
> "Then leave them," my cousin said. "We have the line."
> "The line isn't on me," I said.
- Problem: Ground/referent. Every character except the queen takes they/them, and at this moment the attendant is still sliding along the queen's braced body. "Leave them" can mean "don't carry my kin, we'll haul them" (the intended reading, judging by the narrator's reply) or "stop bracing for your attendant and take my kin". The two readings call for opposite actions at the climax's decision point, and the reader can only resolve it by reasoning back from the next line.
- Fix: Have the cousin say who they mean in their own idiom ("my kin", or a pointing gesture). Illustrative only: "Keep your coils off my kin. We have the line." Check L207 ("The ledge under them is going") in the same pass (see also P3).

### D6 · Major · "I want to keep you" is set up, then turned into a pun

- Where: L117–L137, at the mouth of the signal fissure
- Text:
> "You don't want your prisoner back?"
> "I want to keep you," she said.
> "Six days. Paid. You know surface warning marks, and ours failed to turn you from the basin. I require someone who can tell me why."
> "Keep me employed."
- Problem: The prompt's "keeping" arrives through a setup built to deliver it: the narrator's "prisoner back" hands "want" straight to her. She is precise everywhere else, so she would not choose "keep" to mean "hire" unless she meant more by it or the word is not hers. Neither reason is on the page. The narrator's "Keep me employed." turns a moment of fright into a dry gloss. The queen then falls back into terms ("If you accept." / "Leave when the upper path opens."). Within four lines, the charge the prompt promises has become a job description. "Six days. Paid." also borrows the attendant's telegraphic register (see D8).
- Fix: Keep "I want to keep you" and the beat where it stops them with one knee in the fissure. Give her word choice a meaning. Either "keep" is her language's ordinary word for retaining someone (show it through one mismatch the narrator notices), or it carries a want she cannot comfortably say: someone who survived the basin and can tell her why her warnings read as lures, or simply not wanting the one who held on to her to go. Replace "Keep me employed." with a reaction that is wrong-footed or afraid rather than witty. Let her explain the offer in full sentences instead of a fragment list. Illustrative only: "Keep me how?"

### D7 · Major · The last queen–narrator exchange is a contract

- Where: L377–L403, at the upper mouth
- Text:
> "Two silver weights per day. Meals. Surface work only once the path is safe."
> "No door," I said.
> "There is no door."
> "Unrestricted. And when the upper path opens, I can leave before the six days are done."
> The queen said, "Accepted."
- Problem: This is a negotiation routine at the last meaningful exchange between the queen and the narrator. The narrator has just remembered begging her, asked to be coiled, been carried, and watched her bleed. The cousin has ordered them to refuse. The exchange runs clause, confirmation, clause, ruling ("Yes." "Yes." "Accepted."), and the attendant's "Necessary." adds another ruling. The terms do real work, because the doorless niche and the right to leave test whether they are captive, and they should stay. The problem is that nothing else passes between the two. There is no embarrassment about the plea and no acknowledgement of the carry. There is no sign of what "keep" meant to her, and no visible reason the narrator says yes beyond defying the cousin. "I come to the mouth every day." is also unclear: the niche is already beside this mouth (L383), and the story has two mouths (L59, L343).
- Fix: Keep the terms but trim them to what the narrator actually tests (the door, the right to leave). Let one or two lines carry the relationship: the narrator testing whether "keep" still means what they feared, or the queen answering one term in a way that shows the want or the cost (her wound, the failed warnings). Let at least one of her replies be something other than "Yes". Clarify or cut the mouth condition (see C2). Illustrative only: "In the basin. How many times did I say it?"

### D8 · Major · The attendant's list register is shared by the queen, the carrier and the narrator

- Where: throughout; clearest at L79 (attendant), L99, L127 and L379 (queen), L367 (carrier), L395 (narrator)
- Text:
> "Two dry blankets. Half a water jar. Salts. One sound lamp. Your left hand is cut."
> "Six days. Paid.
> "Requested. Released at the shelf.
> "Unrestricted. And when the upper path opens
- Problem: Identical voices. The attendant's verbal identity is the verbless inventory or triage report. The queen (L99, L127, L379), the carrier (L367) and, at the end, the narrator (L395) use the same fragment-list rhythm. With the tags covered, "Requested. Released at the shelf." or "Two silver weights per day. Meals." could be the attendant's. The queen's own markers (no contractions, formal verbs) are then spent on legal diction ("governed", "require", "Accepted") instead of showing how she thinks.
- Fix: Reserve the verbless list for the attendant. Give the queen full, formal, uncontracted sentences that show what she attends to. Keep the carrier in rope-and-load talk, with verbs and some gruffness. Keep the narrator on contractions and trail reasoning. Sweep the lines listed under Where. Do not lengthen the crisis commands (L151, L175–L179, L263), where short is right.

### D9 · Major · The carrier's testimony recites the story's evidence rule

- Where: L367, the accusation at the upper mouth
- Text:
> "Requested. Released at the shelf. I saw the queen hold the attendant when the ledge went. I did not see what happened below before we arrived."
- Problem: Philosopher and secondary chorus. The carrier stepping in is useful, because a third party checks the cousin and shifts the balance of the argument. But it arrives as a sworn statement (I saw, I saw, I did not see) that recites the story's evidence discipline, and L413 then restates it in narration. A rigger who thinks in loads has been handed the author's rule to say.
- Fix: Keep the carrier stepping in, and keep "I saw one carry." Tell what they saw through the rope: who asked, who held what, and when the load came off. Cut the closing disclaimer sentence, or make it blunt and partial. The cousin's accusation can be checked without anyone announcing the limits of evidence.

### D10 · Minor · Last-word lines

- Where: L87 (whistle scene), L357–L359 and L371 (upper mouth)
- Text:
> "Earlier than you. Not early enough."
> "I wasn't asking you."
> "I was answering the load."
> "I heard half the sounds in that throat."
- Problem: Each closes an exchange with a shaped antithesis or a figurative comeback. The reader has to decode "answering the load" from the carrier's jargon as a retort, and "I wasn't asking you" exists mostly to set it up (Punchline Patch). "Not early enough" never says what she was too late for.
- Fix: Replace these with plain answers, or end the exchange on the line before. At L359 the carrier can shrug off the cousin or keep working. At L371 the carrier can say plainly that the fissure ate her words (keep the acoustics). At L87, say what she was too late for.

### D11 · Minor · The queen's precise diction misfires

- Where: L107 (the queen reacting to the narrator's stagger), L219 (the air rising), L237–L239 (choosing the chimney)
- Text:
> "That is the low air's remainder," she said.
> "The basin is being pressed upward."
> "Enough for us," the queen said.
- Problem: For a speaker built on precision, each of these picks the wrong word. "Remainder" stands in for aftereffect. She says the basin, not its air, is being "pressed upward", so the narrator has to translate ("Bad air rising?"). "Enough for us" after the attendant's "Enough air" could mean air or room, and it exists only as a small correction of the attendant.
- Fix: Name the right thing plainly: the water pushing the bad air up, and the gap behind the slab being wide enough for bodies. Let the attendant and the queen give their information without one correcting the other.

### D12 · Minor · Narration explains the line just spoken

- Where: L113, L137, L231, L375
- Text:
> as if that ended the subject.
> No plea for trust. No smile.
> Her precision had shortened to its bones.
> The command landed where concern had been. I understood both and liked neither.
- Problem: Each tells the reader what the preceding line meant or how it sounded, after the dialogue has already shown it. L375 in particular interprets the cousin's line before the reader can.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Awkward anatomy and object phrasing

- Where: L35, L39, L93, L113 and L209, mostly in the first chamber scene
- Text:
> One of her two arms came beneath my shoulders.
> covered the join of her arms to her scaled trunk
> A thread of black-green scale remained caught in the broken leather.
> The attendant, one arm hanging close to the body
- Problem: "One of her two arms" counts her arms just to inform the reader. "Join of her arms to her scaled trunk" puts her arms on the snake body. A scale is not a thread. "The body" should be the attendant's own. "The stoppered jar" (L113) gets a definite article though it was never introduced.
- Fix: Use plain anatomy (the wrap covers where her human waist becomes scale). Write "a black-green scale" or "the torn edge of a scale" and "close to their side". Introduce the jar in the attendant's inventory, or call it "a stoppered jar".

### P3 · Minor · Singular they for four characters at handoffs
