# Coiled Together

The first thing I knew was warmth. The second was that it had muscles.

Something thicker than my chest lay across my thighs. Another length circled my waist, pinned my left arm to my side, crossed my ribs, and rose behind my shoulders in a wall of dark gold scales. When I tried to sit up, the whole arrangement tightened with quiet, effortless attention.

I stopped moving.

My clothes were still on. My shirt was torn at one shoulder and wet to the skin, but my belt remained buckled, my boots laced, and nothing hurt except my skull. That should have been reassuring. It was difficult to feel reassured when I could not move from shoulders to ankles.

A woman's face leaned over mine.

Black hair hung loose around bronze-brown cheeks. Her eyes caught the cave's amber lamplight and returned it in narrow green slits. From the waist up she might have passed for a court beauty dressed in hammered-gold ornaments and a wrap of gray silk. From the waist down she filled half the chamber.

I knew those scales from bounty sketches. Every caravan scout did.

“The Gilded Hunger,” I said. My voice scraped.

Her mouth curved just enough to show the tips of two teeth. “Humans do favor the name they gave me over the one I possess.”

“Sivareth.”

“Better.”

At thirty-two, I had survived a bridge collapse, two border skirmishes, and an ox maddened by bluefly fever. I had never expected to die in bed. The bed, I noticed, was a broad nest of furs tucked into a hollow of the Hushvault. Clay lamps burned in wall niches. Beyond them, the chamber ended at a black cleft from which water hissed far below.

I tested the coil across my chest. It yielded less than stone.

“If you're going to eat me,” I said, “do I get to stand first?”

“You may stand when the glass-rain weakens.” Her gaze traveled over my face with an attention that felt worse than hunger. “I am not eating you.”

“Forgive my lack of gratitude.”

“I already have. You were more courteous while unconscious.”

I twisted again, sharper this time. The loop around my ribs flexed. Not tighter, exactly. It followed me, allowing no gap.

“Let me go.”

Something changed in her expression. Not anger. Disappointment, perhaps, which was more unsettling.

“You asked me not to.”

“I am asking now.”

“You are fevered, your last hour is broken, and six armed humans wait above the eastern throat.” She spoke as if listing rain, altitude, and poor road stone. “I made you a promise.”

“I don't remember making you one.”

“You did not. You begged. I promised.”

Outside the chamber, the storm hurled a fistful of glass against rock. The sound went on and on, a thousand tiny blades skittering down the mountain. Our caravan had made camp below the ridge before dawn. Captain Halvek Prynn had sent me ahead to find a sheltered way through. He had tied a charm around my neck, touched two fingers to it, and said even serpentfolk disliked the smell.

After that, I had only scraps: a yellow gleam under stone, my boot sliding, a drop where the floor should have been.

I swallowed. “Free my right arm.”

Sivareth studied me. “Why?”

“Because I asked.”

The upper coil lifted at once.

Cold air struck my wet sleeve. I pulled my arm free, half expecting the loop to snap shut and break it, but she settled her weight beneath my elbow instead. I touched my face, throat, ribs. No blood. No punctures. A tender stripe crossed my palms, as if a cord had burned through them. My knife was gone. So was the small pack I wore when scouting.

The charm's leather thong remained around my neck. It had been cleanly cut below the knot.

“You took it.”

“I removed a lure.”

“A ward.”

“Is that what the man with the red scarf called it?”

Halvek's scarf had once been crimson. Six dusty seasons had reduced it to the color of old brick. I had not mentioned him.

Sivareth reached to a stone shelf without shifting the coils around me. Between two claws she held a copper cage no longer than my thumb. Inside it gleamed a bead of cloudy resin. She had split the cage lengthwise. Even from the bed I caught its scent: hot metal, sweet rot, and the heavy musk that had followed us up the ridge.

I had blamed the pack mules.

“Your kind made this,” she said. “Copper drawn through a square plate. Hide glue boiled with pine gum. The cord was knotted by a right hand missing the tip of its smallest finger.”

Halvek had lost that fingertip to frostbite.

“You expect me to accept that because you can describe a trinket?”

“No. I expect you to be stubborn until the truth has bitten you somewhere tender.”

I reached for the cage. She let me take it.

The copper edges had been filed with a pattern of shallow nicks. Not serpent marks. Caravan marks, used to tell sealed parcels apart in darkness: two, pause, three. Halvek's private stores. I pressed a thumbnail into the resin. Its skin broke, releasing another breath of musk, and the room seemed to tilt.

Rain cut my cheeks. A ledge shuddered beneath my boots. Someone shouted from above, not in warning but with furious impatience: *Go farther in.*

Then the shelf fell.

I came back with my hand clenched. The copper cage had bitten my palm.

Sivareth's hand hovered near my wrist without touching. “That poison coaxes heat from a body and muddles the newest memories. It makes prey bright to my senses. It also makes the prey wander.”

“You sound familiar with it.”

“Three hunters used it last spring. Two fled when I found them. One reached the clutch passage.” Her teeth showed again, without humor. “The stories told about me are not all lies, Kerric Alver.”

Hearing my full name in her mouth chilled me more than the rain. “How do you know that?”

“You told me while I held you over the chasm. You also told me you were too old to die for Halvek Prynn's bad judgment.”

“That sounds like me.”

“Then perhaps some of your judgment survived.”

She shifted beneath me. Scale slid over scale with the soft scrape of dry leaves, and my treacherous body relaxed into the renewed warmth before my mind could stop it. Sivareth noticed. Her gaze softened in a way I did not trust.

“Do not mistake that for permission,” I said.

“I mistake nothing.”

“You're holding me after I told you to let go.”

Her coils went very still.

“You said it while afraid and ill.”

“I am still allowed to mean it.”

She looked toward the dark cleft. “And I am allowed to prevent a fevered man from walking into a barbed net.”

“A healer can prevent him. A queen can advise him. A captor decides his answer does not count.”

The thin membranes at her temples flared, then flattened. She had ruled the Hushvault for seventy winters, according to road tales, and killed challengers older than my grandfather. I had managed to offend her before breakfast.

Instead of squeezing, she unlooped the length across my ribs.

“Look,” she said.

Set into the wall opposite us was a sheet of pale mineral, thin as horn and veined with gold. Sivareth lifted one lamp from its niche and snuffed it between finger and thumb. The mineral dimmed. Then cloudy blooms appeared across it: one large amber shape around the bed, my smaller red-gold heat nested inside; six pale smudges in a hooked line above the eastern passage.

No, seven. One stood farther back near the mouth, broad at the shoulders.

“Stone remembers warmth for several breaths,” she said. “The wall is thin there. It cannot tell me why they wait. It tells me one carries a bow, two hold the corners of something wider than their bodies, and your red-scarfed friend remains where the rain cannot touch him.”

One of the smaller blooms vanished. A moment later it returned beside another.

“It can't show a bow,” I said.

“It shows a warm arm held away from a warm chest.”

“Could be a shovel.”

“Then he means to garden in the dark.”

Despite myself, a laugh escaped me. It hurt my head. Sivareth's smile widened, pleased and proprietary, and the sound died in my throat.

She touched the mineral. The vast coil around my legs shifted closer.

“You are safe here,” she said. “I will keep you until the hunters are gone.”

“Until when?”

“Until you are safe.”

“That's not a time.”

“Safety is not governed by clocks.”

“Nor by queens, however much they might prefer it.” I pushed at the last loop across my waist. “Release me completely. Give me my knife and pack. I will see the entrance myself.”

Her claws curled against the fur.

“No,” she said.

“Then you are keeping a prisoner, not a promise.”

Water thundered through the lower cave. The heat-shapes on the mineral held their positions.

Sivareth watched me for so long that I became conscious of every place her scales touched: the pressure under my knees, the broad warmth along my spine, the tip of her tail lying over my left boot as gently as a hand. Want lived plainly in her expression. Not appetite for flesh. Something lonelier and, in its way, no less dangerous.

“When you fell,” she said, “I caught your wrist. The lure had made you slick with sweat. You were sliding out of my hand. I wound around you because there was no ledge for my weight, and you said, ‘Don't let go. Whatever happens, don't let go.’”

A shred of memory returned: green eyes below me in the dark, a gold-brown arm stretched until it trembled, the terrible swing of my body over open air. My own voice tore out of me: *Don't let go.* Her answer came through clenched teeth: *I will not.* My hands burned because I had clung to her wrist.

“Then I woke,” Sivareth continued, “and for one astonishing moment a human wanted my strength instead of fearing it. You cannot expect me to discover that I was mistaken about the moment and enjoy the correction.”

“No,” I said. “But I expect you to make it.”

Her throat worked once. Then the Hushvault moved around me.

Coil after coil drew away. Air found my body in cold strips. I sat up, dizzy, while the immense lengths slid from the nest and gathered behind Sivareth's human torso. Nothing barred the way between me and the passage.

She reached beneath a folded fur, drew out my sheathed knife, and laid it across my open palm. My pack followed. “You are released, Kerric Alver.”

I strapped on the knife. “Thank you.”

“I do not enjoy the correction.”

“I noticed.”

“If you step onto the upper shelf, the net will take you. If you lead them deeper, I will kill any who approach the clutch chamber.”

There was no boast in it. That steadied me more than a claim of harmlessness would have.

I found the eastern passage where the mineral wall narrowed. Meltwater foamed ankle-deep along one side, carrying chips of volcanic glass that glittered in my lamplight. The main tunnel climbed toward a gray mouth fifty paces ahead. Beneath a low arch, the floor pinched into a throat scarcely wide enough for Sivareth's largest coils. Above it ran an old runoff gutter. Someone had stuffed the drainage notch with rolled canvas and tied it off to keep the hunters' ledge dry. Water trembled behind the plug.

Halfway to the mouth, I crouched behind a broken spur of stone. Glass-rain hissed beyond the overhang. I could have stepped outside and gone downslope alone. Fever tugged at my balance; the storm would have opened my face before I found the caravan tents.

A pebble clicked overhead.

“Hold,” Halvek whispered. His voice came through the rock as clearly as it had across a hundred campfires. “The gold one takes the bait first. Then drop both sides.”

Someone murmured, “What about Kerric?”

“If he can walk, he can duck.”

“And if he can't?”

Silence answered.

I pressed my hand against the cut thong at my neck. Another memory rose, whole enough to hurt: Halvek kneeling beside the weakened shelf before he sent me forward, hiding a small iron wedge inside his sleeve. His missing fingertip white against the stone. *Go farther in.*

One royal scale would pay a caravan's debts for a season. Halvek had spoken often of families waiting through winter. Apparently he had decided one scout was a fair price for all of them.

I backed down the passage.

Sivareth waited exactly where I had left her. She had put out the lamps near the cleft and gathered herself into a strike so compact it made the chamber seem larger. Her eyes dropped to the knife in my hand.

“They are there,” I said.

“Yes.”

“They used me.”

“Yes.”

I hated that she did not sound triumphant. It left me nothing easy to fight.

“The throat is flooding,” I said. “You cannot pass it quickly without exposing your back to the shelf. The net has barbs. But they've blocked the old drain to keep their footing. Cut the canvas and their ledge washes out.”

“My hands cannot reach the notch while I shield my spine.”

“Mine can.” I set my pack down and checked the knife's edge. “If you carry me.”

Sivareth's coils stirred, then stopped. “Ask clearly.”

“First, every part of me stays loose until we agree.”

She lowered her hands. “Agreed.”

“You may coil around me until we are back across the flooded throat. No crushing. Leave room for a full breath. My knife stays in my right hand. If I say stop, you let go immediately, even if you think I'm wrong.”

She repeated each condition, word for word. Formal precision stripped the warmth from her voice, but could not hide the hunger beneath it.

Then she asked, “What happens when you say stop?”

“You let go.”

“Even if I am afraid for you.”

“Especially then.”

Sivareth bowed her head. “Ask me.”

The old warnings crowded close. Never invite a serpentfolk to touch you. Never bargain inside her den. Never let one learn the shape of your fear. They had kept me alive on bad roads. They had not prepared me for this.

“Hold me,” I said. “On those terms.”

She moved slowly enough that I could have stepped away. The first coil passed behind my knees and lifted. Another curved around my hips, firm but not fixed, and a third rose over my left shoulder to shield my chest. I could draw a full breath. My right arm remained free, the knife angled away from both our bodies.

“How is this?” she asked.

My heart hammered against her scales. “Acceptable.”

“A stirring declaration.”

“Get us through alive and I may improve it.”

She flowed into the passage.

Speed changed everything. The body that had seemed too large for the Hushvault narrowed, lengthened, and found purchase on wet stone where my boots would have slipped. Her scales flexed under me in waves. At the throat she turned her back to the high shelf and raised two coils like a roof.

The barbed net fell.

It struck with a clang and a chorus of shouts. Hooks scraped gold scales, caught between them, and pulled tight. Sivareth hissed beside my ear. The sound vibrated through my bones. Her loop around my waist contracted, stopped before it stole my breath, then eased by a finger's width.

“Kerric!” Halvek shouted. “Down! Crawl out!”

I saw his boots on the shelf through a gap in the net. The canvas plug bulged above us, its retaining cord knotted around a stone tooth. Sivareth surged forward. An arrow cracked against the coil shielding my chest.

I leaned out and slashed once.

The cord parted. The canvas shot from the notch. A wall of black runoff burst over the hunters' ledge, full of glass grit and storm gravel. Men yelled and scrambled uphill. One side of the net went slack; Sivareth rolled, tore free of the other anchors, and drove through the throat with me held at the center of her motion.

We reached the far spur as a section of ledge collapsed into the channel. The hunters crowded onto a high tongue of rock beyond the break, hemmed against the outer wall. Bruised, drenched, alive. Halvek clung to an anchor spike, his red scarf pasted to his throat.

“Kerric!” he called. “Come up here. One scale, that's all. It feeds every family in the train through winter.”

“You loosened the shelf under me.”

His eyes flicked toward the others. “I knew she would catch the lure.”

Not me. The lure.

Sivareth lifted her head above the broken ledge. The hooked pupils of her eyes thinned. “If you cross into my lower halls again,” she told them, “your families may divide the bounty on your boots.”

No one mistook her meaning.

Halvek held out his hand to me. “You know what she is.”

I did. She had warned me more honestly than he had.

“Storm should drop by noon,” I said. “The eastern slope will be passable an hour after. Tell the caravan I found another road.”

Sivareth carried me back through the throat while the hunters argued behind us. Only when we entered the warm chamber did she lower my boots to the fur.

“Stop,” I said.

Her coils opened.

There was no pause, no plea, no clever claim that the danger remained. One instant I rested inside a living wall of warmth; the next I stood free, swaying in soaked boots with my knife still in my hand. Sivareth withdrew so quickly that the last loop struck the floor with a heavy rustle.

She turned her face toward the dark cleft. “The storm will weaken by noon. I will show you a western path when you wish to leave.”

I sheathed the knife.

On a shelf beyond the nest stood a clay pot, a bundle of dried river fish, and six carefully stoppered jars. Ordinary provisions, except that the jars had been arranged by someone with claws and the pot was large enough to bathe a child. My stomach cramped at the smell of smoke and salt.

“You said you weren't eating me,” I said. “You never said whether you would feed me.”

Sivareth glanced back. For an instant, the queen who had ruled seventy winters looked startled. “For how long?”

“One meal. One honest conversation. Then I decide what story goes down the mountain with me.”

“I will not flatter your species.”

“That would make two of us.”

She busied herself with the pot while I sat on the edge of the furs. The chamber felt larger now that I knew I could cross it. It also felt colder. Water squelched in my boots when I stretched my feet toward the nearest lamp.

Her gaze dropped to my boots.

The tip of her tail paused a short distance from my ankles. “May I?”

I looked at the open passage, my knife, and the queen waiting for an answer.

“One coil,” I said. “Around my feet.”

Her scales whispered over the stone, stopping just before they touched me.

“How tight?” she asked.
