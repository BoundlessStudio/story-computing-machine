# Coiled Together

My first clear thought was that I could not measure the room.

A scout measures before he decides anything: six strides to the door, a handspan of loose scree under the left boot, enough headroom to swing a blade. I had no stride, no ground to test, and barely enough breath to count. A scaled body thicker than a rain barrel crossed my shins. Another turn covered my hips. A third held my left arm against my ribs and ran behind my shoulders, warm and hard as sunlit masonry.

I drew one careful breath. The turn across my chest rose with it and settled when I exhaled.

I tried the right hand first. Free. Both feet answered when I flexed them, though they could go nowhere. My shirt and trousers were wet but fastened. Belt buckled. Boots laced. No blood on my fingertips when I felt my face, no pain from ribs or spine, only a fever pounding behind my eyes.

Then the thing holding me lifted its head.

The woman above the coils had black hair braided with flat gold rings and a face the road sketches had made cruder than it was. Green slit-pupiled eyes followed my hand to my throat. Below a sleeveless gray wrap, bronze skin gave way to dark-gold scales. The rest of her filled the low chamber in overlapping curves.

I was thirty-four years old and had spent fourteen of them finding routes around hazards. None of that training suggested a useful direction.

“Sivareth,” I said.

Her mouth opened just enough to show two fine points. “You remember my name. That is something.”

Humans called her the Gilded Hunger. The same accounts that named her said travelers entered the Hushvault and did not come out. I had never considered whether an adult serpentfolk queen might look tired.

“Move this turn down,” I said, touching the scales over my left forearm. “Two finger-widths. I want my wrist.”

She moved it at once. Not a twitch or a grudging slackening: the whole heavy length rolled below my elbow and left my hand free.

I pulled the arm to my chest and worked each finger. “Now loosen the one over my ribs.”

It opened enough for me to fill my lungs. No farther.

“You make small requests very clearly,” she said.

“Small mistakes kill scouts.” I pushed at the coil over my hips. It did not shift. “Take all of them off.”

“No.”

A request could move a coil. It could not yet get me out of them.

“You are unhurt,” she continued. “You are also fevered, confused, and surrounded by your own hunters. When those conditions change, I will reconsider.”

“My own people are a caravan. My knife and pack?”

“Dry. Behind me.”

“Put them where I can see them.”

She watched my face, then reached into the curve of her body and set both on a shelf cut into the wall. The pack was closed. My knife remained in its sheath.

Another burst of glass-rain rasped across the mountain outside, sharper than sleet and too heavy for sand. Water drummed somewhere beneath us. The chamber held three hooded lamps, a stack of fur blankets, and a black opening in the floor whose far wall I could not see. We were deep enough that the storm sounded like a saw working through bone.

“How did I get here?”

“You came through the eastern mouth carrying a scent meant for me. The shelf beneath you had been cut. It broke. I caught you above the lower shaft.” Her eyes narrowed. “You told me not to let go.”

The last thing I remembered cleanly was Captain Halvek Prynn pressing a copper charm into my palm below the ridge. A ward, he had called it. The memory after that came apart: rain on my eyelids, a yellow spark in wet rock, somebody ordering me farther in.

“I don't remember asking you.”

“You did not ask. You shouted until your voice failed, then begged.”

“That was over a hole.”

“It was.”

“This is a bed of furs.”

Sivareth glanced down as though checking the distinction. “The hole was less comfortable.”

Her humor had no softness in it. She knew exactly how much of the room she occupied, exactly how quickly she could close the breath she had returned.

I found the cut ends of a leather cord at my neck. “The charm.”

She reached for a shallow stone bowl near the lamps. Inside lay a thumb-long cage of twisted copper, split open along one edge and bundled in waxed cloth. A dirty amber pellet glistened through the gap.

“Do not warm it again,” she said. “It speaks in heat and musk. It led me from the clutch stair to your fall.”

“You expect me to believe a human lure caught a queen?”

“Caught?” The membranes at her temples lifted. “It offended my nose in my own halls. I went to remove it.”

She tipped the bowl close enough for me to inspect without touching. The copper had been twisted square instead of round, the way Prynn's supply cages were made so they would not roll off a wagon bed. One side bore three file strokes. I had cut those marks myself when I inventoried his stores two nights before.

Heat from the lamp softened the pellet despite the waxed cloth. A sweet animal smell climbed from it, followed by the taste of old pennies at the back of my tongue.

My memory gave me a wet thumb rubbing that same resin over the charm. Prynn's left hand, the smallest finger ending at the first joint. His other hand tightened the cord around my neck.

*It'll turn them away, Kerric. You take the inside line.*

The chamber tilted. I shut my eyes until the pounding eased.

“It raises your heat,” Sivareth said. “It tangles the nearest memories. That is what this piece did to you. I will not pretend to know every mixture humans have cooked.”

“How do you know what this one does?”

“A man wore one here last spring. He chose the clutch stair after I warned him away.” One pointed tooth touched her lower lip. “He improved my education. He did not improve the stories told about me.”

I looked toward the dark opening in the floor. “Did you eat him?”

“No.”

“Did you kill him?”

“Yes.”

She offered no excuse, and I did not ask for one. A nest under threat could make a wolf dangerous without making every warning about wolves false. The old road tales had kept me from other caves. They might yet keep me alive in this one.

Sivareth shifted a lower coil. My fevered muscles yielded to the warmth before I could stop them. Relief traveled up my legs, bodily and immediate. It changed nothing about who had decided I could not stand.

Her eyes noticed the easing in me.

“My muscles are reacting to heat,” I said. “Don't call it an answer.”

“I did not ask a question.” Yet the turn over my thighs settled more closely.

I tapped it with two fingers. “Back where it was.”

She moved it back.

Across the chamber, a pale sheet of mineral had been fitted into the rock like a cloudy window. Gold veins wandered through it. Sivareth extinguished the nearest lamp, and warmth appeared on the sheet in blurred islands: one great amber mass around a smaller red center, then six dim upright smears beyond us, arranged in two levels along the rising passage.

“Six bodies,” I said.

“Six fires in human shapes.”

“Can that show weapons?”

“It shows heat and position. A hand leaving a chest. A head turning. Nothing of iron, words, or purpose.” She placed one claw beneath the highest three smears. “These have waited above the narrow throat since before you woke.”

“Could be my caravan looking for me.”

“Then your caravan searches with remarkable patience.”

One shape leaned toward another. The mineral recorded only their changing heat, not what passed between them. Useful evidence, incomplete by design. I trusted it more for that.

Water struck below with a deeper boom. A cold thread crept across the chamber floor from the eastern passage and vanished into the shaft.

Sivareth looked at it. “The upper drains are blocked. If the rain holds, the lower chambers flood. I will move you farther inside.”

“How far?”

“Beyond the clutch stair.”

The loops around me tightened by the breadth of a fingernail, an unconscious answer before her words.

“You would carry me deeper while armed people wait at the mouth.”

“I would keep what I saved out of their hands.”

“What you saved is listening.”

Her chin lifted. “And would walk directly under them if released.”

“Probably. I need to see the route before I believe your reading of it.”

“Your fever makes the floor move.”

“Then the floor will tell me how sick I am.” I pointed to the shelf. “Put the pack at my feet. Put the knife on it. Remove every coil.”

“I said no.”

“The fall ended. So did that order. If you want a prisoner, say prisoner.”

The largest bend of her body rose behind her. Scales whispered over stone. For one hard moment I understood how a hunter might die before he saw the strike.

“You entered my home scented for my death,” she said. “Your companions choke my drains. My clutch is below them. I am entitled to very little patience.”

“I did not cut your drains, and I did not know what I carried. You cannot stack me with the things you are protecting.”

Her gaze stayed on the loop across my knees. It took no heat-reading stone to see she wanted it left there.

Then she drew back.

The turn at my chest went first. The weight over my hips slid away, followed by the loops at my knees and boots. Cold found every wet seam in my clothing. I pushed upright on the furs and waited for the cave to stop rocking. Sivareth did not touch me.

She set the pack at my feet and laid the sheathed knife across it, hilt toward my right hand. The wax-wrapped lure remained in the stone bowl.

“That too,” I said.

“It sickens you.”

“It is evidence against Prynn. Fold the cloth over it and wrap it again.”

She folded a second square of waxed cloth around the charm, tied the bundle, and placed it in the outer pocket of my pack. Then she withdrew far enough that open floor lay between us and the eastern passage.

“Nothing of mine touches you now, Kerric Alver.” Her voice had gone formal and flat. “I dislike it.”

“You don't have to like a route for it to be open.”

I strapped on the knife, shouldered the pack, and stood. My knees held. Fever pulled the lamps into short golden streaks, but I could walk a straight chalk line between two cracks in the floor.

When the last warmth of her coils left my palms, memory returned in a flash no longer than a blink: the shelf dropping; my body swinging over black water; Sivareth braced sideways in a fissure, one arm locked around my wrist. My grip had slipped on her wet scales. A gold length had whipped up from below and closed around my waist.

*Don't let go. Please. Don't let go.*

Her answer had come through bared teeth: *Then stop fighting my hold.*

I opened my eyes in the chamber. Sivareth had not moved to reclaim me.

“I remember the shaft,” I said.

Something in her expression sharpened with hope.

“I meant it there,” I added.

The hope narrowed, but did not vanish. “You were less exact while falling.”

“I am going to the eastern mouth.”

“I will not follow unless you call.”

I left her beside the heat-sheet.

The eastern passage climbed forty paces from her chamber to a gray blade of stormlight. Ten paces in, three white quartz ribs crossed the floor. Beyond them the roof lowered and the ground split: the main runoff gnawed westward through a central trench, while a dry shelf hugged the south wall above it. That shelf ran toward the entrance, then broadened into a gallery overlooking the narrowest part of the tunnel.

I crouched behind the third rib and read the damage. Someone had driven two iron anchors into opposite walls above the throat. Dark rope vanished over the gallery lip. Net rig. On the south side, an old cross-drain should have carried water from a swollen wall basin across the gallery and down a sink on the north. The hunters had rammed a canvas bundle into its mouth and lashed it to a piton, keeping their perch dry. Water trembled behind the plug and spilled around its edges into the lower runnel—the cold thread already reaching Sivareth's chamber.

The plug was a foolish convenience. Cut its lashing and the backed water would scour across the gallery before dropping into the north sink. The outer neck of the shelf was shale. One hard rush might split it and leave anyone on the inner gallery stranded above the trench.

The lashing hung an arm's reach above the tunnel floor. Easy to cut if no one shot me first.

I crawled beneath the gallery until voices resolved through the water noise.

“Net pair, wait for the shoulders,” Prynn said. He was close, just above and east of me. “Bow line takes the high turns. Nobody looses while Kerric is on top.”

A woman answered, “And if he's underneath?”

“Then aim better. The lure got her here. We don't waste the opening.”

“The shelf dropped with him on it.”

“I know where I put the wedge.” Prynn's impatience was familiar enough to raise a dozen camp mornings around me. “Watch the chalk mark. Gold crosses, you pull.”

Prynn's sequence told me enough. He had weakened the shelf. He expected Sivareth to bring his bait back. Whether I could crawl away afterward belonged to nobody's assignment.

One boot scraped on the gallery above me. I backed out on hands and knees, rose behind the quartz ribs, and walked to the chamber under my own power.

Sivareth had gathered herself beside the heat-sheet. Six shapes still burned across it. She read my face before I spoke.

“Which claim did the stone fail to prove?” she asked.

“All of them. Prynn proved the rest.” I dropped the pack but kept the knife. “Two on the net, three bows, Prynn calling the drop. He cut the shelf himself.”

Her upper body lowered. “Then I will clear my threshold.”

“Rush the gallery and they drop barbs across your spine. Wait here and the blocked cross-drain sends half the storm toward your clutch.”

“I can endure barbs.”

“Can the chambers below endure the water?”

The end of her tail struck stone once. “Speak, route scout.”

I scratched the passage in the grit: chamber, quartz ribs, central trench, gallery, cross-drain, mouth. “Their dry ledge is the trap and the weakness. The plug is here. I can cut it from below. The wash takes their footing and their anchors, then drops north instead of running toward your clutch.”

“You cannot stand beneath five hunters long enough to cut anything.”

“No.” I looked at the broad shield of her outer coils. “Carry me to it.”

She went motionless.

“You required distance,” she said.

“I required release. I got it.” I stepped close enough to point along her body without touching. “Now I'm asking for cover. Keep my right arm clear. Hold me firmly enough to cross the trench, loosely enough that I can turn to the lashing. Bring me back past the three white ribs and set me down there.”

“If an arrow finds a gap?”

“Close the gap. Not my lungs.”

“And if you order me open before the ribs?”

“You open. I take whatever the trench does.”

Sivareth looked at the knife in my right hand. “You would keep that where it can cut the canvas.”

“Yes.”

“And me.”

“Yes.”

Her teeth showed. “I will watch that hand.”

“If I miss the rope, move me two fingers higher.”

Her lower coils parted, leaving a space before her. “Then tell me where to put you.”

The old road warnings came readily: never enter a serpent den; never accept a queen's gift; never let scales close behind you. None of them had been written for a drain lashing beneath five bows.

“Sivareth, hold me and carry me to the cross-drain. Bring me back to the ribs on those terms.”

She came forward slowly. One coil curved under my boots and raised me above the wet floor. A second supported my hips. The highest loop passed over my left shoulder and across my chest, leaving my knife arm outside. She adjusted once when I could not fill a breath, then waited.

I twisted toward an imaginary overhead knot. My shoulder moved. My wrist reached. Both boots stayed braced.

“Go,” I said.

Sivareth drove east, her scales finding wet stone in a fast alternating rhythm.

Her scales found purchase on both sides of the trench. At the quartz ribs she raised the outer turn over my head. The gallery filled the low ceiling ahead; I could see the net's lower edge folded against its lip and three bow tips between stone teeth.

Prynn shouted, “Mark!”

The net fell before Sivareth reached the chalk line.

She reared into it rather than away. Barbs rang across her upper coils and hooked between two plates. The turn around my chest locked hard enough to halt me, not crush me. An arrow struck the curve above my shoulder and skidded into the trench.

“Lashing,” she hissed.

I leaned from her shelter. The canvas plug bulged over my head, its cord vibrating against the piton. My first cut glanced off wet rope. Fever doubled the knot. I hooked my left hand through the cord, pulled it low, and sawed twice.

A hunter swung from the gallery lip onto the narrow south shelf beside us, reaching for my wrist. Sivareth's free hand caught his bow and broke it against the wall. She could have taken the arm with it. Her claws stopped a finger from his sleeve.

The cord parted.

The plug came out like a kicked door. Black water burst sideways across the gallery, carrying glass grit, shale chips, and the hunters' canvas. The man on the shelf threw himself onto the lip. One net anchor tore free; the other wrenched the barbs across Sivareth's scales before the whole rig sagged into the trench.

She turned around me, took the pull on her outermost coil, and drove west. Behind us, the cross-drain ate through the shale neck in three loud cracks. A section of the hunters' path dropped away. All six crowded onto the surviving inner gallery, separated from the mouth by a widening rush of water and from us by the central trench.

They were stranded, not defeated. Two still had bows. Another had a climbing coil looped over one shoulder. Given time after the storm, they would rig a way out.

“Kerric!” Prynn gripped an iron anchor above the broken shelf. “Throw the knife. We can set a traverse.”

I kept the blade against my forearm as Sivareth backed toward the quartz. “There's a sound crack above your left shoulder and a spare piton under the net rope. Use those.”

“You're leaving with that thing?”

The coil shielding me rose. Sivareth turned her head, and every hunter on the gallery went still.

“If any of you descend toward my clutch,” she said, “I will not strand you twice.”

Prynn's mouth tightened. “I brought you into the company, Kerric.”

“You put a wedge under my route.”

“One scale clears every winter debt we carry.”

I pointed west. Sivareth moved.

Water poured cleanly into the north sink now. The lower runnel was already shrinking. Sivareth crossed the first white quartz rib, then the second. Her forward coil passed the third before she lowered my boots to dry stone beyond it.

“Open,” I said.

Every turn left me.

No last squeeze, no delay while she measured the arrows behind us. Her body slid away before I had finished planting my heels. I stood alone on the chamber side of the ribs, knife in hand, with the western cleft open behind me.

Sivareth remained between me and the hunters. Two barbs hung from a scored outer scale. She pulled them free and dropped them into the water.

“The western cleft climbs to open ground,” she said without looking at me. “The rain should soften before midday. I will point out the beginning. I will not accompany you.”

“And Prynn?”

“He has the crack you gave him. If he comes lower, he has my warning.”

I sheathed the knife and returned to the chamber for my pack. The waxed bundle containing the lure still sat in its outer pocket. I could take it down to the caravan, along with Prynn's own inventory marks and whatever account I chose to give. None of that required another hour inside the Hushvault.

My knees shook when I bent to lift the pack. Fever tilted the floor. When the waxed lure knocked against the pocket, I gripped the strap until my knuckles hurt. On the far shelf sat smoked river fish, flatbread, and a pot that smelled of pepperleaf broth.

“I will eat before I take the western route,” I said. “One bowl. I leave when the rain eases.”

Sivareth looked back.

“You have made a remarkably small visit of being devoured.”

“I haven't tasted the broth.”

She considered that with royal seriousness, then drew the pot toward the lamps. I sat on a separate folded fur with my pack beside my left hand and the passage in sight. Cold water had filled both boots during the crossing. My toes were beginning to ache.

Sivareth's tail lay three feet away. She did not move it closer.

“One turn around the boots,” I said. “Below the ankles. Leave room for me to pull free.”

“How much pressure?”

“Enough to warm the leather.”

The end of her coil circled my boots and stopped short of closing. I tested one heel; it lifted easily. Sivareth set a bowl of broth in my hands.

“My first question,” I said, “is about the hunter who reached your clutch stair.”

Her eyes narrowed over the steam. “Then eat before I answer.”
