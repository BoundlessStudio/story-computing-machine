# Opus review: A Reasonable Declaration

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — every line makes sense and every reply follows, but the boastful memoirist and the clerk speak in the same clipped register, and the price arguments end with one of them repeating and correcting the other's words.
- Prose: PASS — varied, concrete comic close-third with no systemic tics; two narration details have nothing to anchor them.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is fully delivered, and the arithmetic and Queen Street canon hold; one cloak staging gap.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; I agree on coherence and the prompt, but disagree on voice, which it did not examine.

The dialogue here works on the basics, which is rarer than it should be in
this collection. Referents resolve, replies answer what the listener heard, and
the physical comedy (the knees, the circlet, the spectacles under the desk)
gives exchanges real consequences. The problem is register. Barnaby is a vain
memoirist who renamed his equipment for better chapter headings, yet on the
page he talks exactly like the clerk assessing him: both average about seven
words a line, both dry and exact. So he never performs for the one admirer he
has, and his genuinely curt moments (the drop below the precipice, the token)
cannot stand out. The price arguments make it worse by ending in repeat-and-correct retorts.
The edit pass should add asymmetry: let Barnaby expand and show off in a few
chosen places, let Fulvia's fandom break her form once, and remove the
repeat-and-correct lines. Leave the good jokes, the circlet scene and the ending
alone.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Boaster and clerk share one clipped register

- Where: L63–L67 (the passage her sister liked), L75–L91 (grip, nick and aliases run), L171–L181 (circlet names and the fort); the pattern runs throughout
- Text:
> "The ascent?"
> "Bootlace. The leather rotted."
> "It would."
> "That too."
> "In the context of that expedition."
- Problem: Identical Voices / Tennis Match. Barnaby renamed dependable kit to make better chapters, and his admirer has both books open in front of him, yet he never performs. His lines average about seven words, the same as hers. L75–L91 is eight alternating lines of two to seven words, in which the owner of a legendary four-named sword answers like a witness under questioning. At L63 he names the ascent and then offers nothing more. Fulvia's fandom reaches the page through props and narration (the pressed flower, "with renewed pleasure"), but her fan lines are as short and orderly as her rulings. With the tags covered, the two are distinguished mainly by who asks and who answers. This also costs the story structurally: its best dialogue idea is that Barnaby goes short when the danger was real (L125, and ideally the token), and that contrast cannot register when he is short everywhere.
- Fix: Give Barnaby two or three places to perform and be deflated or cut off. The natural spots are L63, where he starts on the ascent and she interrupts with the fight she actually meant, so "Ah. Yes." lands as deflation; L173, where he rolls out the other two names with relish; and L177–L181, where he tells the fort as an anecdote that "Me. I stood in different places." then punctures. Keep him short at L125 and over the token. Let Fulvia's fandom outrun her question order once, at L163 or L171, before she returns to the form. Do not lengthen every line; the target is unevenness, not verbosity. Illustrative only: "The ascent? People generally mean the ascent. Eleven days on that face, and the night on the ledge, which the illustrator never —", followed by her unchanged "The fight afterwards. Where you turn back."

### D2 · Major · Price arguments end by repeating and correcting the other's words

- Where: L43–L49 (the opening bill), L93–L99 (one sword, four names), L241–L247 (the cup); see also D3 at L255–L257
- Text:
> "A hundred and forty-eight crowns," he said. "For things already mine."
> For retaining assessed artifacts in this district.
> "But the sword didn't."
> "It was sharpened."
> "Three? It's a cup."
> "It's a cup that heats itself from a charge."
- Problem: Correction ping-pong / Tennis Match. Each dispute ends with one speaker taking the other's exact phrase and correcting it: "For things already mine" becomes "For retaining assessed artifacts"; "Circumstances changed" becomes "But the sword didn't", which becomes "It was sharpened"; "It's a cup" becomes "It's a cup that heats itself from a charge." Both sides are equally brief and equally exact, so the office sounds like a scored debate rather than an old man haggling with a clerk who holds the pen. Barnaby's objections about money, which is the thing he cares most about today, are among his shortest lines. Any one of these would pass on its own. Four or five in a single assessment make the retort the story's default rhythm. The story already shows the better alternative at L249, where Fulvia just waits with her pen and his scalded tongue ends the argument.
- Fix: Keep "You called it the Siege Chorus." (L225) as the one clean knockout; it is earned because it turns his own naming against him. Elsewhere, stop Fulvia echoing his phrasing. At L45, cut "For retaining assessed artifacts in this district." or give the basis of the charge without mirroring his "For"; the remedy and the deadline carry the line. At L245, cut Fulvia's rebuttal so that "I do the work." answers her writing the three down, then run the pen-wait and the tongue as written. The L97–L99 pair may stay once L45 and L245 are fixed, since it is the wittiest of the three. Barnaby's objections may lengthen per D1 (he underlined the total a third time and has clearly rehearsed). Fulvia's answers should stay short or become action.

### D3 · Minor · "Noise" line exists to set up the table comeback

- Where: L251–L257, lifting buckle
- Text:
> "Can't you allow something for the noise?" he asked.
> "From hitting my table? No."
- Problem: Punchline Patch / unclear referent. The buckle's demonstrated defect is that it removes weight but not bulk, which is why he hit the table. "The noise" names neither that flaw nor anything the buckle does. The word seems chosen so that Fulvia can answer "From hitting my table?", and the reader has to work out what reduction he is actually asking for.
- Fix: Either cut both lines and let "Four crowns." stand after the knock, or have him plead the real defect (it keeps the bulk, as she just saw) and let her answer plainly or not at all. Illustrative only: "It does nothing about the size. You saw."

### D4 · Minor · Token answer is composed and repeats the narration

- Where: L275–L283, exit token
- Text:
> "No. The living seam marked it. The Witness closed it after I returned. It only ever held that route, and the route's finished."
- Problem: Exposition / uniform fluency under pressure. L275 ("no route left in it") and L279 (the Witness closing the token) have already told the reader everything this line says. Fulvia only needs the functional answer for the form. It comes straight after the one memory in the story that frightened him, yet it is his most fluent explanation, which works against the story's own habit of him going short when the danger was real (L125).
- Fix: Cut it to "No." plus at most one short clause (for example, that the Witness closed it). Do not alter the canon mechanics: the living seam marks, the Witness closes, and the token holds one route.

### D5 · Minor · Dialogue mechanics

- Where: L21 (arrival), L171 (circlet), L233 (cup)
- Text:
> "This was your third appointment."
> "The Crown of Command," Fulvia said, with renewed pleasure.
> "It is yellow," Barnaby pointed out, before she could speak.
- Problem: At L21 the past tense reads as a slip, because she is looking at today's slip. At L171 the tag names a feeling that her line already shows. "Pointed out" at L233 is a mild said-bookism; "before she could speak" already shows him getting in first.
- Fix: "This is your third appointment." At L171, cut "with renewed pleasure" or replace it with an action (for example, her finding the cover picture); this may be absorbed by the D1 change. At L233, use "said" or no tag.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Two narration details with nothing to anchor them

- Where: L131 (after the sword's charge), L251 (buckle demonstration)
- Text:
> The lace had long since been replaced, but he still disliked anyone touching it.
> scattered the empty cards
- Problem: Nobody has touched the grip. Fulvia inspected the groove and the nick and compared drawings, so the sentence reads like the tail of a cut beat. At L251, "the empty cards" is unclear: all nineteen cards carry names, and none has been described as blank or set aside.
- Fix: Either show Fulvia reaching toward the lace before L127, which would also explain why he pulls the sword nearer, or cut the sentence. At L251, say what the cards are (for example, the alias cards she has already set aside) or just "the cards".

## Recurring patterns

- Repeat-and-correct retorts: L45, L97–L99, L245, L257. Covered by D2 and D3; clear these as one sweep.
- Uniform short lines: both speakers average about seven words a line, and only L177 and L283 exceed 20 words (both explain how an artifact works). Covered by D1; fix by adding a few long lines, not by shortening the rest.
- Exchanges closing on a retort: L49, L99, L225, L247, L257, plus the good ones at L155 and L161. Keep L155, L161 and L225, and let the others end on action, the pen or silence.
- Tags naming feelings the line already shows: L171, L233 (D5).
