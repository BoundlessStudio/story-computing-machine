# The Afternoon We Kept

Emina put her thumb on the table before the wind could take it.

The table was hardly large enough for a thumb, let alone the paper menu that kept lifting at the corners. It stood at the outer edge of the teahouse terrace, where the hillside dropped away in roofs and chimneys and windows catching the late sun. Two cups would fit if they were civil to each other. Three would have required engineering.

Rumi was seven minutes late.

Not enough to be offended. Emina had checked. Eight minutes could be mentioned with dignity; seven made a person look as if she had been counting. She folded the menu into a narrow strip and pinned it under the sugar bowl.

Inside, the keeper slid a wooden shutter halfway across the pastry case. One piece of strawberry cream cake remained on a white plate, tall and slightly leaning, with strawberries pressed into its middle and three red halves on top.

Three, Emina noted. An indivisible number.

Down the lane, footsteps struck the stone stairs in a hurried, irregular pattern. Rumi appeared between two planters with one hand clamped to her hair and the other held out in accusation toward the entire lower town.

“The bus,” she said, arriving breathless, “has developed a personal theory about me.”

Emina looked at the clock over the teahouse door. “Seven minutes.”

“It believes I need adversity.”

“And stairs.”

“Cruel little bus.” Rumi dropped into the other chair. Her hair had escaped its clip on one side, and she was wearing two different earrings: a silver hoop and something blue that might have been a bead or a piece of painted bean. “Have you ordered?”

Emina turned to the pastry case. At that moment the keeper reached for the last slice.

Both women stood.

“That,” Emina said.

“Please,” Rumi added, more loudly, as if Emina had attempted a robbery without manners.

The keeper glanced from them to the cake. “One slice.”

“Two forks,” Emina said.

Rumi was already peering at the row of tea tins. “And the milky one for me. No, wait. What’s the one that smells like oranges?”

“Orange tea.”

“That seems suspiciously straightforward.”

Emina ordered mint. She sat first and chose the chair with the steadier leg. Rumi remained inside long enough to ask three questions about orange peel and come out with the milky tea after all.

“You’ve changed nothing,” Emina said.

“I considered change very deeply.” Rumi lifted the blue earring between finger and thumb. “Also, this was supposed to be the other hoop.”

“That’s a bead.”

“Thank you. I was afraid my ears had become different sizes.”

There it was: the easy opening, offered as if the last three weeks had not been a scatter of unanswered messages, bad timing, and one photograph Emina had regretted posting almost as soon as she posted it. Emina wanted to step into the ease. She also wanted to leave Rumi standing outside it for another seven minutes.

The cake arrived between them.

The cream had softened in the warmth. One strawberry half had begun a slow red slide down the side.

“Rules,” Emina said.

Rumi picked up a fork. “No.”

“One vertical division. Equal sponge, equal cream. The top berries are allocated one each, with the third divided.”

“You can’t divide a strawberry without upsetting its spirit.”

“Its spirit is prepared.”

“Look at it. It trusted us.”

Emina drew a line through the cream with the side of her fork. Rumi leaned over to supervise so closely that a loose strand of hair nearly entered the cake.

“Your side is wider.”

“Perspective.”

“Fraud.”

“You’re sitting at an angle.”

“I’m sitting opposite you.”

Emina completed the cut and moved half the slice toward Rumi. Before she could divide the last berry, Rumi speared it whole.

For one second they looked at each other.

Then Rumi put the strawberry in her mouth.

Emina made a sound so outraged that the keeper glanced out through the half-shutter. Rumi tried to apologize around the berry and could produce only a wet, solemn hum. Emina held the expression for as long as she could. It broke first at the corners. Rumi’s shoulders began to shake. Soon Emina was laughing hard enough to lose hold of her fork, which struck the plate with a small bright note.

The sound went down into the town with the swallows.

They ate. The cake was a little too sweet and the strawberries a little sour, exactly the combination Rumi liked and would later insist no baker ever got right. Years afterward, at a station counter in a town she would not be able to name, she would bite into a strawberry pastry and see Emina’s offended face before she remembered which summer it belonged to. By then she would swear there had been four berries on top.

For now, Emina scraped cream from Rumi’s side with the clean edge of her fork.

“That is reparations,” she said.

“That was load-bearing cream.”

“Was.”

* * *

Rumi had brought six stories to the teahouse and used up four in the first fifteen minutes.

One concerned a delivery left outside the wrong door; one concerned her cousin’s attempt to cut his own hair with sewing scissors; one had no center at all and wandered through a broken sandal, a woman selling apricots, and a dog that may not have belonged to anyone in the story.

“Wait,” Emina said. “The dog was inside the pharmacy.”

“Was it?”

“You told me this already.”

“Impossible. It happened yesterday.”

“Then a dog was inside the pharmacy twice.”

Rumi stopped with her fork in the air. “There’s an organization.”

“Of dogs.”

“Don’t make it sound ridiculous.”

“I didn’t.”

Emina took the larger of the two remaining mouthfuls from her half. She chewed slowly, eyes narrowed toward a window across the valley that flashed orange whenever its curtain moved.

“Someone over there is signaling us,” Rumi said.

“With laundry.”

“Undercover laundry.”

“It says you owe me half a strawberry.”

Rumi put a hand over the fruit on her plate, although the only piece left was a thin red edge pressed into cream. “I’m being extorted across a valley.”

They gave the windows occupants. A long balcony belonged to three sisters who took turns pretending to be one person. A crooked chimney served a baker who made only square bread and denied every complaint. In the house with blue shutters lived a retired opera singer, according to Rumi, or a family with too many towels, according to Emina.

“There are seven towels,” Emina said. “You can see them.”

“Costumes.”

“Toweling costumes?”

“Opera is a larger world than you understand.”

Emina sipped her tea. Rumi watched for the smile that arrived only in Emina’s left cheek and saw it. Relief made her louder.

She began explaining why she had been impossible to find: two extra evening shifts, her cousin moving apartments and then nearly moving back, a dripping pipe, a week in which every sock she owned seemed to be wet. She made the weeks sound crowded and funny, a parade of small disasters that had swept her along. In this version, she had not quite seen Emina’s messages; she had only postponed answering until the postponement itself felt like an answer. She had not looked at the photograph from Emina’s balcony more than once.

“Thursday might work,” Rumi said. “Unless the pipe learns to climb.”

Emina set her cup down. “Last Thursday might have worked.”

Rumi’s next joke came so quickly she did not find out what it was until it was in the air. “Time travel remains underfunded.”

Emina looked at the cup rather than at her.

The keeper began carrying empty saucers in from the other tables. Wood knocked softly against wood as another shutter closed.

“You mean the balcony thing,” Rumi said.

“I mean the message that said Friday, dumplings, chair inauguration, seven.”

“That was Friday.”

“Yes.”

“You said Thursday.”

“Last Thursday might also have worked. I was demonstrating range.”

Rumi smiled. Emina didn’t. The joke had reached the edge of the table alone and fallen off.

“I thought you had people,” Rumi said.

“I know people.”

“In the picture. There were at least—”

“They came later.”

Rumi drew her fork through the crumbs. “Right.”

“Also, people can know other people and still invite you.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

Emina’s voice had gone quiet and careful, each word placed on its own dry square of ground. Rumi knew this manner. If she hurried, Emina would become more exact. If she explained, Emina would find the false seam in the explanation and put one finger on it.

Rumi hurried anyway.

“I saw it while I was carrying a laundry basin and then my cousin called because the wardrobe would not go up the stairs, which I had told him, and I meant to answer after, except then it was late and I thought you’d already arranged everyone, and when I saw the picture I figured you were fine, which you were, obviously. You looked—”

Happy, she had nearly said. Happier. Fine without me.

“You looked busy,” she finished.

Emina moved her cup half an inch, revealing the damp ring beneath it. “I made nine dumplings.”

“Nine?”

“I had eleven. Two tore.”

“You could still eat torn dumplings.”

“That is not the point.”

“No. Sorry.”

For once Rumi did not rush to fill the space. Below them, a bus wheezed around a bend and disappeared behind the roofs. Not theirs yet. Somewhere inside the teahouse a spoon dropped into a basin.

Rumi looked at the cake plate. She had been making a country out of crumbs, pushing the largest ones into a crooked border. “Did you eat all nine?”

Emina’s eyebrows lifted.

“I’m asking properly.”

“I ate four before anyone came.”

“Emina.”

“They were small.”

“They were not small. Your dumplings are the size of fists.”

“Baby fists.”

“That does not help.”

The left cheek gave in first.

Rumi waited. It cost her something visible; she pressed her lips together and kept rearranging the crumbs instead of seizing the smile and turning it into permission.

“I set out two bowls,” Emina said. “Then I put one back. Then I took it out again because putting it back looked pathetic, though no one was there to see it.”

Rumi’s fork stopped.

In the photograph, behind three raised glasses and the blur of someone’s sleeve, a chipped blue bowl had sat at the far corner of the table. Rumi had noticed it because she had given Emina the bowl after dropping its twin in the sink.

“The blue one,” she said.

Emina looked up.

“I thought it was for whoever took the picture.”

“No one took the picture. I balanced the timer on the flowerpot.”

“That’s why half of you was missing.”

“My best half.”

“Debatable.”

Emina’s eyes narrowed, but the precision had gone out of the silence. She turned the plate so the last intact forkful of cake, mostly cream with a good stripe of berry, faced Rumi.

Rumi looked at it, then at her.

“Don’t make this unpleasant,” Emina said.

Rumi ate the offered piece. She closed her eyes as if receiving a sacred rite, but only for a second. “Thursday,” she said, mouth empty. “Tell me a time. I’ll say yes or no while you’re looking at me.”

“That is an extremely low standard.”

“I can go lower.”

“Six-thirty.”

Rumi thought of the shift list folded in her bag. “No. Seven-fifteen.”

Emina considered this. “Seven-ten.”

“You bargain over minutes now?”

“I have always bargained over minutes. You keep arriving after the negotiations.”

“Seven-ten.”

Emina inspected her face, apparently found the offer usable, and nodded. Then she stole the largest remaining crumb.

* * *

By the time the keeper brought them the bill, the sun had lowered behind the upper ridge and left its light caught in everything else: chimney pots, railings, the rims of their cups. Their tea had cooled. A moth bumped once against the lantern beside the door and vanished under the eaves.

The keeper nodded toward the lane. “Last downhill bus in twelve minutes.”

“Fourteen,” Emina said, checking the clock.

“Twelve if you want to sit down on it.”

Rumi looked at the slope below. “What if we run?”

“Eleven, then. You’ll stop to explain the running.”

The keeper carried away the sugar bowl before Rumi could answer.

They should have left. Instead Rumi pointed at a square of gold near the top of the opposite hill. “Opera towels has turned on a lamp.”

“The family with the towels is preparing dinner.”

“Rehearsal.”

“Soup.”

“A tragic soup opera.”

“No one wants that.”

“The spoon loves the ladle. The ladle is promised to a whisk.”

Emina put both hands around her cold cup. “The colander knows and says nothing.”

“Because the colander is full of holes.”

Emina stared at her. Rumi stared back.

It was a poor joke. They both understood this. The understanding made it worse, and then much better. Rumi made the first helpless noise; Emina tried to swallow hers and produced a squeak. Their laughter rose out of all proportion to the colander, the opera, the day. Rumi bent over the table. Emina pressed her forehead to her hands. The terrace blurred at the edges, all amber roofs and darkening leaves, while the keeper waited with the patient severity of someone who had seen customers defeated by far less.

Much later, Emina would lift a cup from a table and find a brown ring cooling underneath. For a moment she would be back inside the ridiculous seriousness of that laugh. She would remember Rumi going quiet before she asked about the dumplings. She would not remember what tea they drank. Some years she would think it had rained.

On the terrace there was no rain. There were only crumbs stuck to Rumi’s sleeve and the bill trying to escape under the empty plate.

“Nine minutes,” the keeper said.

They paid badly. Emina counted the coins twice because Rumi kept moving them into a smiley face. Rumi disputed the value of one coin she had mistaken for another. The keeper removed the plate, destroying the face, and opened the gate onto the lane.

They went down the stairs side by side, then single file where the houses squeezed the lane narrow. Rumi took the lead for six steps, turned to continue arguing about whether a whisk could inherit property, and nearly walked into a planter.

“Forward,” Emina said, catching her by the elbow.

“I can’t tell the story if you keep changing the requirements.”

“Walking forward is not a late amendment.”

They heard the bus before they saw it: the complaint of brakes below the next turn.

“Run,” Emina said.

Rumi ran. One earring flashed silver, the other blue. Emina ran after her, laughing again before there was any cause, though Rumi immediately supplied one by losing a shoe. She hopped back for it. Emina caught the bus door with her palm. The driver sighed at both of them with the weary accuracy of someone confirming an old theory.

In the back seat, winded and warm, they divided the last sip from Rumi’s bottle and disagreed about who had saved whom.

“Thursday,” Emina said as the bus pulled away from the hill.

“Seven-ten,” Rumi said. “Bring the blue bowl.”

“Why?”

Rumi looked scandalized. “For the torn dumplings.”

Emina’s laugh startled two passengers in front of them.

On an evening in her old age, Rumi would bite a strawberry and laugh before she knew why. The terrace that returned to her had four tables and a green railing; the cake had six whole berries. She would be sure of the blue earring and Emina’s left-cheek smile.

In her own old age, in another year, Emina would lift a cup and find the wet ring underneath. Her terrace had rain beyond the railing and only two berries on the cake. She could not recall the teahouse’s name, or whether the spoon loved the ladle. She remembered Rumi going quiet. She remembered the other chair drawn close.

From the hillside below came the bus, not yet missed.
