# The Quiet Party

The Mallowgate alarm began ringing just as the guildmaster wrote *Quiet Party*
in the charter-name box.

The bell did not improve his penmanship. The last stroke kicked sideways across
the paper, making *Party* look faintly accusatory.

“There,” he said. “Since none of you cares to contribute a better name.”

Across the contract table, three adventurers looked at the wet ink. Veda Crake
went very still, which Merrit Loam already recognized as a warning sign from
the stack of reports under her arm. Osket Firth raised his eyebrows with such
elaborate patience that the expression seemed to require structural supports.
Pim Fallow drew a waterproof slip from their case and wrote:

*We object to the assignment, not the rescue.*

They laid it precisely over the charter clause threatening all three with a
month’s suspension from Latchwater contracts if they refused.

Merrit had wanted one uncomplicated field evaluation. One clean report,
correctly witnessed and properly indexed, might put her behind the open senior
registrar desk, where weather happened to other people. Instead, the alarm bell
shook rain from the guildhall eaves, and the guildmaster had exercised the
corrective-composition rule on the three most persistently silent names in his
files.

“A joint civic contract,” he said, “with a conventional chain of command. It
may demonstrate why oral debriefs and audible readback exist.”

Merrit shifted the reports. None recorded a field failure. Veda had been
suspended from civic rescue work after declining three oral debriefs. Osket’s
rigging outcomes were excellent and his written comments on guild procedure
were, in the guildmaster’s phrasing, *unhelpfully complete*. Pim’s contract work
funded a small field practice; their safety record had fewer blemishes than the
guildmaster’s table.

The bell rang again.

A runner’s board at the door now showed the problem in dripping chalk:
Mallowgate relief gate jammed. Two sluice keepers stranded. Reservoir rising.

“Before we leave,” Merrit said, “the rule also states that a corrective unit
clearing a graded civic rescue without safety demerits becomes a standing
local party. Its demonstrated protocol then enters the field circular.”

The guildmaster glanced at the three faces opposite him. “I accept the risk.”

Veda turned her charcoal slate over and drew three columns. Under each she
sketched equipment, weights, and arrows of transfer. Osket unhooked two rope
coils from his own pack, set the lighter at Pim’s feet, the heavier beside
Veda’s shield, and waited until each had inspected the change. Pim checked
their medical roll, wrote *splint wrap*, and held the slip where Merrit could
read it.

Merrit found the missing roll in the guild cabinet. By the time she turned
back, all three were watching for her acknowledgement.

She raised it. “Received.”

The lesson had begun without waiting for its chain of command.

* * *

Runoff had taken four planks from the approach culvert and left one beam over
the brown, churning cut.

“Crossing order,” Merrit began.

Osket crouched, pressed the beam, then bounced once on his heels without putting
his full weight on it. He placed a hook against the stone lip on their side,
laid a second at the far end of the route, and traced the proposed line between
them with one finger. Veda examined the near wall. When Osket held out an open
palm, she planted her boots above the soundest course, put her shield edge
against the masonry, and wrapped his line around both.

He did not load it. He looked at her.

Veda pulled once, tested her stance, and nodded.

Pim showed Merrit a slip.

*LOAD?*

“Nine stone with the pack,” Merrit answered.

Pim adjusted Merrit’s harness only after holding up nine fingers and receiving
her nod. Osket crossed, low and quick, then demonstrated where Merrit should set
each boot. He gave her a spare brass safety hook for the side line. She clipped
it on and went over without so much as a wet cuff.

On the far bank, Veda extended an open hand toward the tube beneath Merrit’s
arm. Merrit passed her the old Mallowgate service drawing. Veda studied it
while Osket brought Pim across, then marked four maintenance positions around
the manual release assembly.

Merrit looked down at her rubric. It separated *instruction sent*,
*acknowledgement received*, and *correction integrated*. Custom expected all
three to arrive aloud.

She checked the second box anyway.

A culvert, she told herself, was a simple thing.

* * *

Mallowgate was not.

The relief works straddled the gorge below the reservoir: a stone gatehouse on
Merrit’s bank, a cracked service platform over the gate, and a spill pool
boiling on the far side. A broken catwalk ended six feet short of the platform.
The two keepers stood marooned there under the counterweight frame, one holding
the other upright.

“Water’s a hand below the crest!” one called. “And the weight’s skipping!”

A raft of storm debris pressed its branches against the gate. A grey-green
reedmaw nest had lodged among them, secured to two forked branches by three
thick loops woven from its outer wall. Two broad-headed reedmaws circled it,
their fringed jaws swelling whenever the chain groaned.

“We retrieve the keepers first,” Merrit said, raising her voice over the water.
“Then clear the relief chain, then release the gate.”

Veda had become still. She tapped a white crack running through the platform
lintel, opened the service drawing on her slate, and sketched the gate in
cross-section. The counterweight bore down on one side; the debris raft and
nest bound the other.

Osket knelt beside the drawing. With loose cord and four hooks, he made a model
of a high traverse from the gatehouse roof to the counterweight frame. He
pinched the cord where a rescue loop would descend to the keepers, then looked
to Veda.

She shifted his proposed anchor two inches left on the slate, away from the
crack.

Pim was watching the reedmaws, not the drawing. They counted three small
movements inside the woven reeds, wrote *YOUNG INSIDE*, and placed a bitterroot
bundle on the spill-pool side of Veda’s diagram. Osket widened his cord loop to
enclose the nest. Veda added a shield-shaped brace beneath the lintel. Pim
crossed out a route that would take the adult animals between Osket and his
anchor, then drew an arrow around him.

Nothing about it was instant. They offered, inspected, altered, and checked.
Every change occurred under Merrit’s eyes.

The counterweight rose half a foot. A worn tooth missed with a crack like a
snapped plate, and the whole frame dropped back.

“Next one locks her solid!” the standing keeper shouted.

Separate operations were no longer possible. Merrit could see that much in the
service drawing and in the dark water climbing the gauge. She took out her
pencil.

“Field plan authorized. Rescue line, nest sling, structural brace. Overlap on
my mark.”

Three faces turned toward her. Veda pointed to each element on the slate. Osket
physically lifted each required rope in sequence. Pim held up the bitterroot,
then pointed to the keepers, their medical case, and the safe bank.

Merrit checked *acknowledgement received* and, with a small private
stubbornness, wrote beside it: *No verbal readback.*

* * *

Osket climbed the gatehouse drainpipe, crossed the roof ridge, and sent a
weighted line over the counterweight frame. Veda caught it on her shield rim
without stepping onto the fractured platform. She fixed the anchor through a
sound iron staple in the gatehouse wall and backed it with a second hitch. Then
she set her shield edge beneath the lintel at the point marked on her slate.
The rescue anchor and structural brace were both taking strain before Osket
clipped on.

Osket clipped on with the nest sling and a short checkline at his belt, then
eased out over the gap.

Halfway across, his boot hovered above a timber brace. Veda’s body locked
still. She rapped the exact pale split in the timber with her charcoal, then
angled the slate until the crack and her sketched load arrow shared one line.

Osket followed her gaze. His eyebrows climbed. Very slowly, he presented the
sole of his hovering boot to the crack, as though introducing two officials who
had failed to correspond. Then he shifted to the upper rope without loading the
brace.

On the platform, the stronger keeper dragged the rescue loop around the other.
Osket fixed his checkline from a platform ring to the high traverse. He pointed
to Veda’s drawn load arrow, pulled the line until she nodded, and marked the
limit of the platform’s expected rise with a twist of pale cord. Then he fed
the broad sling beneath the reedmaw nest without lifting it.

“They’re frightened of the gate noise,” the keeper called. “Can’t get them to
move.”

Pim crossed as soon as Osket secured the loop. Before approaching the injured
keeper, they knelt on the platform side of the nest. Osket pointed out the
three outer loops wound around the forked branches. Pim parted the inner reeds
only far enough for Merrit to see three young huddled in the floating bowl,
then wrote:

*THREE CENTERED. OUTER LOOPS CLEAR.*

They held the slip for Osket and Merrit, cupped both hands around the protected
bowl, and raised three fingers. Osket took the nest’s weight on the sling. With
the tapered end of a spare hook, he opened the first outer loop and lifted it
off its branch, then did the same with the second. The third had cinched tight.
He stopped rather than tugging, showed Pim the snag, and waited.

Pim checked inside again and held up three fingers. Osket eased the sling half
an inch to take pressure off the binding, unwove the third loop strand by
strand, and tucked its free end back into the nest wall. When he lifted the
sling a handbreadth, the intact nest rose while both branches stayed with the
raft. Pim inspected the bowl, raised three fingers once more, and wrote
*PROTECTED.*

Only then did they approach the injured keeper. They touched their own chest
and demonstrated one slow breath.
The keeper’s breath stuttered. Pim repeated the motion, slower. Then they held
up a slip:

*LOOK HERE. ONE STEP.*

They pointed to the loop, waited until the keeper tracked the gesture, and
showed how it would tighten under the arms. Only after the keeper copied the
motion did Pim raise a hand to Osket.

He looked to Veda. Veda tested the anchor and brace, then nodded. Osket hauled.

Merrit received the first keeper at the broken edge and talked them through the
last reach. She did the same for the second, but it was Pim’s pace she followed:
one instruction, one observed response, no movement until fear had made room
for comprehension. Before the final lift, Osket pointed to the pale mark on his
checkline. Veda drew the platform’s predicted upward movement on her slate and
shifted her shield to meet it. Pim waited with the keeper until Merrit, Veda,
and Osket had each acknowledged the changed load.

The keeper left the platform. It rose exactly to Veda’s mark and stopped
against the checkline. Her brace held. The detached nest floated intact in
Osket’s sling.

Both keepers reached the bank. Pim checked the injured one’s wrist, wrapped it,
and showed the keeper how to hold it against their chest.

“Strain, is it?” Merrit asked.

Pim flattened one hand—steady—then wrote *No break. Keep dry.*

A fresh pulse of runoff burst from the reservoir’s feeder cut. It came around
the gatehouse shoulder carrying a waterlogged trunk broadside, already too
close for any line to turn it. Osket locked the nest sling. Veda sank behind
her shield and brace. The trunk struck the debris raft, driving its branches
sideways while the freed nest moved only a handspan in its separate sling.

“Chain!” cried the other keeper.

The new impact slammed the raft against the gate. The last intact counterweight
link snapped, whipped against the frame, and vanished into the water. Veda’s
brace kept the lintel from following it; Osket’s sling kept the nest whole.

The relief gate drove deeper into its channel. Water covered the bottom of the
reedmaw nest. From inside came three thin, reedy chirrs.

At the gauge, the dark line reached half a hand below the crest.

The rescue was clean. Its precautions had held. The storm had supplied the next
emergency anyway.

* * *

The keepers could make the walk back with the gatehouse wall between them and
the gorge. Merrit sent them toward town.

“You’ll have to go too!” one called back. “That lintel’s moving.”

It was. Veda stood below it with her shield canted against the least fractured
stone, tightening the brace after the trunk’s impact. Osket crawled along the
high line, checked each of the three freed outer loops, and settled the intact
nest deeper into its sling. Pim looked into the bowl, showed Merrit three
fingers, then crushed bitterroot in the spill pool’s shallows. Its sharp,
medicinal smell cut through mud and rain.

The adult reedmaws lifted their blunt heads. Pim held the root low, let the
current carry its scent, and backed away one measured step.

Above Veda, a loosened block edged out of the arch.

“Above you!” Merrit shouted.

The nearest reedmaw flared its jaw fringe and lunged from the water—not at
anyone, merely away from the sound. Its heavy tail struck Veda’s brace.

Veda caught the shift behind her shield. Osket did not haul against the sudden
movement; he opened both hands and let slack run through his gloves. Pim
dropped over the nest, forearms shielding the young from the tilting reeds.

The loose block fell six inches and wedged against the release wheel.

For one held moment, nothing moved except the rain.

Then Veda pointed at Merrit’s mouth, at the reedmaw’s swollen fringe, and at the
jammed wheel. Her expression contained no triumph. That was worse.

“Yes,” Merrit said. “That was mine.”

Her command had been conventional, immediate, audible—and unsafe in that
particular instant. The three people whom she had annotated for lack of verbal
readback had corrected for her error without adding one of their own.

The water touched the crest gauge’s final mark.

Veda wiped her slate and redrew the assembly. With the counterweight gone, the
manual release required all four service positions she had marked at the
culvert: one at the animal channel, one controlling the nest sling, one bracing
the lintel while turning the wheel, and one pulling the recessed release pin.
She drew four circles.

Osket took Merrit’s brass audit token from the top of her rubric. He set it on
the fourth circle, then put one hand on the pin housing and the other on
Merrit’s safety line. He waited until she looked directly at both.

Pim wrote *WITH US?*

Veda indicated the wheel and herself. Osket touched the sling line. Pim pointed
from the bitterroot to the adult animals, then showed an open palm for *hold*.
Each looked at Merrit. Nobody presumed her answer.

The observer rule required her to remain outside active work. After a structural
downgrade, it required retreat. The lower Latchwater roofs lay visible beyond
the gorge, rain silvering their tiles. Retreat would put the reservoir through
those homes. It would also leave three young animals tied to a drowning gate.

Merrit wanted the senior desk. Wanting it had been a small, warm room in her
mind for two years.

She took Veda’s charcoal and wrote across the slate:

*FIELD VARIATION AUTHORIZED.*

Then she clipped Osket’s spare hook to the pin-side safety line and stepped
onto the wet stone.

* * *

They rebuilt the plan around the jammed block.

Veda drew a new stress line down the left side of the lintel. She demonstrated
the angle at which she would hold her shield, then placed Merrit’s hand on the
release-pin grip and moved it through its required quarter turn. Merrit repeated
the motion herself. Veda corrected her wrist by two inches and watched her do
it again.

Osket showed how the nest would travel: up clear of the gate teeth, outward
under the high line, then down into the spill pool. He pulled the unloaded rope
through the route. At each turn he stopped for Merrit, Veda, and Pim to inspect
the clearance.

Pim made three slips. *HOLD.* *CLEAR.* *STOP.* They showed each in turn, pointed
to the young in the nest and the adults circling the bitterroot scent, and
checked every face. When Osket pointed out that *CLEAR* might be hidden by the
sling, Pim changed position until the slip remained visible from all four
stations.

Veda raised her hand, palm down, and made one clean downstroke. She pointed to
the same mark drawn beside the four circles. Osket copied the downstroke. Pim
did too. Merrit returned it.

Proposed, inspected, acknowledged. Nothing more mysterious than that.

They moved into position.

Pim waded along the spill pool’s protected edge, trailing bitterroot. One adult
reedmaw followed, jaw fringe slowly settling. The second circled the nest. Pim
stopped. Their *HOLD* slip stayed high.

Osket crouched on the counterweight frame with the sling line around his back
and both hands free to manage tension. He watched Pim, not the rising water.

Veda set her shield beneath the lintel. Stone grated on metal. With her other
hand she took the spokes of the obstructed release wheel.

Merrit fitted the brass hook to the safety staple, wrapped both hands around the
manual pin, and looked along the chain of stations. Pim met her eyes. Osket
followed. Veda last, her face tight with the weight over her shoulder.

Pim backed another pace. The second adult reedmaw turned toward the bitterroot.
They began to lower *HOLD*.

A small head pushed through the nest reeds.

The young reedmaw slid into the water and paddled back toward the gate.

Pim’s hand stopped. *HOLD* remained overhead.

Osket froze the sling exactly where it was. He did not anticipate the clearance.
Veda felt a change in the load and pointed with her chin toward the left stress
line. Merrit looked down. Her safety rope crossed it, pulled taut by her current
stance.

She did not yank the pin.

Osket tapped his own hook, then pointed to the higher staple beside Merrit. She
understood the proposed correction, but he still waited. Merrit pointed from
the higher staple to the rope path and held up a questioning palm. Osket traced
the safer path with two fingers. Veda checked the stone above it and nodded.

Merrit shifted the spare hook to the higher staple. The rope lifted clear of
the crack.

Meanwhile Pim placed the crushed root at water level and retreated sideways,
never reaching for the young animal. It tasted the current, turned toward the
scent, and paddled after the adults. When all three young heads were visible
beyond the sling’s landing path, Pim moved to their new position and raised
*CLEAR*.

Osket looked at the slip, then at Veda. Veda checked the brace and looked at
Merrit. Merrit tested the pin and looked back to Pim.

Four acknowledgements passed around the sluice, each plain enough to audit.

Veda gave the agreed downstroke.

Pim swept the bitterroot toward the safe shallows. Osket hauled. The sling
lifted the nest free of the gate teeth and swung it outward. Veda drove her
shield into the new brace angle and turned the release wheel. Merrit twisted
the pin through its quarter turn and pulled.

For a heartbeat, the gate resisted.

Veda’s boot slid half an inch. She reset it against sound stone, downstroked
again, and heaved the wheel.

The pin came free.

The relief gate rose. Reservoir water thundered beneath it, seized the debris
raft, and drove the branches through the gorge. Osket paid out rope at the rate
he had demonstrated. The nest descended beyond the surge and settled upright
in the spill pool. Pim kept the adults on the sheltered side until the water
smoothed, then released the last of the bitterroot. They nosed back toward
their young.

The crest gauge shuddered. The black line dropped one mark, then two.

Veda eased the wheel into its catch. Osket secured the nest above the pool’s
eddy. Pim counted three young heads, inspected the adults, and raised three
fingers.

Merrit sat down on wet stone because her knees had reached an independent
administrative conclusion.

Nobody had been injured. The lower roofs remained merely wet. The reedmaws had
their nest. The sluice held.

She looked at the rubric plastered to her sleeve. *Acknowledgement received.
Correction integrated.*

The quiet, she understood at last, had been crowded with questions.

* * *

The guildmaster read Merrit’s report the following morning with the expression
of a person who had found a live beetle in a locked cashbox.

Veda, Osket, and Pim sat opposite his desk. They had reviewed the report before
Merrit filed it. Veda had corrected the drawn angle of the lintel brace. Osket
had rerouted one rope in Merrit’s diagram and made her demonstrate the revised
path back to him. Pim had crossed out *animals successfully removed* and
replaced it with *animals safely redirected; nest relocated intact.*

Merrit had accepted all three corrections.

The guildmaster tapped the evaluation line. “Insufficient cohesion remains
available as a finding. Given the absence of oral readback, it would be
defensible.”

It would also leave the senior desk available to her. Through the open office
door Merrit could see it: polished wood, orderly pigeonholes, one small window,
and no floodwater whatsoever.

“It would not be accurate,” she said.

He tapped the next line, harder.

Merrit read it aloud. “Flawless reciprocal coordination. No safety demerits.”

Veda watched her steadily. Osket’s eyebrows had begun another ambitious ascent.
Pim waited with pencil poised over a blank slip.

“I have also recorded,” Merrit continued, “that acceptance under threat of lost
contract access does not support the corrective premise. Their communication
was observable throughout. Mine caused the only preventable complication.”

The guildmaster turned to the charter. His own wet scrawl occupied the name
box. Beneath it, the corrective-composition clause was offensively legible.

“So,” he said, “the Quiet Party is now a standing party.”

Merrit allowed herself one nod.

“And its demonstrated protocol enters the Latchwater field circular.”

Another nod.

From the outer office came the slap of requests landing in a tray. Members had
heard about Mallowgate. Written briefings, diagram space, demonstrated
readbacks, and equivalent acknowledgement options were suddenly popular enough
to require a queue.

The guildmaster looked toward the noise. “The senior registrar will need peace
to catch up on promotion files.”

Merrit watched her quiet desk disappear.

“You,” he said, “will administer this.”

Pim slid a waterproof slip across to Merrit.

*LOAD?*

Beside it, Veda drew an in-tray reinforced well past necessity. Osket placed the
guildmaster’s untouched spare pencil on top of the first request and waited
until Merrit met his eyes.

The portfolio was noisy, inconvenient, and several inches thicker than the
rule that had created it.

Merrit took the pencil. “Acknowledged.”
