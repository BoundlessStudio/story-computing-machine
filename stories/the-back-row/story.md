# The Back Row

*Thirty-one Octobers* is how I plan to begin. It's a lovely way to give out a number. Bess has heard me say it four times and hasn't had a chance to find out what I'll say next. There's a man at the cash window who can't have his windshield clear and his lights off, and he's brought her a page out of his owner's manual to prove it.

“Ray, you do the radio the way you've got it here and turn the rest off. I've got twelve dish towels under this window. There's one with a blue stripe you gave me and on the occasion of our closing I would be very happy to give it back.” She opens the drawer with her foot, finds the stripe on top, and passes the towel out. Ray folds his piece of paper twice more and puts it in his shirt pocket. He drives off with the towel over his shoulder.

Bess has to turn one shoulder down to put anything through the window. She has the shape of the witch hazel at the back of the lot where one of its branches comes out over the gravel. I can picture the flowers on it right now in the cold, the thin yellow twists that look like the pieces Bess leaves on the counter when she cuts her hair. I get close enough to see she's missed a piece over one ear. I could get my thumb under it if we were anywhere else.

“Bess,” I say. “If you'd listen to the first page, so I can see how much time it takes.”

“Joanie, you go put our car up by the witch hazel. Get it in close, so you can just about open the passenger door. Then come back and tell me what you're going to say about me.”

It's the last show at the Winch Drive-In and I've written two pages so everybody in the lot can hear how much my wife has meant to me. That little bit of hair is all I can think about.

I drive up by the woods. Years ago we sold the speakers to a fellow from Barre who collects them. The bare poles give the lot the look of a field at the end of a fair. A strip of October sun lies right across the white screen. From any row but this one you can't see the ridge behind it. I used to stand up here toward the end of the night and let the air get the popcorn stink out of my clothes before I went back for Bess. For a long time I thought she liked her evenings to end with another job she could get done.

The flowers are still easy to see through the few leaves left on the witch hazel. Common witch hazel, the kind that waits till you're putting things away. The night I first heard it I was up here having toast for dinner, leaning toward the open window so the butter would drip outside the car. Something struck the dash. I ducked far enough to bang my elbow on the horn, and down at the window Bess looked right up. A little black seed had landed in the ring of dust in the change tray. I found the brown cases on the shrub, some of them split wide, the new flowers coming out along the very same branch. The seeds had taken all year to get to the point where they could do that. I sat there and finished my toast with the window open in case it happened again.

I take my coat off the passenger seat and pull it on over my good shirt. When I shut the door, a branch passes over the glass with a sound like paper tearing. I check the speech. The second page has a whole thing about the school band, how Bess let them practice on our gravel while the gym roof was being done, and how you could hear the tuba from clear across town on a still morning. I was the one who said yes on the phone and my wife was the one who brought out the chairs. I know exactly how I want that part to sound.

* * *

By the time I get to the booth, the lot is filling in from the middle. Somebody always wants to be exactly where the screen is square to them. Bess has left the light on over the popcorn kettle downstairs. The oil has a sweet edge to it at the end of a night, and judging from the smell she's decided what was left in there at the end of yesterday would do us fine. She comes out of the concession with two cartons filled up past the top. She gives me one to carry up the stairs and sets the other on the ledge by the microphone.

The boy who ran our Thursday pictures last year is already at the projector. I see his coat through the glass of the projection room. He's marking the show times with the same little pencil he used to carry behind his ear. I reckon he came to say goodbye and Bess put him straight to work.

“The car's where you asked for it,” I say. “We have time for my two pages if I can read them to you right now.”

Bess puts her reading glasses on while I'm still pulling the pages out of my coat. She has always done that. I can come into a room with a badly printed receipt or the stone I got out of my shoe and she'll reach for those glasses so she can see what I've got for her. I move the microphone aside and lay the speech where she can follow it with a finger. On the first page I've put a little upright mark every place I think I ought to look up.

“Thirty-one Octobers,” I say. “By the end of October it gets so cold at the cash window that I go home with two good fingers on one hand and one on the other. I can still turn the key in the lock when I get home because Bess puts a cup of hot water on the sill just about every night at seven. I can tell you how many tickets I have sold and how much I have taken for them. I'd have to start over at the beginning of these thirty-one years to tell you all the people who have had a little cup of something from Bess.”

There's a place to look up after that, and when I do, she's got her finger halfway down the page. She's reading ahead. She's reached the sentence about the band and the look on her face says she wants to hurry up and find out what that lot might be doing in her speech.

“Remember when their roof went?” I say. “The tuba player always took his own chair back to the stack by the door. He'd do it with his instrument still around his neck.”

“He was out there this afternoon with two kids. He wanted to know what year we got rid of the speakers. I told him what year he put his tuba through one and he could subtract from there.”

She moves her finger to the next line and a pair of headlights comes on and stays on, low down in front of the screen. The black patches at the foot of the screen go white. Bess pushes up the little side window and leans out to point with her whole arm. Down below, a woman gets out of her car and points up at Bess, pleased to have recognized her. The cold comes in under my cuffs. I catch the first page on its way to the floor.

“Your lights, hon,” calls Bess. “The ones on the front of your car. You can make them go off from outside if you've got the key on you.”

The woman feels the outside of her dress and opens the car again. Bess keeps her arm out until the lights go out. Through the window I can hear somebody's radio checking for us, that soft wash that goes from a hiss to a man's voice and back to a hiss as the person overshoots our number.

“You can see right up to your car from this window,” Bess says. “I had a good view of it the first night you parked there for me.”

“I sat for the whole first picture that night,” I say. “That was a long time to keep a bag of popcorn on my lap.”

“I came up here to find the projectionist and saw his lunchbox on the stool. I thought he'd gone to the toilet and I could be down in five minutes. I put the first reel on and came over to this window. You'd turned the light on inside the car and you were leaning all the way across the seat. Your coat had that white lining that came out of your sleeve every time you put it on. I could see it when you lifted the coat off the seat. I'd got as far as the top step when the phone rang. His wife wanted to know if he'd left his lunchbox. He'd been sick all day at the quarry and he'd gone home. I was going to get down there before you put that coat back.”

I put the first page under the second so one corner of it stays out. Bess is still looking up the lot. I can see from where I stand how far her eye has to go. The first few bays come up to you easy and then there's a rise where the cars go from being makes to being colors. Our car's a dark length against the grass. I remember picking a loose thread out of the lining of that coat and pulling it until it caught, then winding it around my little finger so I'd have both hands to move the popcorn off my lap when she came.

“By the time I put the coat back I thought I'd been downright forward to ask you,” I say. “I couldn't even say I didn't like the picture afterwards. You'd have wanted to talk about it and I couldn't have named a single thing I'd seen.”

Bess asked me to go bowling after that. I was so glad to be asked that I told her I liked it. I had to go on liking it for the best part of a year.

Bess takes off her glasses with two hands. “I told that boy at the projector I'd give him a hundred dollars to come down here tonight. He went through this whole thing on the phone about how he'd do it for fifty. I said I'd put him straight out of the building if he sold me short when it was that important to me. I want to sit beside you in that car, Joanie. I want to be there when they put the first picture up.”

The cold from the window is still in the neck of my shirt. Bess comes back from the sill and leaves the window open a crack. She picks up a piece of popcorn from the ledge and eats it. There's time for me to say any one of the things that come right up to my teeth. The boy would have done it for nothing. I wish she'd told me what she saw while I still had that coat. I go to put my hand through the little space where her elbow comes away from her side. Then I stop, because I want her to get to see me clear that seat on purpose one more time.

“You wanted to say something before the picture,” she says. “I can give you the microphone. It'll take exactly as long as it takes.”

I look back at the speech and find the cup of hot water where I left it. The tuba player's there, too. In my mind his two kids are just big enough to carry the next two chairs in. I have a whole list of the good things Bess has done which she won't think to tell anybody herself. I straighten the pages. Bess holds her hand above the microphone and waits for me to nod.

“Evening,” she says when the switch comes on. “Ray, you've got the towel clear across your windshield. You'll see more if you put it in your lap. I can give everybody another minute to settle while my wife has the microphone.”

She hands it to me and leans back. I can still hear the tick of that witch hazel seed on the dash. It had spent a whole summer swelling on a branch I parked beside every day, and when it struck I ducked as if somebody had taken aim. I can see the white lining of my old coat now as clearly as if I'd found it under the ledge. Bess has had that picture in her head all this time. I wish I could get a look at my own face from where she's standing.

Ray's just below the booth with his window down and his radio up. I draw breath and hear the sound from his car. The first little mark I made on the page is where I would look up and see her if I read the part about the cup of water. Bess lays her hand on my wrist. I almost ask her to move her hand so I can turn the page. I want to hear what she'd say if I asked her a question first.

“I have a seat for you up by the witch hazel, Bess,” I say. “Would you go to the movies with me?”

She tips her head toward the window. A bit of the light from the booth catches the hook of her shoulder in the glass, where a minute ago there were headlights. She can look at the whole field from there and I think she's going to take her time about answering, which is her right after thirty-one years.

“I would,” she says. “I have all my own teeth and I'll pay for the popcorn.”

I laugh so loud I hear myself again out by the stairs. Bess is laughing by the time I find the switch. She takes the two cartons off the ledge, one in each hand, and nods at the window.

“You get our seat warm,” she says. “I'll be right up.”

* * *

I go sideways up the last slope to keep the gravel out of my good shoes. There's enough light through the booth window to show the shape of Bess's head while she talks to the boy. I get into the car and put my coat across both seats. I turn on the dome light and lay the coat over my knees. If she happens to look this way, there it is. I sit under the yellow light long enough for my eyes to get so used to it that when I switch it off again the whole lot goes away.

Bess opens the passenger door about six inches before it gets to the witch hazel. She draws it back and passes me the popcorn, then tries the space with one elbow up. She has to open the door again to put out a branch that's come in with her. One short yellow petal stays on the seat. I catch it with my thumb as I set a carton in her lap.

“Are you going to sit way over there through the whole thing?” she says.

My carton tips. For years I've been able to tell popcorn spilling by its sound, and I can hear it now going down beside the seat belt buckle and around our feet. Bess reaches over to where I am and I put a hand behind her neck. The first light of the picture comes in through the windshield and I have that bit of yellow hair under my thumb at last. I've missed the first shot. I can hear her start to laugh right against my cheek.
