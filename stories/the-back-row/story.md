# The Back Row

## Opening 1

The January after the Winch Drive-In closed, I lifted the rubber liner out of the change cup in my car and found a hard black seed. In October it had come through the open window and hit the dash with such a sound that I'd ducked. I was having toast for dinner that evening and leaning out of the car to keep from buttering my own good slacks. I was close enough to the common witch hazel to see the little brown cups open on it, and new flowers right beside them. I'd seen the flowers coming on for a week. I had forgotten what the plant could do at the other end of a year.

I took the seed indoors to Bess. She put on her glasses before I'd even made it to her chair. If you ever want to know what I'm doing getting out of bed every day after thirty years with a person, there's a minute to watch, the time it takes her to put on those glasses to see what I've got for her.

I had some idea of saying a thing like that to a lot full of people on our last night. What I wound up writing was about a band she'd allowed to practise in the lot when the school roof was getting done. Before the picture that evening I waited by the cash window for a chance to read the speech to her. She had leaned out on the elbow that goes sideways whenever she has to have a word with somebody. I'd been so long on the other side of that window that seeing her from inside it took me back to the time before I even had a key. A loose curl lay against the frame and shivered when she spoke. She always had a bit of hair on that side that was yellow as anything still growing up by the trees. The driver said something I couldn't hear. She turned her shoulder another inch to get at what he was saying. I held the sash up until she was ready to come back in.

## Opening 2

*Thirty-one Octobers* is how I plan to begin. It's a lovely way to give out a number. Bess has heard me say it four times and hasn't had a chance to find out what I'll say next. There's a man at the cash window who can't have his windshield clear and his lights off, and he's brought her a page out of his owner's manual to prove it.

“Ray, you do the radio the way you've got it here and turn the rest off. I've got twelve dish towels under this window. There's one with a blue stripe you gave me and on the occasion of our closing I would be very happy to give it back.” She opens the drawer with her foot, finds the stripe on top, and passes the towel out. Ray folds his piece of paper twice more and puts it in his shirt pocket. He drives off with the towel over his shoulder.

Bess has to turn one shoulder down to put anything through the window. The witch hazel at the back of the lot takes just that shape where one of its branches comes out over the gravel. I can picture the flowers on it right now in the cold, the thin yellow twists that look like the pieces Bess leaves on the counter when she cuts her hair. I get close enough to see she's missed a piece over one ear. I could get my thumb under it if we were anywhere else.

“Bess,” I say. “If you'd listen to the first page, so I can see how much time it takes.”

“Joanie, you go put our car up by the witch hazel. Get it in close, so you can just about open the passenger door. Then come back and tell me what you're going to say about me.”

It's the last show at the Winch Drive-In and I've written two pages so everybody in the lot can hear how much my wife has meant to me. That little bit of hair is all I can think about.
