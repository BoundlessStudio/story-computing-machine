# The Back Row

*Thirty-one Octobers* is how I plan to begin. It's a lovely way to give out a number. I've written two pages about my wife to read on the last night at the Winch Drive-In. Bess has heard me say the first two words four times. There's a man at the cash window with a page from his owner's manual flat on the sill. He cuts his engine and the headlights go out. His windshield's going white at the corners.

“Ray, put the key here for the radio,” she says, tapping the picture of the ignition with her fingernail. “I've got twelve dish towels under this window. There's one with a blue stripe you gave me and on the occasion of our closing I would be very happy to give it back.” She opens the drawer with her foot, finds the stripe on top, and passes the towel out. Ray folds his piece of paper twice more and puts it in his shirt pocket. He drives up into the lot with the towel over his shoulder.

Bess has to turn one shoulder down to put anything through the window. She has the low sideways reach of the witch hazel at the back of the lot, where a branch forks out over the gravel. I can picture the flowers on it right now in the cold, the thin yellow twists that look like the pieces Bess leaves on the counter when she cuts her hair. Below the sill another car window comes down. I get close enough to see she's missed a piece over one ear. I could get my thumb under it if we were anywhere else.

“Bess,” I say. “If you'd listen to the first page, so I can see how much time it takes.”

“Joanie, you go put our car up by the witch hazel. There's that little space past the last speaker pole. Then come back and tell me what you're going to say about me.”

I go up through the bare speaker poles and turn in past the last one, where the ridge shows over the top of the screen. The speakers themselves went years ago to a fellow from Barre who collects them. I used to stand up here toward the end of the night and let the air get the popcorn stink out of my clothes before I went back for Bess.

The flowers are still easy to see through the few leaves left on the witch hazel. Common witch hazel, the kind that waits till you're putting things away. The night I first heard it I was up here having toast for dinner, leaning toward the open window so the butter would drip outside the car. Something struck the dash. I ducked far enough to bang my elbow on the horn, and down at the window Bess looked right up. A little black seed had landed in the ring of dust in the change tray. I'd watched the cases swell all summer. Now I could see them split wide with the new flowers coming out along the very same branch. I sat there and finished my toast with the window open in case it happened again.

I go round for my coat and the passenger door opens far enough to get one arm in before it brushes the witch hazel. I fish the coat out and pull it on. When I shut the door, a branch passes over the glass with a sound like paper tearing, and I feel for the speech in my coat pocket. The Winch High band is on my second page. When they needed a place to practice while the gym roof was being done, I was the one who said yes on the phone and Bess was the one who brought out the chairs.

* * *

The lot is filling in from the middle as I come down from the woods. Somebody always wants to be exactly where the screen is square to them. I stop at the concession, where Bess has left the light on over the kettle. The oil smells nearly sweet. I can tell she decided what she had left yesterday would see us through tonight. She comes out with two cartons filled up past the top, gives me one at the foot of the stairs, and we carry them up to the ledge by the microphone.

Through the glass of the projection room I see a little pencil marking the times on the show sheet. It's the one the boy who ran our Thursdays last year always kept behind his ear. I reckon he came to say goodbye and Bess put him straight to work.

“The car's where you asked for it,” I say. “I'm figuring three minutes for both pages, if you can let me try them right now.”

Bess puts her reading glasses on while I'm still pulling the pages out of my coat. I can come into a room with a badly printed receipt or the stone I got out of my shoe and she'll reach for those glasses so she can see what I've got for her. I move the microphone aside and lay the speech where she can follow it with a finger. On the first page I've put a little upright mark every place I think I ought to look up.

“Thirty-one Octobers,” I say. “By the end of October it gets so cold at the cash window that I go home with two good fingers on one hand and one on the other. Just about every night at seven Bess puts a cup of hot water beside the cash drawer. I dip my fingers in it between cars, and by the time the next one comes up I can tell a nickel from a quarter again. I can tell you how many tickets I have sold and how much I have taken for them. To count up everybody who's had a little cup of something from Bess, I'd have to go all the way back to that first October and start from there.”

I look up where I meant to, and Bess already has the second page out with her finger on the part about the band.

“I keep thinking of that boy with the tuba,” I say. “He always took his own chair back to the stack by the door. He'd do it with his instrument still around his neck.”

“He was out there this afternoon with two kids. He wanted to know what year we got rid of the speakers. I told him it was the October after he put his tuba through one. He took the kids out to show them the pole.”

She moves her finger to the next line just as a pair of headlights turns the foot of the screen white. Bess pushes up the side window to call down to a woman who's getting out of her car, and the cold comes in under my cuffs as the first page sails off the ledge. I catch it on its way to the floor.

“Your lights, hon,” calls Bess. “Turn them off before you get too far.”

The woman leans back into her car and the screen goes dark. Through the window I can hear somebody's radio checking for us, that soft wash that goes from a hiss to a man's voice and back to a hiss as the person overshoots our number.

“You can see right up to your car from this window,” Bess says. “I had a good view of it the first night you parked there for me.”

“I sat for the whole first picture that night,” I say. “That was a long time to keep a bag of popcorn on my lap.”

“I came up here to find the projectionist and saw his lunchbox on the stool. I thought he'd gone to the toilet and I could be down in five minutes. I put the first reel on and came over to this window. You'd turned the light on inside the car and you were leaning all the way across the seat. Your coat had that white lining that came out of your sleeve every time you put it on. I could see it when you lifted the coat off the seat. I'd got as far as the top step when the phone rang. His wife was calling about his lunchbox. She said he'd come home sick. I went back to the projector. I was going to get down there before you put that coat back.”

I put the first page under the second so one corner of it stays out. Bess is still looking up the lot. I can see from where I stand how far her eye has to go. The first few bays come up to you easy and then there's a rise where the cars go from being makes to being colors. Our car's a dark length against the grass. I remember picking a loose thread out of the lining of that coat and pulling it until it caught, then winding it around my little finger so I'd have both hands to move the popcorn off my lap when she came.

“By the time I put the coat back I thought I'd been downright forward to ask you,” I say. “I thought I'd tell you the picture was rotten, to give you some idea what kind of night I'd had. You'd have wanted to talk about it and I couldn't have named a single thing I'd seen.”

Bess asked me to go bowling after that. I was so glad to be asked that I told her I liked it. I had to go on liking it for the best part of a year.

Bess takes off her glasses with two hands. “I told that boy at the projector I'd give him a hundred dollars to come down here tonight. He went through this whole thing on the phone about how he'd do it for fifty. I said I'd put him straight out of the building if he sold me short when it was that important to me. I want to sit beside you in that car, Joanie. I want to be there when they put the first picture up.”

The cold from the window is still in the neck of my shirt. Bess comes back from the sill and leaves the window open a crack. She picks up a piece of popcorn from the ledge and eats it. The boy would have done it for nothing. I wish she'd told me what she saw while I still had that coat. I put out a hand toward the crook of her arm. When she looks straight at me, I set it on the edge of the speech. I wish I could get a look at my own face from where she's standing.

“You wanted to say something before the picture,” she says. “I can give you the microphone. It'll take exactly as long as it takes.”

I put the first page back on top and square them both on the ledge. Bess holds her hand above the microphone and waits for me to nod.

“Evening,” she says when the switch comes on. “Ray, you've got the towel clear across your windshield. You'll see more if you put it in your lap. I can give everybody another minute to settle while my wife has the microphone.”

She hands me the microphone. Ray's just below the booth with his window down and his radio up. I draw a breath and it comes out of his car. I can still hear the tick of that witch hazel seed on the dash. Bess lays her hand on my wrist. I almost ask her to move her hand so I can pull the first page a little closer. Then I see my little upright mark and look up.

“I have a seat for you up by the witch hazel, Bess,” I say. “Would you go to the movies with me?”

She tips her head toward the window. A bit of the light from the booth catches the hook of her shoulder in the glass. I hold the microphone out between us and she leans into it.

“I would,” she says. “I have all my own teeth and I'll pay for the popcorn.”

I laugh so loud I hear myself again out by the stairs. Bess is laughing by the time I find the switch. She takes the two cartons off the ledge, one in each hand.

“You get our seat warm,” she says. “I'll be right up.”

* * *

I go sideways up the last slope to keep the gravel out of my good shoes. The light in the booth is still on. I can see the boy reach past Bess to shut the side window. I take off my coat and get in. I spread it across my lap and the passenger seat. As I switch on the dome light I lift the far end back over my knees and leave the seat beside me clear. If she happens to look this way, there it is. I sit under the yellow light long enough for my eyes to get so used to it that when I switch it off again the whole lot goes away.

Bess opens the passenger door until it rubs against the witch hazel and hands me both cartons through the gap. She pushes the door a little farther and comes in sideways, one elbow up against the branch that follows her. I hold the cartons clear while she pushes the branch out and shuts the door. One short yellow petal stays on the seat beside her knee. I catch it with my thumb as I set a carton in her lap.

“Are you going to sit way over there through the whole thing?” she says.

My carton tips. The popcorn goes rattling past the seat belt buckle and down around our feet. Bess gets an arm around my shoulders and I put my hand behind her neck. The first light of the picture comes in through the windshield as I get my thumb under that bit of yellow hair. The curl is cold and Bess is warm behind the ear. I've missed the first shot. I can hear her start to laugh right against my cheek.
