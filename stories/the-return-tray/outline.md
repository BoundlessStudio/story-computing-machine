# The Return Tray

Provisional direction: a short dark comedy in the prompt's second person, closely held by a mortuary attendant on the last shift before retirement. The magical girl, an adult with a ridiculous combat name and a practical civilian manner, has been a regular for years. She wakes on the slab, yawns, and immediately resumes their argument about the previous chess game. The attendant rolls their eyes: dying does not entitle her to take back a move. A small board has become part of her return tray, beside the effects that resurrection cannot replace.

She wants to beat this particular opponent fairly, an ordinary skill she has worked at and cannot acquire through her spectacular magic. The attendant has enjoyed the rivalry but assumes she enjoys a convenient free opponent; neither has had to choose the other's company outside the room that supplies it. Show that assumption early through an ordinary handoff: the attendant has packed the chess set with things for the incoming worker. Today she has brought her own clock and a studied attack. Their matches are a recurring rivalry, not one impossibly long game. Her restoration is a local spell she sets in motion while alive and which finishes after bodily death; resurrection is familiar here, does not renew personal belongings, and is not universal magical-girl lore.

The final match stays comic and competitive. Work interrupts it in mundane ways, and her bright heroic costume is an awkward fit for both the room and her adult irritability. Retirement comes out through the attendant's practical preparations. She asks a direct question about the packed set: the new worker may be perfectly nice, but she has prepared to beat this person. Do not make the attendant throw the game or make her deliver a speech about the error. Their old no-take-backs dispute supplies trust and comic ammunition; it need not become an allegory for every emotional move.

After the work handoff is complete, the attendant elects to stay for an actual finished game and offers the home address while the result is undecided, risking that she will find this inconvenient or decline. The clock is stopped for this personal choice; once play resumes, the attendant has to defend seriously against her prepared attack and ultimately wins fairly. Her appetite to earn a victory remains. A brief final scene outside the workplace makes her reciprocal choice concrete: she arrives alive, through a front door, on the agreed day, with ordinary shoes and her own chess clock. Their competitive rudeness survives; the attendant still has reason to sigh and roll their eyes. No speech about the value of life, no grim secret price for resurrection, and no institution to reform.

The precise game and interruptions remain open. Protect the prompt's cheerful professional familiarity, the girl's appetite to win, and their right to be petty with someone they trust. Watch whether the handoff assumption is actually demonstrated, the late finish costs something these people would care about, and the attendant risks an invitation rather than merely accepting her proposal.
