# The Return Tray

Provisional direction: a short dark comedy in the prompt's second person, closely held by a mortuary attendant on the last shift before retirement. The magical girl, an adult with a ridiculous combat name and a practical civilian manner, has been a regular for years. She wakes on the slab, yawns, and immediately resumes their argument about the previous chess game. The attendant rolls their eyes: dying does not entitle her to take back a move. A small board has become part of her return tray, beside the effects that resurrection cannot replace.

She wants to beat this particular opponent fairly, an ordinary skill she has worked at and cannot acquire through her spectacular magic. The attendant wants to leave work on time and to end their acquaintance neatly as another finished duty. Today she has brought a prepared opening; the attendant has begun packing the board. Their matches are a recurring rivalry, not one impossibly long game. Her restoration is a local spell she sets in motion while alive and which finishes after bodily death; resurrection is familiar here, does not renew personal belongings, and is not universal magical-girl lore.

The final match stays comic and competitive. Work interrupts it in mundane ways, and her bright heroic costume is an awkward fit for both the room and her adult irritability. Retirement comes out through the attendant's practical preparations. She objects less to the retirement than to learning that she has been classified as someone the attendant only knows at work. The attendant attempts a charitable blunder to give her the victory she wants and make the goodbye pleasant. She recognizes it, refuses the false win, and names the specific offense: she has spent years learning this game; the attendant does not get to decide that being kind means she need never actually succeed.

The repair costs both of them their preferred neat ending. The attendant withdraws the offered victory and plays properly; she accepts that there is no guaranteed triumphant last game. She loses, or they run out of the attendant's remaining shift before a result. She asks where the attendant is moving and proposes a real rematch that requires her to arrive alive, through a front door, at an agreed time. A brief final scene outside the workplace lets their competitive rudeness survive the change: the attendant can still sigh and roll their eyes at her arrival, while she comes bearing ordinary shoes and her own chess clock. No speech about the value of life, no grim secret price for resurrection, and no institution to reform.

The precise game, interruptions, and outcome remain open. Protect the prompt's cheerful professional familiarity, the girl's appetite to win, and their right to be petty with someone they trust. Watch whether the retirement supplies a real choice or only a sentimental frame, and whether a rematch becomes a tidy ritual instead of an altered relationship.
