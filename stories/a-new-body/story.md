“I need a new body.”

“Is there some problem or defect with your body?”

“No, but I’ve met someone, and I’m afraid that when they discover my true nature, they’ll reject me. Can you do this for me?”

Doctor Anjet put down her pencil.

Virelle had gathered herself into the visitor's chair, an approximate woman in a loose grey coat. It was tiring to keep the outline. She let her shoulders come apart. Ivory bodies, each no larger than the last joint of a thumb, lifted on gold-dusted wings and settled along the chair arms, the measuring frame, the pencil Anjet had just laid down. A few stayed inside the copper voice cup on the seat. Their wings trembled against its diaphragm.

“I have six weeks,” she said through it.

“That should be enough. May I?”

Anjet held out two fingers beside the pencil. Virelle stepped onto them and felt the warmth of a living hand through six small feet. The doctor brought that part of her closer to the lamp. From the chair, Virelle watched herself being examined; from the doctor's fingers, she saw white light caught in the lenses of Anjet's spectacles.

“How many?”

“Sixty-three.”

“And you need room for all of them.”

“All of me.”

Anjet crossed out *occupants* and wrote *forms* above the number.

“Yes, I can do it. A body with places for you to rest against the nerves. You supply the thinking. While enough of you is in contact, you'll be able to speak and move through it.”

Virelle left the fingers and took a turn around the lamp. The room smelled of warm growing clay. On a rack behind the doctor, unfinished hands flexed very slowly in their glass sleeves.

“Will it feel things?”

“Provided I do my job.”

“And look ordinary?”

Anjet regarded the hands.

“You'll have to be more specific.”

***

At her first fitting, Virelle tried a left hand.

It lay on a cushion in a pale sleeve that ended at the elbow. She entered through the open end and found the soft channels inside, the small responsive surfaces Anjet had described. The first sensation was so large she rose from the cushion in a startled cloud.

“What was that?”

“The cushion.”

Virelle returned. She rested again against the contacts and spread five fingers. The cushion pressed into the hand's palm, and she felt that pressure everywhere, alongside the cooler touch of the table beneath her other bodies' feet. She curled the fingers into the fabric. A fold gathered and stayed gathered.

“Can I have the other one?”

“You can have everything up to the shoulder today.”

By the third fitting she could close a button. By the fourth she could do it while looking out of her new eyes, without keeping part of herself on the table to watch from below.

She chose deep brown skin, dark gold eyes, and black hair cut at the jaw. Anjet offered a book of noses. Virelle went back twice to a broad one with a small rise along its bridge, which cast a shadow she liked. She kept the first hands.

The body grew in a loose white gown, seated among its supports. When she was wholly inside it, the room narrowed to the view ahead. Then she turned her head, and the room went with her.

Walking took longer than she had expected.

For forty-seven years, if she wanted to cross a room, she had crossed it. Now there was a business of putting weight on one foot, trusting the other, and deciding which way the knees ought to bend. During the first lesson, she held both Anjet's hands.

“Stop looking at your feet.”

“They’re doing it wrong.”

“They’re doing what you tell them.”

At the end of that afternoon, she managed the length of the growing room alone. The floor was warm near the window. She went back to that patch and stood there for a while.

That night, when Sef asked over the reading line why she sounded tired, she said she had spent the afternoon learning something difficult.

“Can you do it now?”

“Badly.”

“Splendid. That's the interesting part.”

Someone else joined the line, apologizing for being late, and Sef did not ask what it was.

He had first stayed on after the others left to argue about a carriage. Virelle had said the heroine of *The Ninth Horseman* could have escaped six chapters earlier if she had taken the carriage standing in the courtyard. Sef had said the driver was in it. The following night, he returned with the book and the page number.

She had made him read the next paragraph, in which the driver went to breakfast.

After that, they found reasons to remain on the line. He liked stories in which people rode through fire and made promises from the tops of towers. He read them with his whole voice. Virelle, who could watch a room from twenty corners at once, would gather around the voice cup and listen to him climb a perfectly unnecessary tower.

She knew that he had a chipped front tooth, because he had complained about biting thread with it. She knew he was forty-five, and that he put off answering letters until he could no longer find them. He knew she liked the hour before sunrise and always lost count when pouring things. Neither of them had asked what the other looked like.

Once, many years earlier, Virelle had shown herself to a man who liked her voice. He had watched her fill his sitting room, then kept looking toward the door. Even after she explained, he asked where the woman was. The next evening he had not answered her call, nor any evening after that.

When Sef suggested they meet, she had said yes before the fear caught up with her.

At the last fitting, Anjet showed her the access seam at the back of the new neck. It opened beneath the hair.

“I can't reach that easily,” Virelle said.

“You can use a hook.”

She pictured undressing each night, finding the hook, and hanging her hair out of the way before she could stretch herself around her own room.

“Can I have an opening here?” She turned up a palm. “For going out. And coming back.”

Anjet drew a curve across it with the blunt end of a pencil.

“About so?”

“Yes. Will it show?”

The doctor pressed the skin gently together.

“When it's closed, no.”

Virelle looked at the place where the line had been. Then she held out her other hand.

“Both, please.”

***

On the evening they had chosen, the glasshouse stayed open late for the winter flowers. Virelle arrived twenty minutes early. She walked once around the outside, then went in and was astonished to find herself too warm in her coat. She took it off, smoothed her green dress, and held a wrist against her cheek to feel how hot she had become.

She was still enjoying that when Sef arrived.

He was narrower than his voice, with a short beard going grey at the chin and a blue coat buttoned wrong at the bottom. There was the chipped tooth. He had a book under one arm.

She knew him before he spoke.

“Virelle?”

“You've brought evidence.”

He looked down at the book, then back at her. His smile opened slowly.

“I thought we might get through one chapter without somebody's aunt ringing on the line.”

“Which chapter?”

“The escape.”

“There are four.”

“The good one.”

“That narrows it less than you think.”

For a moment they stood smiling, with all the familiar ease of their conversations and none of the wire between them. Virelle had wondered what to do with her hands. Now she wanted to put one against his cheek. She folded them around her coat instead.

They went into the east gallery, where tall, red-leafed plants nearly touched the curved roof. White flowers hung among them in loose bunches. Most were still shut. A couple on the next path passed without looking twice at Virelle; one of them was pointing out a flower beginning to open.

“I expected more people,” she said.

“I checked. The flowering is better tomorrow.”

She turned to him.

“Then why did you suggest tonight?”

“Fewer people.”

He had said it so promptly that she laughed. His eyes stayed on her face, and she realized he was hearing her laugh in the same air for the first time.

They found a bench beneath a plant whose leaves spread over them like fingers. Virelle folded her coat over one end. Sef sat, opened the book and inspected a damaged corner.

“You have read that rather hard,” she said.

“It’s a book.”

“You could let it rest between readings.”

“It rests while I'm at work.” He turned to a page marked with an old ribbon. “Now. Shall I begin, or will you tell me again about the carriage?”

“How long did the driver take over breakfast?”

“Ten minutes.”

“I think I could steal a carriage in ten minutes.”

“I'm beginning to believe you.”

He took a breath, and his shoulders changed. She had never seen the small physical preparations he made to become someone else.

“‘By the time the ninth rider reached the gate,’” he began, “‘the bells were already sounding from the western tower.’”

His ninth rider had a splendid, entirely unreasonable voice. Virelle let her head rest against the bench and listened. She could feel the air from an open roof vent on her cheek. Her feet sat squarely on the flagstones. It was a comfortable arrangement, this sitting beside someone.

Sef read the rider's leap from the tower to the moving carriage with enough conviction to set his free hand gripping the bench.

“Stupid man,” she murmured.

He stopped.

“Reckless.”

“He threw away the rope.”

“He cut the rope so they couldn't follow.”

“Before he jumped.”

“Yes.”

“He might have missed.”

“She was driving toward him.”

There was a little heat in his answer. Virelle looked up from the page. He was looking at her over it, with his thumb pressed hard against the margin.

She had meant to tease him. He wanted her to see the thing he saw.

“Go on,” she said.

“You don't have to like it.”

“I want to hear the jump.”

He considered her, then went back a sentence. This time, she watched his face as he read. He knew exactly where the carriage would be, and what the rider would seize, and how the girl driving would turn with one hand still on the reins. His whole body leaned toward the catch.

When he reached her first line, Virelle took it.

“‘You’ve ruined my best carriage.’”

Sef's eyes lifted. He gave her the rider's answer, delighted, and she answered him again. They passed the voices back and forth until a woman farther along the gallery glanced over. Virelle lowered her voice. Sef lowered his too, which made the villain sound as though he were hiding under their bench.

She lost her place laughing.

He marked it with a finger and waited for her. When she put her hand beside his on the seat, he looked at it, then at her, and turned his palm upward.

Virelle laid her hand in it.

There was nothing difficult about this part. His fingers folded around hers, and she folded hers around his. The skin beneath his thumb was rough. When he shifted his grip she felt the movement through the long bones of the new hand, through its wrist, throughout herself.

She pressed a little harder. He answered.

They sat that way while he read the next paragraph. She listened to his voice and watched the hollow at the base of his throat move with it. A grey curl lay against his collar. Beneath it there would be warmth, and the vibration of each word he made. She could rest lightly there, and along the back of his hand, and at his wrist. She could feel the same pulse arriving in several places.

Her palm was warm against his. She wanted to keep it there. She also wanted to come closer in the way she knew best.

Sef turned a page one-handed and, without pausing, made the housekeeper sound exactly like the rider.

“I think your hero's mother has arrived,” Virelle said.

He looked at the page.

“Oh, no. I'll never get her back now.”

She smiled, but she could feel herself gathering inside the new body's channels, as she did before a flight.

“Sef.”

He stopped trying the housekeeper's voice.

“I'd like to touch you another way.”

He went very still. A little colour came into his cheeks.

“All right.”

“I need to show you something first.”

He waited.

“This body is new. I bought it for tonight.”

His hand loosened without leaving hers.

“Bought it?”

“I live in small bodies. A lot of them. All the time we've been talking, I've been sitting around a voice cup, making the words with my wings.”

He looked at her mouth. She had practiced smiling with it for an hour before she left home.

“I wanted to meet you,” she said. “I was afraid that if I told you, you wouldn't come.”

Sef set the book beside him, face down, keeping the place.

“Small how?”

She drew her hand from his and laid it open on her knee.

“May I show you?”

He nodded.

She opened the seam. It rose in a small curved door beneath her fingers. One part of her climbed into the light, gold on ivory, and spread its wings. Another followed, and another. She felt the cooler air outside the new skin. From the first pair of eyes she saw Sef's face turned up toward the face she had chosen.

Then he jerked away.

His heel struck the leg of the bench. The book slipped. Virelle caught it with her other hand before it reached the floor, and at the same time flew upward to a leaf.

“Wait,” he said. “Give me a moment.”

She stopped.

Three of her rested outside the body. The rest held its eyes open, its feet against the stones, its mouth closed. She could see Sef from above and in front. There was no angle from which he looked as he had a moment earlier.

He rubbed the hand that had held hers.

“Which one is you?”

“All of them.”

His eyes went to the leaf.

“That one too?”

She lifted her wings once.

He looked back to her face.

“And you're still in there?”

“Yes.”

She had caught the book by its pages. They were crumpling in her fingers. Carefully, she eased her grip and set it between them.

“How long have you had this?” he asked.

“It was finished yesterday afternoon.”

The flower above his shoulder had opened while they were reading. Its white petals turned slightly backward at the ends. From her place on the leaf she could smell it; from her new nose she could smell Sef's coat, warm wool and a trace of smoke.

He was looking at her hand now. The little door stood open.

“Did you mean to tell me?”

“I didn't know.”

He took a breath, then let it out again.

“I wish you'd told me on the line.”

She could shut the palm. Bring herself back inside, put on her coat and walk away. She knew how to do all those things now.

“I'll go if you'd like,” she said.

He shook his head, then stopped, as though even that had been too quick.

“I don't know what I thought you were going to show me.”

“I've bent your book.”

“It’s a book,” he said.

He gave a short, uncertain laugh. Then he looked toward the path, where the other visitors were moving beneath the plants. Virelle waited with her hand open. It had begun to feel heavy.

“Are they fragile?” he asked at last.

“My wings are.”

“Would I hurt you if I touched—” He stopped and spread his hand beside her knee. “How do I do it?”

She looked at his fingers. They were not quite steady.

“Let me land. Don't close your hand.”

He laid it palm up on the bench.

She left the leaf and came down slowly. He flinched when her feet met his skin, but he kept his hand open. She stood on the pad beneath his thumb. It was warmer than the air. Another part of her came from the edge of the cuff and settled on his first knuckle. The third followed to the base of his little finger.

His pulse reached her in three places.

Sef watched. He turned his hand very slightly, and she adjusted her feet.

“There?” he said.

“Yes.”

He looked from his palm to her face. She could feel his breath against the smallest hairs of her wings.

“When I spoke just then, could you feel it?”

“A little.”

He said her name, much more softly than he had when he arrived.

“Yes,” she said. “Like that.”

He sat back against the bench. There was space between their shoulders. After a while she closed the little door in her palm and lifted that hand from her knee. He met it with his free one.

They stayed there until the bell sounded from the entrance gallery.

Sef looked down at the book between them.

“I don't think I can do the voices now.”

“You don't have to.”

“I'd like to finish the page.”

She moved all three small bodies from his palm to his wrist so he could pick it up. He lifted the book with that hand, then shifted closer and laid it across both their knees. The crushed pages would not lie flat. He smoothed one with his thumb.

“Here?” Virelle said, finding the line.

“Yes.”

Before he began, she looked at the grey curl lying against his collar.

“Could I sit there?”

He followed her glance, then nodded. She moved from his wrist and settled beneath the curl, barely touching the skin. He raised his shoulder at the tickle, lowered it carefully, and waited until she was still.

Then he read. The first words were uneven. She felt them forming against her feet before she heard them.

Her new hand lay warm inside his. With her free hand she held the page down, and when her line came, she answered.
