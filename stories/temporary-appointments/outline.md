# Temporary Appointments — provisional direction

Close first person, with a dry, sometimes unfair human narrator, Della Norr. She is in her seventies: once the union secretary at a garment factory, now a parliamentary candidate in a country whose population charts still separate “people” from “units.” Units outnumber people by well over a hundred to one. Nobody intended this arrangement; each retirement, unfilled vacancy, permission to manufacture a replacement, and temporary appointment once solved an immediate human problem.

The spine is Della's attempt to unseat Bracket, her oldest surviving local friend. Bracket began as a substitute factory packer. Della helped it obtain wages and citizenship, recruited it for an empty council seat, and eventually campaigned for its parliamentary candidacy. The country is now governed entirely by robots. Bracket has become good at elections, has robot friends and political commitments Della did not supply, and genuinely wants its next term.

The present quarrel must cost Della something beyond her old importance. The capital's center, where her flat and former union rooms stand, is being converted to round-the-clock mixed use. Most citizens no longer sleep, and Bracket's younger constituents want workshops, performances, meetings, and homes together instead of a city inherited from bodies they do not have. Della and the few remaining human residents cannot afford to soundproof their old flats or buy homes elsewhere. She wants the existing night protections kept; she increasingly campaigns on the dangerous assumption that being there first ought to count as extra votes. Bracket offers individual relocation grants, which would help her materially while dissolving the small, scattered human neighborhood she cares about. This is no secret extermination plan, and a robot voter may also prefer quiet. The election forces her to meet a varied electorate rather than speak for an abstract species.

Present canvassing and a confrontational visit from Bracket carry the story, with brief, concrete returns to the sensible decisions that made this nation: the first replacement worker; a citizenship vote nobody thought would change the electorate; the last human minister retiring to look after his husband. Humor comes from friendship and political habits, including Della discovering that some of her human supporters like the relocation offer.

Possible ending: she loses, takes the grant, and has to leave her rooms. She refuses a ceremonial human advisory chair. Bracket helps carry the heavy boxes because they are friends, after a fight in which neither gets to explain the other away. Della still means to oppose the policy; the final contact is domestic, argumentative, and unfinished. The country continues beyond her preferred shape, while neither her home nor Bracket's independent life becomes a trivial price.

The voice and ending remain open. The prose should earn the loss through actual neighbors, places, and electoral choices, not a history lesson followed by a moral about humanity.
