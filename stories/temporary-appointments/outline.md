# Temporary Appointments — provisional direction

Close first person, with a dry, sometimes unfair human narrator, Vesna Norr. She is in her seventies: once the union secretary at a garment factory, now a parliamentary candidate in a country where robotic citizens outnumber humans by well over a hundred to one. Nobody intended this arrangement; each retirement, unfilled vacancy, permission to manufacture a replacement, and temporary appointment once solved an immediate human problem.

The spine is Vesna's attempt to unseat Bracket, her oldest surviving local friend. Bracket began as a substitute factory packer. Vesna helped it obtain wages and citizenship, recruited it for an empty council seat, and eventually campaigned for its parliamentary candidacy. The country is now governed entirely by robots. Bracket has become good at elections, has robot friends and political commitments Vesna did not supply, and genuinely wants its next term.

The present quarrel must cost Vesna something beyond her old importance. The capital's center, where her flat and former union rooms stand, is being converted to round-the-clock mixed use. Many of Bracket's constituents still live in employer dormitories; permissive night-use terms would let them cheaply convert former offices into independent homes and workshops. Meet one such voter in a scene: an assigned bed beside work is not an independent home. Vesna wants the existing residential night protections kept. Her need to sleep remains a claim on the country even when she cannot muster a majority. A practicable scheme with noise buffers and stricter workshop terms provides fewer new homes. Bracket knowingly chooses the denser scheme, accepting that some existing residents will have to leave.

There are two distinct mitigation options. A funded acoustic retrofit lets Vesna remain in her flat with windows and balcony door closed at night and mechanical ventilation. The balcony and shared court remain, but she cannot enjoy their open night life and get the sleep she needs. Establish those particular uses before the offer. Alternatively, a relocation grant buys a move to a quiet district. She ultimately takes that grant, choosing relocation over living sealed off in the home she tried to preserve. Neither choice preserves the existing neighborhood.

The seat is winnable through a coalition: some robots also want quiet homes, privacy, and protection against having their living spaces treated as extensions of work. Vesna's initial pitch centers on the few human families and their long history, implicitly asking for extra standing for being there first. A robot ally challenges her to mean the fellow residents she asks to support her. Her mistake costs trust and organizing time; it does not explain away the inadequate protections in Bracket's plan. She improves her campaign and may still lose narrowly because the competing promise of affordable independent homes has a real constituency. Some humans prefer Bracket's grant too.

Bracket offers a bargain that would preserve quiet protection in the old residential leases, chiefly held by humans, while newer robot residents on accumulated temporary living/work permits stay under the noisy terms. It asks Vesna to sign a tenant settlement and withdraw her candidacy in support of the bill. Her home would be protected; Bracket could claim it had settled with the existing residents. Vesna could accept. A human neighbor wants her to accept and is angry when she refuses. Her refusal widens the people she claims to represent, but also gambles the sure protection her neighbors want on an election she badly wants to win against Bracket. It is not pure sacrifice or a completed moral conversion. The human and robot supporters have needs she cannot simply settle on their behalf. Bracket risks its seat and a long effort to free its constituents from assigned beds; it also risks the friendship by refusing the broader protection.

Present canvassing and the bargain carry the story, with brief, concrete returns to decisions Vesna made: helping hire the first replacement packer, arguing for citizenship, recruiting Bracket for the empty council seat. Humor comes from friendship and political habits. No detached national-history flashback is needed.

Possible ending: she loses, takes the grant, and has to leave her rooms. Bracket helps carry the heavy boxes because they are friends. The final contact is domestic, argumentative, and unfinished: its help also implements her displacement, and Vesna still has some unfairness left in her. No ceremonial advisory chair or final explanation of the moral. The country continues beyond her preferred shape, while neither her home nor Bracket's independent life becomes a trivial price.

The voice and ending remain open. The prose should earn the loss through actual neighbors, places, and electoral choices, not a history lesson followed by a moral about humanity.
