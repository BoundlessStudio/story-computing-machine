# Opus review: The Kingdom of Later

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the slater's reply at the trowel beat (L99) does not follow; the attic exchange with the fairy has an uptake slip, borrowed vocabulary, a set-up-for-correction line and an opaque rule sentence; the opening scenes run on the narrator's comebacks.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, well-paced craft prose; only a few narrative caps, two opaque phrases and incidental gutters.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the prompt's promise, POV, length (about 3,090 words) and resolution are met, but what the slater's recorded promise holds is shown inconsistently (L79–L99), and there are small token, layout and object slips.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree — it passed an incoherent reply at L99 and a fairy exchange whose replies answer things nobody said.

The dialogue is best when bodies are under strain. On the roof, lines shrink to "Knee right." and "Hammer. Mine. Now." and every word carries weight, and the miller ending is plain and exact. It breaks where the story has to teach its magic. There the talk turns into rulings: the slater's explanation (L99) says the opposite of what the rule needs; the narrator suddenly speaks the fairy's words ("undertaking", "compelled") just before the narration says her categories are "all piled together under the word *roof*"; and the fairy answers a promise that was never made. The opening collector and clerk scenes lean on three parallel comebacks from the narrator, which make a cobbler watching damp curl half a winter's money sound like a wit scoring points. The world does earn careful, "now"-weighted speech from commoners who have learned not to promise, and the clerk's passive process is good satire; the edit should keep both and fix the specific exchanges where replies do not follow what was said, where the reader cannot recover the rule, or where the narrator trades epigrams instead of pleading, bargaining or going quiet.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Opening comebacks and a due-now slip

- Where: L29–L43, rent collector at the door; L51–L55, roof clerk's counter
- Text:
> "The roof was due before the last three rents."
> "The rain has joined them."
> "My leather does not."
> "I can record interior damage for future assessment. Payment remains due now."
- Problem: Epigram/last-word pattern (Punchline Patch). The narrator's first three lines to other people are parallel retorts built on the other speaker's wording (due/rents, processes/joined, eligible/does not). A cobbler with damp curling half a winter's money should sound anxious, short or bargaining, not like she is winning a debate. Two Ground slips ride along. Rent is weekly (L25, L29) and the token has slid for three months (L17), so "the last three rents" undercounts by about ten. The collector says rent is due "at sunset" (L29) and then "due now" eight lines later; the narration builds its aphorism on the second phrase (L39), so the contradiction sits under a rule the story needs.
- Fix: Keep at most one dry line from the narrator across both scenes; replace the others with what she needs from each man (time, someone on the roof, a look at the damage). Fix the arithmetic. Make the collector's "due now" agree with a sunset collection so L39 still stands, and let him sound like a working collector rather than borrowing the clerk's "interior … assessment". Illustrative only: "It's due now. I collect at sunset."

### D2 · Blocking · Slater's reply does not answer "other hand"

- Where: L95–L99, in the lane below the roof
- Text:
> "Use your other hand," I said.
> "I undertook the observation, not the trowel. The grace is thorough when it gets the chance."
- Problem: Ground/uptake. The narrator suggests he point with his free hand. His reply distinguishes the observation from the trowel, which read plainly argues that the trowel should be free, the opposite of what just happened, and it never says whether the other hand would work. This is the beat meant to show the reader what a recorded promise holds, and the reader cannot recover the rule from it. "The grace is thorough when it gets the chance" is a last-word aphorism covering the gap.
- Fix: After C1 settles what is held, have him answer the question asked: either hand is held because what he promised the clerk was to point; he can still say where. Cut the aphorism. Illustrative only: "Won't matter which hand. I told him I'd point."

### D3 · Major · The present-request test is unstaged

- Where: L111–L117, just after the catch
- Text:
> The trowel still hung at his thigh. The slate rested freely in my arms.
> I set the slate on my bench. "Put it beside the basin now. If you choose."
> I put my hand on the fallen slate and lifted it. Nothing stopped me.
- Problem: Staging, and a line decoded only by the narration after it. They are in the lane (L91) with the slate in her arms; the next sentence puts it on her bench indoors with no move inside. She then asks him to move the slate she has just set down, and only "No thread stirred" tells the reader this was a test of whether a present, optional request binds. It is the story's rule-discovery beat and must read on first pass. The old review cites this sequence as making the test possible; the test is right, the staging is not.
- Fix: One clause taking them indoors (or keep them outside and use a lane-side sill or step). Let the request show it is a test as it is spoken, for example by her watching the thread at his wrist, so "No thread stirred" confirms rather than explains. Keep "now" and "If you choose".

### D4 · Major · Fairy answers a promise that was not made

- Where: L161–L163, attic, knife held
- Text:
> "I want to patch it myself."
> "A replacement promise does not release the first one.
- Problem: Ground/uptake. The narrator states a wish; the fairy, the one speaker who keeps every category separate (L173), replies as if she had promised. The reply answers what the author needed to explain, not what the listener heard.
- Fix: Either have the narrator actually offer a promise (a spoken "I'll do it myself, then") so the fairy's reply follows, which also makes the swallowed "I will do it" at L201 a lesson learned on the page; the withdrawal's "without a substitute undertaking?" at L199 already covers that spoken promise. Or keep "I want to" and have the fairy treat it as a want before stating the claim's hold. The first is the smaller edit and strengthens L201.

### D5 · Major · Borrowed vocabulary and a line set up for correction

- Where: L165–L171, attic
- Text:
> "Then move the undertaking back to now."
> "No one is being compelled. No one is here."
> "You are here. The slater is below. The rope-maker has accepted one present act. I will protect each refusal separately."
- Problem: Identical Voices plus Punchline Patch. The narrator suddenly talks like the fairy ("undertaking", "compelled"), and two lines later the narration says her categories are all "piled together under the word *roof*" (L173), so the gloss contradicts the lines it follows. "No one is here" is plainly false (she is standing there) and exists so the fairy can correct it. The fairy's answer also does not follow: nothing said explains why moving the crown's promise to now would compel the narrator, the slater or the rope-maker, none of whom made it, and the slater's presence below has not been shown (he is first placed at the ladder at L209). L165 and L175 ask the same thing (make the remedy happen now / make the clerk send someone).
- Fix: Cut L165–L171 and let L175 carry the question, or give the narrator one line in her own terms (the water, the leather, being allowed to cut) and have the fairy answer that directly. If the fairy is to guard the slater's and rope-maker's refusals (paid off at L227–L229), tie it to something in view: the rope and slates the narrator has just bought from them. Keep L173 once her lines are messier than the fairy's.

### D6 · Major · The grace's rule is opaque at the premise beat

- Where: L179–L181, attic
- Text:
> "No. The grace preserves the right not to begin work merely because a crown or master has made later impossible. It cannot judge whether delay is being abused."
- Problem: Ground/referent at the story's key exposition, the "because" the prompt asks for. "Made later impossible" has to be decoded as "demanded the work at once", and "the right not to begin work merely because…" stacks negatives so the reader cannot easily tell who is protected from whom. The second sentence lands; the first does not.
- Fix: Keep the content (it began as a protection against being forced to start promised work early; it cannot tell honest delay from abuse) in words a cobbler could repeat, one short sentence for each half. Do not replace it with an aphorism.

### D7 · Major · Rope instruction contradicts the geometry

- Where: L219–L233, the rope-maker's pull
- Text:
> "Draw it toward the chimney now," I called. "Stop when the knot reaches the gutter."
> "She may stop before the knot," the fairy said.
- Problem: Ground/staging. The knot is where the rope is tied through the leather at the eave (L219); it later catches halfway up the slope (L233) and the narrator crawls up to free it (L241). Pulling toward the chimney moves the knot away from the gutter, so "stop when the knot reaches the gutter" names a point it has already passed, and the fairy's clipped "before the knot" has no usable referent. This beat forces the climb, so the reader needs to see it.
- Fix: Give the stop signal something the rope-maker can actually see or hear (the narrator's call, or a marker on her side of the ridge) and match the fairy's line to it. Illustrative only: "Draw it up toward the chimney now. Stop when I shout."

### D8 · Minor · Small line-level slips

- Where: L51 (clerk's counter), L85 (clerk and slater), L123 (rope stall), L211 (ladder), L299 (roof)
- Text:
> "The slate over my stock is open.
> "Don't add to it."
> "No hemp left dry, and I can't close my hands by noon."
> "No higher from the ladder. Your weight goes to the roof before your foot."
> I moved it before it broke.
- Problem: L51: a slate is not "open"; the roof is. L85: untagged right after the clerk's line, so the reader must infer it is the slater and not the narrator. L123: "can't close my hands by noon" reads like a scheduling limit rather than swelling. L211: the instruction (put your weight on the roof before lifting a foot off the rung) is not recoverable on first read. L299: two "it"s with different referents (hand, edge) in one short sentence.
- Fix: One-clause repairs: "The roof over my stock…"; a tag or beat for the slater; "my hands won't close by noon"; a plainer weight-and-foot instruction; name the hand or the edge.

### D9 · Minor · Mirrored antithesis in the roof craft exchange

- Where: L255–L259, on the roof
- Text:
> "A loose seam drinks water."
> "A roof sheds it. Top over bottom. Ease your pull."
- Problem: The cobbler-versus-slater clash is the right content, but "drinks"/"sheds" is a matched pair that reads as authored, unlike the plainer roof talk around it.
- Fix: Keep the disagreement and "Top over bottom. Ease your pull."; break the mirror so the slater answers with the practical point.

### D10 · Minor · Slater walks off without his boots

- Where: L313–L321, after the patch
- Text:
> "Your heel repair is due now."
> "Leave the boots."
> He did, and limped toward his paid roof.
- Problem: Staging. As written he leaves his boots and limps off, bad knee and all, to work another roof in stockings. "Your heel repair" can also be misread as a repair to the narrator's heel. The "due now" callback to the collector is good.
- Fix: Make clear whose repair it is and how it is paid now: he sits while she does it, or she does it before he goes. Keep "due now".

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Major · What the slater's recorded promise holds is inconsistent

- Where: L79–L99, clerk's office to the lane
- Text:
> "I'll point out the damage from the lane. Nothing else."
> The slater tried to lift his trowel. It jerked flat against his thigh, the first useful motion of the promise already held for dawn.
> "I can look from here," he said. "There. Rotten lath under the left corner. Patch can't bear on that."
> He raised the trowel to point. It would not rise.
- Problem: His recorded promise is to point out the damage, and it is "held for dawn". At the office his trowel is stopped although nothing he could do there is a useful motion toward her roof, and the reader is not told why he lifts it. In the lane he then points the damage out in words, and the trowel is stopped a second time. The reader cannot tell whether the hold covers the observation, the pointing gesture or the trowel, which is why D2 collapses. The later knife hold (L141), with buying and carrying left free, is consistent enough and needs no change.
- Fix: Put one rule on the page: the first physical act of the promise (the pointing gesture, either hand) is held; speaking is not. Cut the office trowel beat at L89, or make it a visible pointing attempt with a reason, so the lane is the first clear demonstration; then write D2's reply to that rule.

### C2 · Minor · Token and bell continuity

- Where: L17–L21 (opening), L47 (to the market), L149 (fairy appears), L341 (sunset)
- Text:
> At every dawn bell the date scratched on the tin moved ahead one day.
> On the token, *today* slid neatly into *tomorrow*.
> The token was a thin square of tin nailed beside the attic ladder.
> carried my token there in my pocket
> Outside, the sunset bell began, and all across the kingdom tomorrow moved one day farther away.
- Problem: The token shows a scratched date at L17 but the words "today" and "tomorrow" at L21. It moves at the dawn bell (L17) but at the sunset bell in the close (L341). It is nailed up, carried in a pocket to the market, and back in place for the fairy to stand on at L149, with no prising off or rehanging.
- Fix: Pick one display (date or word) and one bell. Add a clause where she pries the token loose and later sets it back, or have her carry something else as proof.

### C3 · Minor · Shop and attic layout drifts

- Where: L11–L27 (opening), L137 (attic), L241 (roof gap), L305 (hatch), L339 (miller)
- Text:
> A second drop hit the cutting bench, close enough to the rolled hide that I felt the splash on my wrist.
> In the attic, the leak sounded bigger.
> The gap showed the attic below: basin rim, cutting bench, the pale roll of hide.
> He stood, stamped once, and walked the length of the shop. Above him, one drop fell into the basin.
- Problem: The cutting bench, hide and basin sit under the leak in the room the collector enters by the door (L27), yet the roof gap looks down into an attic holding the same bench and basin, the slater climbs to a hatch to see the underside (L305), and the basin ends up "above" the miller in the shop. The reader cannot picture one building, which blurs the D3 test and the patch.
- Fix: Choose one layout (for example a loft workroom above the shop, reached by the attic ladder) and fix the few phrases that contradict it.

### C4 · Minor · Object and limit slips

- Where: L119, L171, L193, L233, L261
- Text:
> The fairy's gaze went to the ladder rope and the bought slate.
> The miller's good leather lay under my hand.
> Halfway, the knot caught beneath a tile edge
> The slater would not climb.
> He passed me one slate at a time from the ladder, never taking the last rung.
- Problem: "Ladder rope" is the slater's coil (L71), not the tarred rope she bought; she bought slates, plural (L135–L137); the hide under her hand is her own (L25), not the miller's. The roof is slate, not tile. The slater "would not climb", yet works from partway up her ladder and climbs to the hatch (L305), against his one-climb knee (L75).
- Fix: "tarred rope", "slates", "my good leather", "slate edge"; narrow the slater's limit to not going onto the roof, or have him pass slates up from the ground.
