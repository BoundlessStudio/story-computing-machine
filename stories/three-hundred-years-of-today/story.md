# Three Hundred Years of Today

Bet is going to lose the pencil behind her ear. If I tell her, she’ll put her hand up to the wrong ear. If I tell her which ear, she’ll put both hands up, out of spite. I’ve had some good mornings with this pencil. Once I sent it the whole way down the cabin, from hand to hand, and a boy in the last row drew a picture of us with our legs sticking out of the bottom of the *Mayfly*. That was a particularly fine morning for the last row.

I keep my left hand on the stick and open my right. The gust comes up under the glider’s floor. The pencil lands lengthwise across my palm in a strip of morning sun, better than I could have asked for. There’s no one up here to applaud but Bet, who takes the pencil and gives me a look she’d have given it if it had flown all the way back to her by itself.

“You said you saw us land,” she says. She has her mouth near my ear because otherwise I won’t hear. The other side of her face, the one the passengers can see through the open cockpit door, is smiling pleasantly.

“Oh, yes. God, yes. The landing’s lovely.”

I want her looking for the landing field beyond the ridge. I might get us down the face before she sees my hands shaking and takes the stick. I put the pad of my thumb under the button on the stick. Bet taught me that on a morning when we had nearly an hour to spare. You can do an astonishing amount of shaking without moving the stick if you have your thumb there.

Our shadow comes out of the cloud onto the first of the Nine Tines. I could draw it with my eyes shut, right down to the pleat where the cliff breaks in two. In most of the versions where I get as far as seeing it, I’m overhead looking down. Today I push the nose over and get us around the white knuckle of rock low on the face. Looking ahead, I find a whole slab of daylight where I’ve never put a wing. Then the shadow starts to climb, faster than we’re going down. Somewhere off to my right the captain scuffs a heel against the floor. I can taste my own teeth.

“Kitt. I’m going to need you to level off.”

The warmth coming out of the vent against my knee goes away. The wing shudders and one long strip of red covering tears loose. It comes across the right window and goes up. For a few feet I can see it fly red side down along the face of the cliff. We’re falling right beside it. I have enough time to think that maybe we ought to have made the whole ship out of that stuff. Bet’s mouth is moving again when I turn to her.

“There it is,” I tell Bet, and the rock knocks the stick straight out of my hand.

* * *

The pencil rolls across my breakfast tray. It catches in the fold of the paper where I’ve put my thumb, and for a second I think my whole arm has gone on ringing from the blow. The noise is the boy in the row behind me kicking his feet against the seat. He does it when he wants to ask a question and has been told it’s too early in the day for questions.

“Could you pass that up to the captain, Miss Sayer?” says the man across the aisle.

We’re high enough at this hour to see the frost on both wings. The smell of wet coats has risen all the way to the roof where the warm air comes out. I have a tangerine to peel, and my shoulder aches where I slept on it. I turn sideways and put both feet down in the aisle. There’s a long yellow strip of paper that says NO BAGGAGE HERE in three languages. I could get down on the boards and put my ear to it. Several times I’ve put my face straight down on the floor in the morning and stayed there until somebody stepped over me. Today I can still see exactly where the cloth rose beside the rock. I want to put it down on paper before I lose the place.

I try the pencil on my thumbnail. “I should like to borrow it for a bit first,” I tell the man. “Are you particularly attached to the point?”

“Captain’s pencil,” he says. “She gives me one every flight and raises hell if I keep it. You’ll have to ask her what you’re allowed to do to the point.”

The boy stops kicking me. He wants to see me sharpen the pencil with my thumbnail, which is a cheap little thing I do at shows. I turn over my breakfast ticket and draw the cleft between the first two Tines. I make the white knuckle bigger than it really is, run the pencil along the right wall to put in the shelf, and mark where the cloth went up just beyond it, close against the rock. I leave the point there. Even the ragged end had gone up.

The boy comes around the seat to see. His orange is in his fist with the peel standing out in one piece. On one of the good mornings I taught him how to get it off without breaking it, and he has done just as well without me.

“Did you see that from up here?” he says.

In a great many mornings I’ve told him about all the things I can see from here. I’d like to hear him ask somebody else. I have Bet’s pencil, which means I also have about five minutes till she puts a hand out for it. I slide the ticket into my cuff and hand the boy the pencil.

“Do you want to come up and give this back to her with me? You can ask her what she can see.”

He walks up the aisle with his right shoulder to the wall, touching the backs of the seats all the way. Bet takes the pencil and lets him stand by the cockpit door. She shows him the flat black cup of the compass, and where he can look across the left wing and still see the lights of the field we’ve already passed.

“How long till there’s lights again?” he says.

“Watch for the dark line where the land falls away on the other side of Nine Tines,” Bet says. “Long before you see anybody’s house, you’ll see where the houses could go.”

The left seat has a cushion in fine red leather where I’m supposed to sit for the pictures. Bet’s cushion has a slit up the side with a ragged seam that she picks with her thumb. By the time the boy goes back she’s worked a little blue loop loose. She puts the pencil behind her ear.

“Your man wants his picture of the clairvoyant at the controls,” she says, nodding toward the first row. My photographer has the end of his tie out to polish his lens. “When the sun comes in, have him stand where the boy was. People will think we nearly tipped over if you let your elbows go loose and grin at the camera. Give me the aircraft back when I ask for her and we’ll get along fine.”

I lay the ticket on my knee and point to the narrow part between the Tines. “Would you give me the pencil again? I want to show you a way into this cleft. There’s a shelf I’ll need you to see from your side.”

She hands me the pencil with the tip toward herself, something she does with children and people she considers incompetent with a sharp thing. I spread the ticket on my leg. I need a fine point to get the line through the gap beside the shelf. I come to the end of a tune without remembering the start of it. “Dolly has the bedroom, we sleep on the stair.”

Bet’s hand goes down flat on my ticket. “Where did you hear that?”

“It’s the one with the apple tree. Everybody knows it.”

“The apple tree’s in the first verse. I made up the line about Dolly. I was six, and my mother had brought her pony into the house in a storm.”

I know the next line. The pony ate the ribbons they put out to dry. My mother had words of her own for that verse, about something round with a hole in it. I can feel her hands going under my ribs to lift me high enough to see what she was doing at the stove. The only words I can find are Bet’s, and I can hear Bet stumbling a little where she has to get the pony’s name in. I’ve surprised her by joining in before. Now she holds my ticket hard enough that the paper is starting to tear on the bend in my knee.

“If that was for the man with the camera, he can still have his picture of us sitting here. I don’t want you touching the stick.”

“He has nothing to do with it.”

“You walked up here to tell me where to fly, and you sang me a song I used to sing in the back room of a house that fell down before you were born. I can think of two or three people who might have heard it. Is that the kind of thing they pay you to do? Get one of them talking about the pony?”

I pull the ticket out from under her hand. Her palm stays flat on the fold of my skirt. I could still tell her the cover is going to rattle off the compass, then knock it loose with my knee. I once had almost everybody in the forward six rows calling me by my first name before we got to the bad air. I don’t suppose any of them had got to call for me in time.

“My super power isn’t actually clairvoyance. I don’t see the future, I live it. Whenever I die I can Groundhog Day and wake up the previous morning in a time loop.”

She takes her hand off my knee and puts it back on the stick with the other one. “Why are you telling me this now?”

“Because I’ve been stuck on today for three hundred years!” I’ve got the pencil out toward her, point turned in, as if getting it back in her hand will count as an apology. I put it down in the map holder. “I have something new I want you to see. I can get us into that cleft. If I tell you I’ve seen us come out, you might let me take us the whole way in and do everything I say until I get us killed again. I need you to know where I’ve been and how much of it I still have to guess.”

The air coming up from the cabin is cool now. The window on my side has a little patch in it that runs right down the join. It starts to whistle as the sun comes up over the cloud. I count the spaces between the screwheads beside it. I have spent the better part of a year doing just this, in a year when I knew no more at the end than I did at the beginning and had got rather sick of hearing my own name.

“The first time,” I say, “I woke up in the back and came up to do the trick in the good light. I was in the same skirt I’m in now. I went back and sat down when I’d done it. I was halfway through thinking of something gracious to say about your aircraft when we hit the ground. I woke up here with a pencil trying to go off the edge of the tray. I thought I was very lucky to get that much time to tell you to turn. The towplane had left us while I was asleep, but we could still see the field. You turned for me after you saw the cloud coming off the ridge, and we spent all the height we had trying to get back, with the air going down faster than we could fly out of it. The field lights went out one at a time behind a shoulder of rock while I was still looking at them. There were men on the radio who said they could come as far as the field. We needed them a good bit further on than that.”

Bet keeps her face forward. Her loose hand is on the edge of the compartment beside her seat. One finger works underneath it. I can see bare nail where the soft pink polish has been worked loose.

“For a while I tried to get you to tell me about every other place in the Nine Tines we could get through. I came up here and gave you more and more peculiar things to try. Sometimes you let me do them with you. Sometimes I went to the back instead. We used to talk for a while when it was still fairly calm and you could turn this way. Once I got you to tell me what kind of house would hold a pony. I asked you to sing me that song the way you used to sing it when the pony was alive. It took me a long time to try it without asking. By the time I was getting it right I think I’d stopped hearing my mother’s way of singing it. I’d open my mouth to try it on the first good breath I got all day, and there would be your pony on the landing.”

“It couldn’t get up the stairs,” she says. “I made different words for it every time.”

“You do. Some of the words you forget from the end of the song to the beginning of it. I used to try never to miss one.”

She drives the loose loop of blue thread into the cushion with the pencil point. She knows how to tuck it away with one neat push. I’ve never seen her do it before. She puts the pencil behind her ear.

“You’re sitting with me now, Sayer. What did you come up here to ask me?”

I set the ticket flat where we can both see it. She gives me the pencil. I put it on the lump I drew too big. “I’m going to go down while I’m still a long way out from the knuckle. I can be lined up for the cleft by the time we get around it.” I run the pencil in from the edge of the ticket, past the knuckle and into the cleft. I stop it beside the shelf. “I can see the way ahead. I want you to look down over the wing on your side and tell me when we’ve passed this.”

She puts her thumb on the shelf. “Have we been this close to it? Were you flying then, or was I?”

“I had the stick. Once I was looking straight past the end of the stone. The noise stopped in this window. I banked right, and the wing came down hard on the shelf that was still beside us. Last time I went down as far as I could while there was still a way around the knuckle. I only saw this much of the cleft.” I set the pencil point in the cross I’ve drawn. “A piece of our wing went straight up beside the rock while we were falling. There’s air rising there. I think it’ll take the weight of the aircraft if we can get into it high enough.”

“While you were looking up at that piece of wing,” she says, “what was I doing?”

“You were telling me to level off. I was so glad to see the cloth that I went on looking. When I turned to you I could see your mouth going again, right where I could hear you if I’d had a little more time, and everything went into the side of the hill.”

She shifts herself up in her seat until the belt lies flat. “Go slow with me. I have to see the whole shelf behind the right wing. Then I have to tell you to turn before we sink out of the cleft.” She takes the ticket and the pencil and looks out her window as far back along the wing as she can. “Can you keep her level long enough for me to see that much?”

“I can give you longer than I had the last time. I’d like something from you when the shelf passes. Just say clear. I can hear that through a good deal of noise.”

She puts the pencil behind her ear and looks out the front. From here the bad air starts with a dent in the cloud across the width of a field. There are strands of it falling off the ridge and through the bottom of the dent. She can see all the way down where they go.

“We’ve got room up here,” she says. “Before I give you the descent, tell me what I’ll feel in the air between here and the rock.”

“See the dent? The left side will fall when we go in. Then you’ll get a kick up under that same wing, quite a sharp one. I’ve flown that bit more often than I’ve done the stunt.” When I do the stunt in good light, I raise one hand to an audience who usually have no idea whether I am greeting them or asking divine assistance. Today I keep both hands in my lap.

The glider goes slack on my side first. There’s a hiccup in the floor where the weight drops away. Bet’s fingers close on the stick. She looks out at the left wing and back at the space between her knees. “Take her, Kitt.” I get my hand on my stick. Bet lets go, and the kick comes up so hard that her fingertips strike the underside of the dash. People usually shout here. I let the nose go down the little bit it wants and the wings come level in time for the boy behind me to ask at full volume if that was the trick. The wood and cloth go on making their big airy racket, and otherwise the boy has the cabin to himself. Bet comes forward far enough that I can feel her sleeve on my arm. The left window is whistling hard now.

“I’d like him buckled in when we get down beside the rock,” she says. “Him and everybody else. I’m going to put the word down the aisle while you take us into the cleft.” She has one hand on her own belt. “Tell me before you start down. I want to feel it coming.”

* * *

The steward has to kneel down to help with the boy’s belt. A bottle knocks against a seat leg at the back and rolls until somebody stops it. I have the white knuckle out in front of me with room enough to go down and get pointed at the opening. The last time I started down, it was already almost under my feet. I wait until the steward is buckled in at the front. He tucks his hands between his knees, in the way I used to when I rode out the descent in the back.

“We’re going down now.”

Bet puts her boots flat on the floor. The nose comes down farther than seems a decent thing to do with people sitting upright, and as the glider gathers speed my skin goes tight on the bones of my face. The rough air has shaken grit out of the seam around the window. I get some between my teeth. All the seats creak at once behind me. From the back you feel the belt ride up until the buckle’s under your ribs. I could never pull mine tight enough.

The white knuckle slides up the windshield and the cleft opens beneath it. I have the nose pointed straight down the gap and take us in well above the place the cloth went up. There are little bushes on the right face of the rock, moving in the wind and showing white underneath. I can hear the window over on my side and the wind outside doing different things at once. Ahead there’s sky and below it the bottom of the gorge. Slabs have broken off the walls and come to rest on edge against one another.

The whistle in the window stops.

It would be a kindness to everyone to make the turn now. If I can get the nose around into the air that took the cloth, there might still be a cup of tea, the photograph, all the small foolish things I expected to get out of the flight. My hand is already tight on the stick. When the wing came down on the shelf it made one wooden sound through all the wood in the ship, like a drawer shoved hard into a dresser. Something up by the door had gone on rattling after it. That was about as much time as there was to understand what was happening in the rest of the aircraft.

I put my thumb where Bet first showed me and keep my hand in the middle of my body. Our nose is out of the cleft. The tops of the slabs rise in the windshield. Beside the right wing, where I can’t see it, there’s a piece of stone I can still feel in my hand. Bet has her face low to the window with one hand spread against the glass so she can see past the reflection. I start to say please. Bet leans farther forward. I have time to see that three of her fingers are bending individually with the effort of holding her forehead off the glass. Her breath makes a little patch of white between them.

“Clear,” she says. “Come right.”

I push the stick over and feel my weight move. For a fraction of a second there’s nothing in the air to take it. I keep coming right until the windshield fills with the stretch of rock where the cloth went up. I’m close enough to see each of the bushes going under in a row. One turns so far over I can see where its roots have split the rock. As we come across it I feel the seat press up under the backs of both my legs. There’s a long ripple through the wing and then there’s so much less noise that I can hear the pencil drop into Bet’s lap. The cliff moves down. It keeps moving. I learn that there’s grass all the way across the top of the first Tine. There’s a gray bird up there having some difficulty with a length of white grass, and I see it trail the grass out behind it all the way into the air beside us.

By the time I think to look at Bet, she’s upright again with both feet on the floor and her hand still flat on her knee. She takes the pencil and taps out an uneven little roll on the edge of the map holder. I put the end of it together with the beginning and recognize the tune.

“Where are we going to put it?” I have to take a breath in the middle of asking. I would like to fly the glider on and on. We are high enough to see the next ridge coming apart into its different hills and a great shining distance to the east. I could go on looking for as long as the air would have me.

“I’ll come down with you,” says Bet. “We can go down over the low fields and see where they’ve put the lights.”

We turn and see the whole green top of the Nine Tines shining brown along one side in the low light, from the first cleft to the long smooth end where the ground goes out beneath them like a coat spread out and left to dry. The landing is a long wet slap that brings everything in the cabin that’s been hanging on somewhere under a seat into the aisle. For a startling length of time we keep going after the wheels touch. I catch first one, then the other of Bet’s little breaths through her nose. When we stop she lifts her hand off her knee. “Open the door for me, Kitt. I want a line on the grass where we can see every last one of them.”

In the night I wake with my hand going out toward a tray that isn’t there. They’ve given me a room over the field office with a low bed and a window I can push open with my knee. Bet is sleeping on the other side of a wall thin enough for me to hear her turn. After a while I get up and pull my coat over my nightdress. Nobody’s put the landing lights on yet. The first color from the east is touching the high rock on the far face of the ridge. In a little while the first Tine will throw a shadow. I can’t tell where it’s going to go from here. I get as close to the glass as I can and wait to see it move.
