# At the Back of the Picture

The royal family had won the war at noon and by three the little girl had had enough of it. She was twisting out of her mother's hand. Ireta was working on the hem of the girl's stocking, where a patch of silvering took away the seam and put it back as the lamp swung over. The stocking had been mended at the heel. They must have dressed the princess in a hurry. The king still wore his field boots, with bits of fern stuck to the wet laces, although it hadn't rained in Tarse for six weeks.

Someone in the stairwell set down a tray with enough force to make the cups ring. The sound seemed to come from under Ireta's feet, which was where the bottom flight of the public stair went. It climbed from the street, turned below the old ticket room, and came out among the gallery seats above her. From her worktable she could see a slant of their varnished underside. She put her wrist against the edge until the vibration passed. Across the doorway a fan with a bent blade knocked once each time it went round, stirring the sweet smell out of the wool hangings somebody had taken out of a chest that morning.

She'd had five or six goes at the battle photograph, all from the same copy in the state history, until the steward had managed to borrow the original for tonight's projection. In the book the king looked confidently out at the class. When the silver moved off his face Ireta saw that his eyes were shut. She was glad for him. Imagine having to look pleased for another hundred years.

She moved the lamp down toward the torn corner. Behind the king's sister there was a line too fine for the fibers of the paper. It came down from a woman's bowed head, slid across her mouth, and ended where the light caught the top of her shoulder. Once Ireta picked that hair out of the paper she could see how much of it there was, lying loose down the front of a white breastplate. It had the same metallic shine as the field of damaged silver, but she could turn the photograph all the way around and the hair kept falling from the woman's head.

Ireta looked back at the little girl. It was the woman she'd been twisting to see. One of the woman's shoulders hung lower than the other and her white glove disappeared into the shadow of a pillar. Behind it a pale stripe followed the wall right into an open doorway. A cut in the stone, shaped a little like a letter, caught the light beside it. In the first crop Ireta had taken it for a scratch on the print. She left it alone this time. There was a chip out of the plate just above the woman's knuckles, a dark crescent with a pinprick of light inside the curve. Ireta went back to that mark three times before she was satisfied that somebody had actually photographed it.

The hall steward knocked the way people knocked when they already had the door open. “I can give you the full cloth for the king. He's had in the neighborhood of fifty years to go out of fashion, but people ask for him. It's the feet that worry me. You've got him cut off at the ankle in the one you sent up yesterday.”

“His feet are the best thing about him.”

The steward had a pencil in his mouth and two more tucked in the loop on his tie. He propped the door with his heel while he tried to figure out what she meant. Downstairs the seats for the anniversary count had been set out on the flagstones, but several people had already gone up to the gallery to look for better ones. A woman with a reed-colored cane had put her gloves over two places in the front row. A girl who called her Grandma, wearing an apron over her coat, had carried up a cup for her and was holding it by both rims. The brewer's logo ran right across her stomach. Ireta saw her cradle the cup while the older woman turned her very firmly by the elbow to show her where to look.

She brought up the version he'd already approved and kept her thumb over the left edge until he bent to check it. When he left she took the thumb away. On that copy the door was a black notch at the very edge. The princess looked up into the frame.

Ireta opened the scans of the coronation paintings. In the first the king had his full head of hair and his sister was in white silk, with foxes embroidered all the way up the fronts of her shoes. The painter had taken up a good third of the canvas with people looking down from the east gallery. Ireta followed the light down the painted faces to the dais and found the woman in the white hood behind the new king. A fine black crack ran from the crescent scar to the first joint of the forefinger.

By the next coronation the girl in the stocking was queen. Someone had to get on a stool and lay the crown on her with both hands. Ireta had known the painting since she came to work in Tarse, but this was the first time she'd thought to look for the stool. You had to follow a white-haired bishop's gaze down the back of the queen's dress to find its little feet. The men had made a business of finding somewhere to look. The woman in white had eyes only for the queen. One corner of her mouth was turning up. The new queen, with the man on the stool leaning over her, gathered the cloth of her sleeve under her thumb. Ireta knew what you could do to a sleeve with a thumb if there was nowhere you were allowed to go.

At the base of the armored forefinger the crack had opened wide enough for a single stroke of red. For five minutes Ireta forgot about the screen. She tried the plausible things first. The second painter could have borrowed the figure from the earlier picture. She'd seen a child that size in another royal work and traced her all the way back to a calendar of the saints. But the two men had seen the woman from different angles. Where the first had painted the gauntlet head on, the second showed the fingers curled right around a piece of iron at the pillar. He had tried to carry the dark color of the background over it. It showed through where the paint had thinned.

Ireta had a folder of pictures of the last queen in which nobody had felt obliged to make her look dignified. They had drawn them from the gallery, so if the woman's hand was in any of them she could see what it was doing.

* * *

The execution sheets had been folded inside an account book. The man who sold the book to the district had reckoned Ireta would like the drawings because every one of the people in them was dead. There were six dark figures below the pale oval of the gallery. In the finished print from the same series each wore a face the court artist would have recognized. On the sheets the queen's brother looked like anybody taking his coat off in a hurry, and the woman waiting behind him was the sister whose shoes had been covered with foxes. Ireta knew how hard it was to draw a hand that was trying to untangle wet cloth. The little hard loops of graphite on the prince's cuff took her longer to get past than the black line the artist put under his name to mean he was dead.

One leaf had the queen on it four times. In the largest sketch she had turned her back on the gallery to take hold of the hand of the woman in armor. She had caught it by two of the fingers, the way people do when they hope to go unobserved. The woman had given it to her palm up. In the little sketch below, the queen walked out of reach. The hand stayed where it was, right out in the space between them with the fingers just beginning to fold. The rest of the woman was so still that the artist had drawn her once for both pictures. A strip of cloth came off the back of her hood and hung down against the wood. Ireta went back to see where the woman had let go. She could find the place where the queen's fingers left.

Only when Ireta enlarged the place where the drawings overlapped could she see what the other hand was doing. The painter's little iron loop was nearly the size of a person's head. It came through a slot in the pillar, and the woman's fingers hooked through it from below. The first finger, which had the split near the knuckle, had lost a plate there. The sketch left the shoulder a blank patch of paper but the artist had marked the opening in the glove all the way round in graphite.

Across the partition, the steward had started over from the beginning of the sound check. The first count, fifty-three years ago, was believed to have run from two in the afternoon until the following morning. Tonight everybody was supposed to get home in time for supper. When the steward had asked her for a picture of the jubilant public on the day of the count, Ireta had given him a still from the security camera above the gallery. There was a man with his arm up and his mouth open, which had pleased the steward greatly. In the full frame the man's other hand was locked around the rail. She could have saved that hand when she cropped it. She went back for the moving version.

The man got one hand on the rail as he stood up with the others to see what the tellers were doing. Below him, the camera's pale green shadows made the walls look damp. The familiar red in the broken gauntlet looked as if somebody had put it in with a pen. Then the woman raised her face. For as long as the woman looked up Ireta couldn't remember how old the picture was. It was the same face she'd turned the lamp to find, as clear under the broken edge of the hood as if the two of them were standing together in the dark.

There was a sound like a tray of cups dropped down a stairwell. The iron bar slid out of the pillar and the wooden steps of the gallery dipped away from the stone dais. The woman caught the eye at the end of the bar. When she stopped it, the steps stopped. The man at the rail had one hand up and his mouth open. He found his feet again and hurried down on unsteady knees. The woman beckoned him to the dais with her right hand. She turned that palm up. He stepped over before he had to take it.

On the radio a voice said there was an unauthorized woman on the floor. A second voice wanted to know which floor. The public shouted over the answer. They had finished counting votes at one of the tables and were calling up a number to the gallery. The woman drew her hair out of the hinge of her elbow with her free hand and settled against the weight. She had been looking up all that time without once looking at the men at the tables.

She put the full still in the folder with the new scan of the king. There was no telling how many people would recognize him with his eyes shut. Ireta was still in that folder when she heard the steward's voice come through a speaker under her hand.

Somebody had switched the sound check to the live gallery feed. In the lower corner of the picture she could see the older woman's cane among the wooden benches and the white hood beyond it, down by the pillar. The red between the plates looked like a line of numbers seen through a piece of torn paper. As Ireta got up the woman shifted against the bar and the first finger of the armored glove fell past her hip and struck the dais. For the length of the noise it made, Ireta stood still.

* * *

The draft at the top of the public stair was cold enough to dry the sweat on Ireta's back. She came out into the gallery, where two people with cups were taking their time deciding whether to make room for her. The woman was on the dais beside the pillar, close enough to see between their shoulders. Ireta went down the wooden steps toward her and landed hard on stone.

The steward was there with two spare pencils tucked into his fist. He looked past her as if he was waiting for the person with the authority to make her run like that.

“Go keep people off the public stair,” Ireta said. “Then get everybody up here down onto the stone.”

“Down here for what?”

A bench scraped as somebody dragged it nearer the rail. The last wooden step dropped away from Ireta's heel. The woman had the eye of the bar in her left hand, close against her waist, and at her feet there was a piece of the iron that had been holding it up. “Don't send them back under me, love,” she said. “It's coming down empty just the same.”

The steward set both pencils on the floor and went back up the steps two at a time. From the gallery Ireta could hear him asking the people on the public stair to turn and make their way back down, passing the word all the way to the street. “It happens every year,” he called after them, though this was the first year anybody had persuaded him to open the gallery.

Ireta had the childish thought that there ought to have been men in the hall big enough to stand beside the woman and get her arm down by taking a little of the weight each. The woman looked straight at Ireta from so deep in her torn hood that for a second Ireta thought she was going to pull it up and show her what she looked like. Then she felt her own shoulders come down. The woman's eyes had been moving over her the way you look over a child coming in from the cold, to see how much there is to do. A strand of her hair was caught under the bar. When the iron slipped a finger's width she tipped her head to give it slack. Behind her, just clear of the hanging, the pale stripe on the wall made a turn.

Ireta pulled the tablet from her coat and opened the photograph to its full width. The turn in the stripe led to a cut in the stone, and there the door stood open. She could see the bottom half of the cut where the hanging sagged. She caught up the wool and flung it aside. There was a leather handle behind it, cold and shiny, and when she braced a foot against the wall and hauled on it the smell of malt came in so strongly that she knew where she was before she saw the brewery stair. There were people going up and down it, pale hands sliding on the black rail. She put her shoulder against the door before the spring could take it back.

The steward tried the bottom step with one foot. “Come my way, friends. Take your cups with you. The brewery will find you a place to put them.” A man got his son down onto the dais and people came after them. Ireta leaned round the door to ask the man to keep hold of his boy. Beyond him it was getting dark so fast she had to keep turning back to the lit hall to see who was coming.

By the time the grandmother with the cane reached the last wooden step, the gap between it and the dais was as wide as her shoe. Ireta had watched somebody's knees go under him in an instant on the old recording. Here it took time. The old woman put down her cane and the little rubber foot shuffled along by itself until it met the edge of the stone. She bent from the hip and put the cane down again. She had both feet under her and all of her attention on the strip of air at the bottom of the steps.

The girl came down the last two steps fast and stopped with her hands flat against her apron. She had a long loose tie trailing on the wood. The woman in armor said, “Take the bottom of your apron in both hands and lift it up. Look at where you've put your feet. You've got boots on, they'll go farther than that.” The girl looked down and in that time her grandmother put a hand behind her and pushed with the tips of her fingers. She stepped far enough that she ran up against the woman's knee on the stone. The woman rested the side of her bare cheek for a moment against the top of the girl's head. “Keep on. Right round the door. Watch where the stair goes left.”

“I used to come up here from the brewery,” the grandmother said. Her mouth worked for a while. She was watching her granddaughter and spoke once the girl had turned to hear. “My father had me by the strap of my dress, the whole length of the stairs. The crowd was trying to go two ways on the landing. He could never wait till the end of anything. He put me up on the rail there to see the queen and before she'd even gone he had me under his arm and I was looking at the back of his knee again. I thought I could show her just where I'd been.”

She raised the cane a third time in the direction of the benches. Ireta could see her foot turn as though she meant to go back and put her granddaughter where she'd been. Behind the old woman the benches were beginning to shift downhill by themselves. A cup went over the rail. Ireta knew from how long it took to break that it had gone all the way down to the public stair.

The woman lowered her head and got one shoulder under the bar. Ireta heard the iron come down on the shoulder plate. The woman straightened her knees and the step lifted, just a little, along the edge of the stone. A light at her collar went out. There had been another at her wrist, a deep steady red, which Ireta was still looking at when it began to blink. Slowly the ordinary bow of her body showed through her armor. With her free hand, the one the queen had reached for, she brushed the hair out of her face and held it open toward the grandmother.

“Oh, it was you up on the rail,” the woman said. “I could see your shoes from down here. One of your ribbons was untied and your father kept trying to get at it. Put the stick down, love. I've got you.”

The old woman looked right at her. Ireta had never seen the girl in the gallery who could have looked out of those old eyes. She had the head of the cane in both hands now. Her grip loosened as she looked, until one hand slipped off and the cane fell to the wood. The woman put her good thumb under the grandmother's fingers and guided them in against her palm, with a patience that took longer than the step. For an instant Ireta thought the two of them were only going to stand there, greeting each other. Then the old woman drew the fingers closed, stepped out over the strip of air, and put her foot down on stone. Ireta heard it take her weight.

The girl had come back as far as the door to lean around Ireta. She held out the apron she'd gathered in both hands, because it was what she had, and her grandmother held on to it as they went down the brewery stairs. As their steps faded Ireta heard the little knock of the fan down in the ticket room. The empty gallery held. The woman was standing where she had always stood, her hair coming down over one side of her face. Ireta had the door heavy against her open hand and didn't use the other hand to wave.

The woman lifted her chin to look out past her. Then there was the small sound of a buckle. The woman slid her arm backwards out of the stiff sleeve of the gauntlet and drew it close against her chest, at the steady pace of someone pulling her arm free of a sleeping child's grasp. She had to bend her knees to get far enough under the iron to turn, and Ireta could hear the fit of the plates change as she did it. The gauntlet stayed closed around the eye. The woman cleared the pillar before the end of the bar struck the stone where she'd been standing.

The projection screen fell sideways with the gallery. Ireta held the door through a gust of stale wool and dust, and somebody on the brewery stair reached through and held it alongside her. Outside it was dusk and the red joints showed under the torn hood where the white parts of the armor were getting harder to see. Up under a lamp across the stair, the housing of the republic's other camera had a little eye of its own. It had the woman in view as she came out and lifted her good hand to tuck her hair under the hood. The other arm fell bare to the wrist from the ragged sleeve of her cape. On the stone just below them the girl had stopped to wait. When the woman drew level the grandmother reached out a bare hand again, and they went down slowly together, broadside to the camera the whole way.
