# At the Back of the Picture

The royal family had won the war at noon and by three the little girl had had enough of it. She was twisting out of her mother's hand. Ireta was working on the hem of the girl's stocking, where a patch of silvering took away the seam and put it back as the lamp swung over. The stocking had been mended at the heel. They must have dressed the princess in a hurry. The king still wore his field boots, with bits of fern stuck to the wet laces, although it hadn't rained in Tarse for six weeks.

Someone in the stairwell set down a tray with enough force to make the cups ring. The bottom flight of the public stair passed right under Ireta's feet on its way up from the street to the gallery. She felt the rattle through her worktable and put her wrist against the edge until it passed. Across the doorway a fan with a bent blade knocked once each time it went round, drawing the smell of cedar out of the wool hangings somebody had pulled from a chest that morning.

She'd had five or six goes at the battle photograph, all from the same copy in the state history, until the steward had managed to borrow the original for tonight's projection. In the book the king looked confidently out at the class. When the silver moved off his face Ireta saw that his eyes were shut. She was glad for him. Imagine having to look pleased for another hundred years.

She moved the lamp down toward the torn corner. Behind the king's sister there was a line too fine for the fibers of the paper. It came down from a woman's bowed head, slid across her mouth, and ended where the light caught the top of her shoulder. Once Ireta picked that hair out of the paper she could see how much of it there was, lying loose down the front of a white breastplate. Ireta lowered the lamp until the silver haze slid off the page. The threads across the woman's breastplate stayed pale, and where they parted she could see the near corner of her mouth, dipped a little as if she was thinking.

Ireta looked back at the little girl. It was the woman she'd been twisting to see. One of the woman's shoulders hung lower than the other and her white glove disappeared into the shadow of a pillar. Behind it a pale stripe followed the wall right into an open doorway. In the stone beside it a cut shaped like a Y caught the light. In the first crop Ireta had taken it for a scratch on the print. She left it alone this time. There was a chip out of the plate just above the woman's knuckles, a dark crescent with a pinprick of light inside the curve. Ireta went back to that mark three times before she was satisfied that somebody had actually photographed it.

The hall steward knocked the way people knocked when they already had the door open. “I can give you the full cloth for the king. The republic's had in the neighborhood of fifty years to put him out of fashion, but people ask for him. You cut him off at the ankle in the one you sent up yesterday. Are you going to put the field boots back in?”

“His feet are the best thing about him.”

The steward had a pencil in his mouth and two more tucked in the loop on his tie. He propped the door with his heel. Beyond him Ireta could see the seats on the flagstones for the anniversary count and, across the hall in the gallery, an elderly woman with a reed-colored cane and two places marked out with her gloves. The woman pointed over the rail at the dais. Ireta got up to see where her finger led and tucked her dark braid into the collar of her work coat. A girl in a Thistle Brewery apron came down the row with a cup held at the very top between her fingertips. “Hot, Grandma.” The old woman laid her cane along the bench. She gave the girl the space beside her and turned her firmly by the elbow to show her where to look.

“Mind, I don't expect the whole war. People have come to see the count. If you put something up with a great deal of black in it they'll think I've let the lights go again.” While he was still considering the lights, Ireta opened the file he'd already approved and tucked the original print under its cover. She wanted the woman to herself a little longer. When Ireta went back to the old crop, the door was a black notch at the edge. The princess looked up past the edge of the frame.

Ireta opened the scans of the coronation paintings. In the first the king had his full head of hair and his sister was in white silk, with foxes embroidered all the way up the fronts of her shoes. The painter had taken up a good third of the canvas with people looking down from the east gallery. Ireta followed the light down the painted faces to the dais and found the woman in the white hood behind the new king. A fine black crack ran from the crescent scar to the first joint of the forefinger.

By the next coronation the girl in the stocking was queen. She sat so high on the dais that the bishop had to get on a stool to lay the crown on her with both hands. Ireta had known the painting since she came to work in Tarse, but this was the first time she'd thought to look for the stool. You had to follow an attendant's gaze down the back of the queen's dress to find its little feet. The men had made a business of finding somewhere to look. The woman in white had eyes only for the queen. One corner of her mouth was turning up. The new queen, with the man on the stool leaning over her, gathered the cloth of her sleeve under her thumb. Ireta knew what you could do to a sleeve with a thumb if there was nowhere you were allowed to go.

At the base of the armored forefinger the crack had opened wide enough for a single stroke of red. Ireta pulled the door to. The second painter could have borrowed the figure from the earlier picture. She'd seen a child that size in another royal work and traced her all the way back to a calendar of the saints. But the two men had seen the woman from different angles. Where the first had painted the gauntlet head on, the second showed the fingers curled right around a piece of iron at the pillar. He had tried to carry the dark color of the background over it. It showed through where the paint had thinned.

Ireta opened a folder of pictures of the last queen in which nobody had felt obliged to make her look dignified.

* * *

The light had thinned across the ticket room. Out in the hall the steward had started over from the beginning of the sound check. She enlarged the first of the execution sheets until the folds where it had been tucked into an account book ran right through the people. The man who sold the book to the district had reckoned Ireta would like the drawings because every one of the people in them was dead. There were six dark figures below the pale oval of the gallery. In the finished print from the same series each wore a face the court artist would have recognized. On the sheets the queen's brother looked like anybody taking his coat off in a hurry, and she recognized his aunt waiting behind him by the foxes on her shoes. Ireta knew how hard it was to draw a hand that was trying to untangle wet cloth. The little hard loops of graphite on the prince's cuff took her longer to get past than the black line the artist put under his name to mean he was dead.

One leaf had the queen on it four times. In the largest sketch she had turned her back on the gallery. The woman in armor had given her an open hand, and the queen had caught it by two of the fingers, the way people do when they hope to go unobserved. In the little sketch below, the queen walked out of reach. The hand stayed where it was, right out in the space between them with the fingers just beginning to fold. The rest of the woman was so still that the artist had drawn her once for both pictures. A strip of cloth came off the back of her hood and hung down against the wood. Ireta went back to see who had let go. The woman's fingers had kept their shape as the queen's left them.

Only when Ireta enlarged the place where the drawings overlapped could she see what the other hand was doing. The painter's little iron loop was nearly the size of a person's head. It came through a slot in the pillar, and the woman's fingers hooked through it from below. The forefinger had lost its knuckle plate. The sketch left the shoulder a blank patch of paper but the artist had marked the opening in the glove all the way round in graphite.

The first count, fifty-three years ago, was believed to have run from two in the afternoon until the following morning. Tonight everybody was supposed to get home in time for supper. When the steward had asked her for a picture of the jubilant public on the day of the count, Ireta had given him a still from the security camera above the gallery. There was a man with his arm up and his mouth open, which had pleased the steward greatly. In the full frame the man's other hand was locked around the rail. She could have saved that hand when she cropped it. She went back for the moving version.

The man got one hand on the rail as he stood up with the others to see what the tellers were doing. Below him, the camera's pale green shadows made the walls look damp. Along the gauntlet the seams gave off a red so definite that somebody might have put it in with a pen. Then the woman raised her head. Her silver hair had caught on the broken edge of the hood and left her mouth clear. The near corner dipped, just as it had in the photograph, and Ireta couldn't remember how old the picture was. She put her hand across the date in the margin of the original print. The knuckles had grown so thick in twenty years at a table that she couldn't lay her fingers flat any longer. The woman on the screen lowered her head and let the silver hair slide down between them.

There was a sound like a tray of cups dropped down a stairwell. Beside the steps a thick beam ran under the end of the gallery. Its tip rested on the iron bar that crossed to the pillar. The beam dipped and pushed the iron out of the bracket in the stone. At the rail the man's knees went under him, his free hand rising as his mouth opened. The woman caught the iron by its eye and lifted it until the wooden steps came level with the dais again. The man found his feet and hurried down the steps to the woman, who had her right hand out to him, palm up.

He stepped over before he had to take it.

On the radio a voice said there was an unauthorized woman on the floor. A second voice wanted to know which floor. The public shouted over the answer. The tellers had finished counting votes at one of the tables and were calling up a number to the gallery. The woman drew her hair out of the hinge of her elbow with her free hand and settled against the weight. She had been looking up all that time without once looking at the men at the tables.

Ireta put the full still in the folder with the new scan of the king. There was no telling how many people would recognize him with his eyes shut. Ireta was still in that folder when she heard the steward's voice come through a speaker under her hand.

Somebody had switched the sound check to the live gallery feed. In the lower corner of the picture she could see the older woman's cane among the wooden benches and the white hood beyond it, down by the pillar. At her wrist and collar the red seams were bright enough to bleed into the green. As Ireta got up the woman shifted against the bar and the first finger of the armored glove fell past her hip. It struck the dais with the bright knock of a coin. Ireta ran.

* * *

The draft at the top of the public stair was cold enough to dry the sweat on Ireta's back. She came out into the gallery, where two people with cups were taking their time deciding whether to make room for her. The woman was on the dais beside the pillar, close enough to see between their shoulders. Ireta went down the wooden steps toward her and landed hard on stone.

The steward was there with two spare pencils tucked into his fist. He had his back to the woman. It was Ireta running out onto the dais that made him look around.

“Bring the people on the gallery down onto the stone,” Ireta said.

“We can get them out by the front. Let me have a word with the people on the stair to make some room below.”

A bench scraped as somebody dragged it nearer the rail. The last wooden step dropped away from Ireta's heel. The bracket had torn out of the pillar. It lay at the woman's feet, still in one piece, with a gray collar of mortar around its base. The woman had the eye of the bar in her left hand, close against her waist. “Don't send them back under me, love,” she said. “It's coming down empty just the same.”

The steward set both pencils on the floor and went back up the steps two at a time. Ireta heard him ask the people coming up the public stair to turn and make their way back down, passing the word all the way to the street. “It happens every year,” he called after them, though this was the first year anybody had persuaded him to open the gallery. The people on the stair turned at once. From underneath the benches their feet sounded like a sudden hard rain.

Ireta had the childish thought that there ought to have been men in the hall big enough to stand beside the woman and get her arm down by taking a little of the weight each. The woman turned her head and looked Ireta over the way you look over a child coming in from the cold, to see how much there is to do. Ireta felt her own shoulders come down. A strand of the woman's hair was caught under the bar. When the iron slipped a finger's width she tipped her head to give it slack. Behind her, just clear of the hanging, the pale stripe on the wall made a turn.

Ireta pulled the tablet from her coat and opened the photograph to its full width. On the screen the stripe ran past the Y and into the open doorway. Here the foot of the Y showed just below the hanging. She caught up the wool and flung it aside. There was a leather handle behind it, cold and shiny, and when she braced a foot against the wall and hauled on it the smell of malt came in so strongly that she knew where she was before she saw the brewery stair. There were people going up and down it, pale hands sliding on the black rail. She put her shoulder against the door before the spring could take it back.

The steward came back along the gallery and stopped at the bottom step, facing the open door. He tried the stone with one foot. “Come my way, friends. Take your cups with you. The brewery will find you a place to put them.” A man got his son down onto the dais and people came after them. Ireta leaned round the door to ask the man to keep hold of his boy. Beyond him it was getting dark so fast she had to keep turning back to the lit hall to see who was coming.

By the time the grandmother with the cane reached the last wooden step, there was enough space between it and the dais to lose a foot. Ireta had watched somebody's knees go under him in an instant on the old recording. Here it took time. The old woman put down her cane and the little rubber foot shuffled along by itself until it met the edge of the stone. She bent from the hip and put the cane down again. She had both feet under her and all of her attention on the strip of air at the bottom of the steps.

The girl came down the last two steps fast and stopped with her hands flat against her apron. She had a long loose tie trailing on the wood. The woman in armor said, “Take the bottom of your apron in both hands and lift it up. Look at where you've put your feet. You've got boots on, they'll go farther than that.” The girl looked down and in that time her grandmother put a hand behind her and pushed with the tips of her fingers. The girl stepped so far she came up against the woman's knee and bowed right into her. The woman tipped her head until her cheek met the girl's hair, her left arm still drawn tight to the iron. “Keep on. Right round the door. Watch where the stair goes left.”

The grandmother waited for the girl to turn. “I came up here from the brewery the day they executed the queen. My father had me by the strap of my dress, the whole length of the stairs. The crowd was trying to go two ways on the landing. He could never wait till the end of anything. He put me up on the rail there to see the queen and before she'd even gone he had me under his arm and I was looking at the back of his knee again. I wanted this one to see just where I'd been.”

She raised the cane a third time in the direction of the benches. Ireta could see her foot turn as though she meant to go back and put her granddaughter where she'd been. Behind the old woman the benches were beginning to shift downhill by themselves. A cup went over the rail. Ireta knew from how long it took to break that it had gone all the way down to the public stair.

The woman lowered her head and got one shoulder under the bar. Ireta heard the iron come down on the shoulder plate. The woman straightened her knees and the step lifted, just a little, along the edge of the stone. A light at her collar went out. The light at her wrist blinked. Her chin sank to the breastplate. With the hand the queen had reached for she brushed the hair off her face, then held it out to the grandmother, palm up.

“Oh, it was you up on the rail.” One corner of the woman's mouth went up. “I could see your shoes from down here. One of your ribbons was untied and your father kept trying to get at it. Leave the stick, love. It's your foot I want.”

The grandmother looked straight at her, with the head of the cane in both hands. Her grip loosened as she looked, until one hand slipped off and the cane fell to the wood. The woman put her good thumb under the grandmother's fingers and guided them in against her palm, with a patience that took longer than the step. Ireta thought the two of them were only going to stand there, greeting each other. Then the old woman drew the fingers closed, stepped out over the strip of air, and put her foot down on stone. Ireta heard it take her weight.

The girl had come back as far as the door to lean around Ireta. She held out the apron she'd gathered in both hands, because it was what she had, and her grandmother held on to it as they went down the brewery stairs. As their steps faded Ireta heard the little knock of the fan down in the ticket room. The empty gallery held. The woman was standing where she had always stood, her hair coming down over one side of her face. Ireta had the door heavy against her open hand and didn't use the other hand to wave.

The woman lifted her chin to look out past her. Then there was the small sound of a buckle. With the bar on her shoulder she slid her arm backwards out of the stiff sleeve of the gauntlet and drew it close against her chest, at the steady pace of someone pulling her arm free of a sleeping child's grasp. The gauntlet stayed closed around the eye.

The woman took one step along the pillar toward the door. The bar slid off her shoulder and struck the stone where she'd been standing.

The gallery fell. At first there was the sound of the benches going over each other, then the long crack of wood from under them. The projection screen came down on its side. Ireta held the door through a gust of stale wool and dust, and somebody on the brewery stair reached through and held it alongside her. Outside it was dusk and the red joints showed under the torn hood where the white parts of the armor were getting harder to see. Up under a lamp across the stair, the housing of the republic's other camera had a little eye of its own. It had the woman in view as she came out and lifted her good hand to tuck her hair under the hood. The torn white cloak left her other wrist bare. A red impression circled her wrist where the gauntlet had ended. On the stone just below them the girl had stopped to wait. When the woman drew level the grandmother reached out a bare hand again, and they went down slowly together, broadside to the camera the whole way.
