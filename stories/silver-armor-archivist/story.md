# At the Back of the Picture

The royal family had won the war at noon and by three the little girl had had enough of it. She was twisting out of her mother's hand. Ireta was working on the hem of the girl's stocking, where a patch of silvering took away the seam and put it back as the lamp swung over. The stocking had been mended at the heel. They must have dressed the princess in a hurry. The king still wore his field boots, with bits of fern stuck to the wet laces, although it hadn't rained in Tarse for six weeks.

Someone in the stairwell put down a tray of cups. From inside the former ticket room it sounded as though they'd dropped them down the stairs. Ireta's worktable stood where, in the photograph, people would have queued to give up their good coins for a seat in the east gallery. She put her wrist against the edge until the vibration passed. In the doorway a fan with a bent blade knocked once each time it went round, stirring the sweet smell out of the wool hangings somebody had taken out of a chest that morning.

She'd had five or six goes at the battle photograph, all from the same copy in the state history, until the steward had managed to borrow the original for tonight's projection. In the book the king looked confidently out at the class. When the silver moved off his face Ireta saw that his eyes were shut. She was glad for him. Imagine having to look pleased for another hundred years.

She moved the lamp down toward the torn corner. Behind the king's sister there was a line too fine for the fibers of the paper. It came down from a woman's bowed head, slid across her mouth, and ended where the light caught the top of her shoulder. Once Ireta picked that hair out of the paper she could see how much of it there was, lying loose down the front of a white breastplate. It had the same metallic shine as the field of damaged silver, but she could turn the photograph all the way around and the hair kept falling from the woman's head.

Ireta looked back at the little girl. It was the woman she'd been twisting to see. One of the woman's shoulders hung lower than the other and her white glove disappeared into the shadow of a pillar beside what looked like a deep-set doorway. There was a chip out of the plate just above the knuckles, a dark crescent with a pinprick of light inside the curve. Ireta went back to that mark three times before she was satisfied that somebody had actually photographed it.

The hall steward knocked the way people knocked when they already had the door open. “They'd like to see the king. Put him up at the full width. If there's something wrong with his feet I can just drop the bottom of the screen a little.”

“His feet are the best thing about him.”

The steward had a pencil in his mouth and two more tucked in the loop on his tie. He propped the door with his heel while he tried to figure out what she meant. Downstairs the seats for the anniversary count had been set out on the flagstones, but several people had already gone up to the gallery to look for better ones. A woman with a reed-colored cane had put her gloves over two places in the front row. A girl who called her Grandma, wearing an apron over her coat, had carried up a cup for her and was holding it by both rims. The brewer's logo ran right across her stomach. Ireta saw her cradle the cup while the older woman turned her very firmly by the elbow to show her where to look.

She brought up the version he'd already approved for the screen. Until he bent to check it, she kept her thumb over the left edge where his crop cut off the things she'd just found. After he left she went to the coronation oils. There were four in the district collection. She had already scanned two for tonight. In the first the king had his full head of hair and his sister was in white silk, with foxes embroidered all the way up the fronts of her shoes. The painter had taken up a good third of the canvas with people looking down from the east gallery. Ireta followed the light down the painted faces to the dais and found the woman in the white hood behind the new king. A fine black crack ran from the crescent scar to the first joint of the forefinger.

By the next coronation the girl in the stocking was queen. Someone had to get on a stool and lay the crown on her with both hands. The painter's solution to the indignity was to have everybody else in the picture look fixedly down. Only the white-hooded woman looked up at the new queen. She was very nearly smiling. The painter had given her gauntlet a theatrical turn, as if whatever came into the queen's head might fall into that hand and become material there. At the base of the forefinger the crack opened wide enough for the artist to put in a single stroke of red.

For five minutes Ireta forgot about the screen. She tried the plausible things first. The second painter could have copied the earlier picture. She'd seen a child that size in another royal work and traced her all the way back to a calendar of the saints. But it was a different angle. The head in the hood had moved. From that angle she could see that the curl of the fingers in the famous pose went right round a little loop of iron set against the pillar. The painter had tried to carry the dark color of the background over it. It showed through where the paint had thinned.

Ireta had a folder of pictures of the last queen in which nobody had felt obliged to make her look dignified. They had drawn them from the gallery, so if the woman's hand was in any of them she could see what it was doing.

* * *

The execution sheets had been folded inside an account book. The man who sold the book to the district had reckoned Ireta would like the drawings because every one of the people in them was dead. There were six dark figures below the pale oval of the gallery. In the finished print from the same series each wore a face the court artist would have recognized. On the sheets the queen's brother looked like anybody taking his coat off in a hurry, and the woman waiting behind him was the sister whose shoes had been covered with foxes. Ireta knew how hard it was to draw a hand that was trying to untangle wet cloth. The little hard loops of graphite on the prince's cuff took her longer to get past than the black line the artist put under his name to mean he was dead.

One leaf had the queen on it four times. In the largest sketch she had turned her back on the gallery to take hold of the hand of the woman in armor. The woman had given it to her palm up. A strip of cloth came off the back of her hood and hung down against the wood. In the little sketch below, the queen walked out of reach. The hand stayed where it was, right out in the space between them with the fingers just beginning to fold. The rest of the woman was so still that the artist had drawn her once for both pictures. Only when Ireta enlarged the place where the drawings overlapped could she see what the other hand was doing. The painter's little iron loop was nearly the size of a person's head. It came through a slot in the pillar, and the woman's fingers hooked through it from below. The first finger, which had the split near the knuckle, had lost a plate there. The sketch left the shoulder a blank patch of paper but the artist had marked the opening in the glove all the way round in graphite.

Across the partition, the steward had started over from the beginning of the sound check. The first count, fifty-three years ago, was believed to have run from two in the afternoon until the following morning. Tonight everybody was supposed to get home in time for supper. The steward had once asked Ireta to find him a better picture of the jubilant public from the day of the count. She had given him a still from the security camera looking down on the gallery, although for all she knew the people on their feet were standing up to let somebody pass. The picture had gone on the anniversary cups. She went back for the moving version.

On the recording, people stood up one after the other along the outer rail to see what the tellers were doing. The shadows on the screen had a pale green cast that made the walls look damp. It took Ireta a moment to find the woman by the red at her knuckle. Her hood was high and broken along the edge. She was squinting up through an untidy fall of silver hair at the people standing, with both hands hanging at her sides. There was a sound like the crack of a tray of cups down a stairwell. The loop of iron moved out from the pillar and she caught it in her split glove.

Ireta rolled the footage back until the long metal eye slid back into the stone. She rolled it forward slowly. There was a flattened rod behind the eye, a black length of it, and it passed through the pillar's open face straight back to the underside of the gallery. As the rod came forward, the high end of the gallery went down. She had never seen wood move that far in a building somebody was sitting in. A man's knees went with it. The woman hooked her left hand through the eye and beckoned the man to the stone dais with her right. She turned that palm up. He stepped over before he had to take it.

On the radio a voice said there was an unauthorized woman on the floor. A second voice wanted to know which floor. The public shouted over the answer. They had finished counting votes at one of the tables and were calling up a number to the gallery. The woman drew her hair out of the hinge of her elbow with her good hand and settled against the weight. She had been looking up all that time without once looking at the men at the tables.

Ireta could hear the steward talking on the same monitor, nearer than voices through the partition. Somebody had switched the sound check to the live gallery feed. In the lower corner of the picture she could see the older woman's cane between the bench legs and the white hood beyond it, by the pillar. The red between the plates looked like a line of numbers seen through a piece of torn paper. As Ireta got up the woman shifted under the load, and the first finger of the armored glove fell past her hip and struck the dais. For the length of the noise it made, Ireta stood still.

* * *

The draft at the top of the ticket stair was cold enough to dry the sweat on Ireta's back. She came out of it along the wall into the gallery, where two people with cups were taking their time deciding whether to make room for her. Farther on there was a space between the benches and the last few steps down to the dais. It was where the attendants had stood in the first coronation oil. She'd thought it was a painter's kindness, a resting place for the eye, but here it was with nothing in it except enough varnished wood to make you watch your feet.

A young man in the aisle held a stack of programs out to her by mistake. Ireta took one. Under the seating plan somebody had put the jubilant public, leaning speculatively out of the frame. She wondered whether the man who had nearly gone down was the one with his hand spread out on the rail.

“Clear the gallery,” she told the steward. He was coming along the stone with the two spare pencils tucked into his fist. “Bring them down onto the dais. The stair runs right beneath these seats.”

“People are still coming up it. We can get them back down if the people at the top wait.”

She had touched down on stone by then and there was no give at all. There was a scrape behind her as somebody dragged a bench a little nearer the rail. The coat of varnish on the last wooden step parted along its grain. The woman by the pillar let out a breath Ireta could hear from the other side of the dais.

“Don't send them back under me, love,” she said. She had been keeping the iron eye up with her hand. With the first finger of the gauntlet gone she had to hold it a little higher, so the iron could turn and sit against the crooked second finger. There was a black plate where the eye came out of the pillar, wide enough to take two hands if you could spare them. A jagged end of iron stuck down from it. “A piece of the bearing's on the floor. It's going to come down empty just the same.”

If she brought her arm down, the hall would have had to answer for the weight it had put there. Ireta had the childish thought that there ought to have been men in the hall big enough to stand beside her and get that arm down by taking a little each. The men who'd been carrying the sound equipment had left a wheeled case on the dais with one of the wheels still turning slowly on its pin.

The woman looked straight at Ireta from so deep in her torn hood that for a second Ireta thought she was going to pull it up and show her what she looked like. Then she felt her own shoulders come down. The woman's eyes had been moving over her the way you look over a child coming in from the cold, to see how much there is to do. Ireta noticed a place under the woman's chin where swinging hair had worn the edge of the plate dull. When the iron moved against her gauntlet the hair gave a small settling movement, like something with its own weight.

“The brewery door,” Ireta said. She pulled up the photograph and spread it until she had room for the pillar, the hood, and the darkness beside the hood. There were two short scratches in the stone at the end of a white stripe. One scratch ran into the other at the bottom. She had taken them out of the earlier crop because the joined line looked like something written on the print. She could see the bottom half of it over the woman's shoulder, sticking out from under the wool hanging.

“What door?” The steward came as far as the hanging and wound the edge of it around his hand. “I know that stair. I go up it every morning.”

“Go down it now and tell them to clear the other stair. Here, hold this out of the way.” She gave him the end of the hanging. A gust smelled of old rain through the door when she lifted the handle, but it only came as far as her thumb. In the photograph the door had been open just far enough to see the rivets in the edge of the wood. It opened inward. She braced on the flagstones and hauled. The leather on the handle was cold and shiny. When she stood back, the side of the hill showed all at once, with boots going past it in both directions.

Somebody tried the dais and called up that it was solid. All along the nearest row, people sat down again to let a toddler and his father go first. Once people could see that there was room to cross and it was possible to go out without putting your cup down, they went. Ireta held the door against its spring. There was a smell of malt on the cold side and a smell of the hot coats as they passed her on the other. A man told her he would shut it when he came back in.

The top step shifted as the grandmother with the cane came close to it. Ireta had watched somebody's knees go under him in an instant on the old recording. Here it took time. The old woman put down her cane and the little rubber foot shuffled along by itself until it met the edge of the stone. She bent from the hip and put the cane down again. She had both feet under her and all of her attention on the strip of air that had opened at the bottom of the steps.

The girl came down the last two steps fast and stopped with her hands flat against her apron. She had a long loose tie trailing on the wood. The woman in armor said, “Take the bottom of your apron in both hands and lift it up. Look at where you've put your feet. You've got boots on, they'll go farther than that.” The girl looked down and in that time her grandmother put a hand behind her and pushed with the tips of her fingers. She stepped far enough that she ran up against the woman's knee on the stone. The woman rested the side of her bare cheek for a moment against the top of the girl's head. “Keep on. Right round the door. Watch where the stair goes left.”

“I used to come up here from the brewery,” the grandmother said. Her mouth worked for a while. She was watching her granddaughter and spoke once the girl had turned to hear. “My father had me by the strap of my dress, the whole length of the stairs. The crowd was trying to go two ways on the landing. He could never wait till the end of anything. He put me up on the rail there to see the queen and before she'd even gone he had me under his arm and I was looking at the back of his knee again. I thought I could show her just where I'd been.”

She raised the cane a third time in the direction of the benches. Ireta could see her foot turn as though she meant to go back and put her granddaughter where she'd been. There was no longer a straight line under the benches. In the hundred-year-old photograph, the edge of the king's cloak had been level with the eye in the pillar. The whole landing had come down the width of a finger in that time. It had come down just as far while people were filing past Ireta on the dais.

The woman lowered her head and moved her shoulder under the projecting iron plate, where Ireta could see the old whitish imprint of the bearing that had been there. The sleeve of her cape had lodged in the gap. She turned against it and the torn cloth pulled away through two little teeth in the plate. The edge of her shoulder scraped halfway over before it caught. A light at her collar went out. There had been another at her wrist, a deep steady red, which Ireta was still looking at when it began to blink. The woman let the weight come down sideways onto her shoulder. Slowly the ordinary bow of her body showed through her armor. With her free hand, the one the queen had reached for, she brushed the hair out of her face and held it open toward the grandmother.

“Oh, it was you up on the rail,” the woman said. “I could see your shoes from down here. One of your ribbons was untied and your father kept trying to get at it. Put the stick down, love. I've got you.”

The old woman looked right at her. Ireta had never seen the girl in the gallery who could have looked out of those old eyes. She had the head of the cane in both hands now. Her grip loosened as she looked, until one hand slipped off and the cane fell to the wood. The woman put her good thumb under the grandmother's fingers and guided them in against her palm, with a patience that took longer than the step. For an instant Ireta thought the two of them were only going to stand there, greeting each other. Then the old woman drew the fingers closed, stepped out over the strip of air, and put her foot down on stone. Ireta heard it take her weight.

The girl had come back as far as the door to lean around Ireta. She held out the apron she'd gathered in both hands, because it was what she had, and her grandmother held on to it as they went down the brewery stairs. As their steps faded Ireta heard the little knock of the fan down in the ticket room. The thumbnail clip in the corner of Ireta's tablet had the empty gallery in it and the woman standing where she had always stood, her hair coming down over one side of her face. Her shoulder was holding the iron. The gallery stopped moving. Ireta had the door heavy against her open hand and didn't use the other hand to wave.

The woman lifted her chin to look out past her. Then there was the small sound of a buckle. The woman slid her arm backwards out of the stiff sleeve of the gauntlet and drew it close against her chest, at the steady pace of someone pulling her arm free of a sleeping child's grasp. She had to bend her knees to get far enough under the iron to turn, and Ireta could hear the fit of the plates change as she did it. The gauntlet stayed closed around the eye. The instant the woman's shoulder came clear, the bearing plate tore out of the pillar and struck the place where she'd been standing.

The projection screen fell sideways with the gallery. Ireta held the door through a gust of stale wool and dust, and somebody on the brewery stair reached through and held it alongside her. Outside it was dusk and the red joints showed under the torn hood where the white parts of the armor were getting harder to see. Up under a lamp across the stair, the housing of the republic's other camera had a little eye of its own. It had the woman in view as she came out and lifted her good hand to tuck her hair under the hood. The other arm fell bare to the wrist from the ragged sleeve of her cape. On the stone just below them the girl had stopped to wait. When the woman drew level the grandmother reached out a bare hand again, and they went down slowly together, broadside to the camera the whole way.
