# What Won’t Take the Light

## Promised experience

An archivist restoring an old victory photograph discovers the silver-haired woman behind the royal family, then encounters the same woman in coronation paintings, sketches of the family's executions, and security footage made by the republic that replaced them. The same white armor grows more damaged with time. The later request for a “ghoust in the images and ghoustly in real life” makes the apparition the reader's first experience at the restoration table and the organizing force through the ending. The pleasure is the mounting unease of finding a living intention in records that should stay still, then meeting an apparition whose presence and absence both have weight. Her nature, allegiance, and relationship to the archivist and family remain creative choices.

## Provisional direction

Ireta restores the photograph in a republic archive in a former palace wing. Beneath the lifting edge of a card window there is someone missing from the familiar published image: a silver-haired woman standing in white armor behind the family. The little boy in the front row is reaching back toward her. As Ireta opens the margin, the woman's gauntlet moves a fraction toward his hand. Ireta puts the card back, and all five fingertips rap beneath it. She needs an independent record that can give the next person what she just saw; she has spent years submitting well-supported corrections that were waved away as something she alone saw in a print.

The coronation paintings deepen the apparition and establish the boy's loss without a full genealogy. The first has him there, still small and human, with a knotted sash the victory photographer did not straighten. The same armored hand bears a first chipped edge. In the second the sash is pinned across an empty child's place in mourning, and the armor has split. A flake of white paint moves against the gallery light where the woman reaches out of the painted posture and a stripe of red from the open wrist joint runs across the split. The execution artist drew several members of the royal family, then the emptied place where they died. Even there the woman is in the same corner, extending her hand toward an impression in the paper the height of a child's head. Over the course of the sketches her articulated knuckles tear the page and the plate below her left wrist is gone.

In republican security footage Ireta sees the building's old passage on a pivoting camera. A low door without a visible person on it moves outward against its chain. The lens swings past the woman. When it comes back she is several steps farther after the door, with her damaged glove held out low. On being caught in the frame she comes to an absolute stop except for silver hair streaming forward, and a piece of the white hood pulls loose. The footage cuts after an empty hand reaches the door's edge. The chain is gone by Ireta's time, but the doorway and the length of the crossing are still there.

## Scenes and ending

1. **Late shift at the restoration table.** Open on the photograph's response as the mask lifts. Ireta tests it by lowering the card, and the sound it makes reaches her without a lens. She brings up the coronation records and begins an account she could expect another archivist to believe. A draft from a sealed corridor moves the edge of her blotter while she watches the long silver hair settle in the plate.
2. **The records, then the corridor.** Give each medium its own kind of impossible motion and trace. Move from the changed paint to the execution sketches to the interrupted crossing in the footage. The woman's repeated reach finds its object in the child's absence. Beyond the door Ireta hears a child run and attempt a much quieter second pass.
3. **The crossing (long scene).** The spectral figure is present at eye level. Ireta can see the edge of a chair through a breach in the white articulated armor. Light remains in the red joints, and silver hair swings where the woman's visible weight ends. The woman wants the boy, and knows his hurry by his steps. Ireta turns the present camera aside when she sees the half-open hand draw up again in its field, then chooses to turn away herself at the woman's quiet request. Camera framing has a visible cost. The second act is a gift of privacy, made before proof of departure. A heavy step beside the chair sounds behind Ireta, and the woman, of her own accord, stops to rest a solid hand against Ireta’s shoulder before she follows the running steps through the door. Ireta waits long enough for the sound to go and then finds two paths of frost on the warm tiles: beside the tracks of the armored boots are the prints of a child. The victory photograph is still out on the table. The place the family has made at its back is empty.

**Voice and casting.** Stay close to Ireta, whose attention to the workmanship of a picture is also how she puts off being afraid. Give the unnamed woman the reserve in the supplied image, the long loose silver hair, splintered white helmet-hood, red seams, and long articulated fingers. Let her watch and listen first, and speak with the dry, practiced patience of someone well acquainted with a single child. The foil is the apparatus of the empty archive and the temptation to make one last picture. The important spaces are the same palace passage in the footage and the present and the little conservation room off it. Underfoot are warm red tiles and hollow spots that carry sound. Give the corridor a trace of the wool, warmed paste, and ticking heat in the ordinary working room. Keep the executions non-graphic and spare any explanation of the ghost's origins.
