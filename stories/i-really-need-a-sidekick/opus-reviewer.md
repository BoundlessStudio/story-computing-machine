# Opus review: I Really Need a Sidekick

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: PASS — every exchange holds up in the scene, it's funny, and it moves the relationship; what's left is local polish (one mirrored volley in the quarrel, two questions that only cue answers, two places named before they exist on the page, one taunt that repeats the narration).
- Prose: PASS — specific, varied comic narration in first person with no em dashes and no reversal or triplet tics; the only repeat is the ironic "apparently".
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — it delivers the sidekick promise, the reference-image elements, the tone and the length (about 3,290 words); there are small staging slips at the climax, and the motive behind the boast is thin.
- Edit scope: polish
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS"; agree, though it missed the referent and climax-staging slips listed below.

This story mostly avoids the collection's house-voice problem. Ottilie and Rococo sound different because they notice different things and behave differently with people. She gives brisk orders in private and grandstands in public ("I've moved beyond cases."). He asks where the spectators are, whether to bring a cape and which part is the triumphant one. Offstage he speaks with polite understatement, and he saves the full villain voice for the last line. The comedy comes from setups and replies the reader can follow: the snagged hem answered with "A short cape.", the pig, "encore" misread as another push. The key exchanges move him from hatch-holder to co-star without anyone saying so. The house pattern shows up only as the occasional clipped volley of counters, most clearly in the mirrored "They came to see…" lines behind the moon. The edit pass should make a few surgical fixes and protect the rhythm, the jokes and the ending. A heavy hand would flatten dialogue that already works.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · Mirrored retort volley in the moon quarrel

- Where: L163–L181, behind the moon right after the knee locks
- Text:
> “They came to see it stolen.”
> “They came to see us win.”
> “I think they'd have enjoyed that too.”
> It wasn't in the way of anything. I simply needed something to do with my hands.
- Problem: The lines make sense, and the second half of the quarrel (L173–L179, ending "It was a good pose.") works. But L165–L169 is the collection's house pattern in miniature: a mirrored "They came to see…" pair capped by a dry last word, four one-liners of equal weight (a mild Tennis Match with an epigram pair). Ottilie has just lost her exit, and her partner's leg, under fire. Yet her blame is as composed and balanced as his, and the narration then has to supply her agitation (the gloss at L171, "That made me angrier" at L181).
- Fix: Break the mirror. Make Ottilie's opening blame concrete and a little unfair (what he did with the bird, for how long). Rococo should answer from his unrepentant pleasure, not with a counter-thesis about what audiences want. Cut "They came to see us win." and replace or cut one of Rococo's two lines around it. Cut the two sentences after the cape tug at L171, since the tug already shows her state. Keep L173–L179 exactly. Illustrative only: Rococo: "Did you hear them, though?"

### D2 · Minor · Places named in dialogue before the page establishes them

- Where: L137, at the plinth before the lift; L223, during the moon push
- Text:
> “Straight into the passage when you have it,” I whispered.
> “Faster. She's at the stairs.”
- Problem: These are Ground/referent failures.
  - "The passage" has not appeared before. The narration that follows has to explain that it leads to the hatch, and only at L149 does the reader learn it runs through the castle and has a door Beryl controls.
  - "The stairs": the only stairs on the page so far are the castle stairs behind the pair (L109, L117, L121). Beryl is on the front gallery, which has no stair mentioned. A reader can picture her coming from the wrong side, and "came off the stairs to our left" (L227) makes that worse.
- Fix: Plant both in the floor description at L81 (a passage through the castle between the towers; a stair at one end of the gallery), or name them in the lines ("the gallery stairs"). Keep the whisper and Rococo's clipped spotter calls; they are the right register for that moment.

### D3 · Minor · Questions asked only to cue an answer

- Where: L211, as the brakes come off; L255–L257, behind the peacock's tail
- Text:
> “What are you going to do?”
> “You're crouching?” he asked.
> “As far as I go.”
- Problem:
  - L211: Rococo proposed the push himself (L185) and has just watched her release a brake. His question exists only to cue her instructions.
  - L255–L257: the narration has already said she crouched lower (L247) and that the tail rose in front of "our crouched bodies" (L253), so the question is redundant. "As far as I go" is an odd way to say "as low as I can", and the exchange stalls at the tensest moment. The real content is his next line, "Come closer. Your coat."
- Fix:
  - L211: let Ottilie give the orders unprompted, or give Rococo a question he couldn't answer himself.
  - L255–L257: cut both lines so "Come closer. Your coat." follows a beat of him checking her. If a question is kept, it should be one he needs answered, since he is gripping the bird from behind. Illustrative only: "Can she see any of you?"

### D4 · Minor · Narration pre-empts Beryl's taunt and misstates the danger

- Where: L243–L245, wedged against the arch post
- Text:
> As soon as we turned the moon to fit, she'd have a clear shot at our backs.
> “You'll have to turn it,” she called. “I'll have you then.”
- Problem: The narration states the tactical problem and then Beryl says it again, so her taunt lands as an echo rather than a threat. "Our backs" is also wrong. They are backing towards the arch with the moon between them and her, so turning the moon would expose their sides or fronts, not their backs.
- Fix: Cut the narration sentence and let Beryl's line deliver the problem; it is better coming from her. If a narration beat is needed, say what turning would expose without "backs".

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · "Publicly disowned" does not match what was said aloud

- Where: L97–L117, the boast and the decision not to use the hatch
- Text:
> “You'll want to watch the towers,” I said. “We shall be entering from above.”
> I could still take it, if I wished to crawl quietly through a plan I'd just publicly disowned.
- Problem: Ottilie never mentioned the hatch aloud, so she has not publicly disowned it. To a reader the boast works as an accidental feint: Beryl immediately guards the castle approach (L105). Turning down a hatch that has just become perfect can then look like the author steering towards the tower set piece. Her real motive is only implied: pride after the "No case today" jab, since crawling out from under the floor is the case all over again.
- Fix: Keep the boast and the choice. Tighten L117 by one clause so the motive is clearly hers: taking the hatch means popping out of a hole in the floor in front of the people who just laughed about the case. Do not add dialogue explaining it.

### C2 · Minor · Staging slips in the climactic push

- Where: L231, the moon push; L271–L279, getting through the arch
- Text:
> She was quick enough to outrun the wagon; a palace wagon stood between her and our unprotected rear
> The wagon rolled through the gap.
> For a moment we stopped with the moon jammed against one post and half the wagon still on Beryl's side.
> freeing the cloth caught against the wheel
- Problem:
  - L231 is missing its contrast ("but"). It also says "a palace wagon" when the established piece is the palace window (L81, L105, L235), so it reads like a new object.
  - L271 says the wagon rolled through the gap, but L275 has it stuck halfway. At the story's peak, the reader has to take back an action already reported as complete.
  - L279 frees a coat snag that was never shown; L275 blamed the stall on a slipping boot.
- Fix:
  - L231: "but the palace wagon…"
  - L271: "rolled into the gap".
  - L275: make the hem cause the stall (her boot on her own coat, or the hem under a castor). The rip at L279 then pays off the coat gag that started at L19.

## Preserve

- The opening: Rococo critiquing her "entrance" while she tightens his face, the coat caught in the drawer, and the whispering bearing ("a sinister glance should not sound like a boot in gravel").
- The planning beat where his wish to be seen surfaces without being stated:
> “And what am I doing?”
> “Then coming out too.”
- "Shall I bring a cape?", answered with the snagged hem and then "A short cape.", paid off when the cape falls over his head at the bow (L87).
- Beryl's "No case today, Ottilie?" / "I've moved beyond cases." / "Glad to hear it.", and the narrator resenting her for it.
- The self-indictment at the boast:
> there was still a moment in which to be a woman with a sensible plan and a regrettable mouth.
- "You drew a hatch." / "I know what I drew." Rococo's theatre knowledge of the forest counterweight (L119). The reversal on the tower, where she wants him to face the audience and he refuses (L129–L131).
- The quarrel's deflating compliment:
> “It was a good pose.”
- The pig exchange (L189–L203) in full. It is the story's real turn:
> “I was inside it.”
- Rococo's practical spotter calls under fire, and Ottilie's breathless "Wheel!"
- The tail as a legal shield, the feather against her goggles, and Beryl checking "my clean goggles". "I was sure I had you." / "So was I."
- The encore misread as another push; "Wait for the fanfare."; the squeeze signal turned back on her (L309).
- The ending, unexplained. "Give me the bird" probably carries the theatre sense of being booed (to "get the bird"), which the closing "waited for the boos" confirms:
> “Oh,” he said softly. Then, loud enough for the whole Rotunda: “You treacherous woman.”

## Suggested edit order

2. D4: trim the narration sentence at L243 in the same passage so Beryl's taunt carries the threat.
3. D3: cut or replace the two setup questions (L211, L255–L257) while working through the push sequence.
4. D2: plant the castle passage and the gallery stair in the L81 floor description, or name them in the lines.
5. D1: break the mirrored "They came to see…" volley and cut the gloss at L171; leave L173–L179 alone.
6. C1: sharpen the pride motive at L117 by one clause.
7. P1: sweep the extra "apparently".
