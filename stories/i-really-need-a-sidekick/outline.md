# Outline

## Story

- Premise and central promise: Mechanic Ottilie Vetch recruits Rococo, a battered robot and retired stage villain, for a sanctioned theft contest: carry a mechanical golden peacock out of a defended theatre. Her elegant caper becomes his comeback performance. Deliver a funny, physical adventure where their competing vanity becomes useful without disappearing.
- Focal pressure or attachment: Ottilie loves ingenious entrances; everyone remembers her self-locking escape case. Winning would advertise her adventures rather than other people's machinery. Rococo misses an audience and wants to try improvising outside rehearsed villainy.
- Counterforce or complication: Champion Beryl Hilt defends the prize with paint shots and movable scenery. She wants another entertaining victory and understands their vanity. A hit on either entrant eliminates the pair; abandoning a partner cannot secure victory.
- POV, distance, and information limit: First-person past through Ottilie, close to bodily effort and selective self-justification. Let actions contradict her flattering account. Rococo's intentions require visible conduct or communication.
- Governing movement and time shape: Workshop preparation followed by one twenty-minute contest, expanding from concealment to an exposed stage crossing. Keep victory and immediate aftermath onstage. Target 2,500–4,000 words.
- Speculative rule or ordinary-world constraint: Electrically powered Rococo has considerable upper-body strength, ordinary senses, and no remote machinery access. Sustained running overheats his damaged knee; he cannot jump safely. The peacock opens a broad tail and sounds a shrill fanfare when lifted: a known feature of the game.
- Dialogue promise: An aspiring dashing adventurer and an experienced ham struggle to make each other look suitably impressive while being impressively inconvenient.
- Dialogic medium: Workshop conversation, audible challenges across the theatre, breathless private argument behind scenery, and agreed hand or shoulder cues when speech would reveal position.
- Dialogue engine: Ottilie's boasts commit them to action; Rococo enlarges those commitments. Beryl addresses whichever ego can spoil their timing. Clear requests eventually coordinate an unforeseen stunt.

## Voice

- Narrative texture: Ottilie notices fit, leverage, clothing, and witnesses to embarrassment. She speeds through excuses but lingers on intended splendor. Physical consequences puncture her self-presentation.
- Conversational texture: Workshop impatience meets theatrical sociability. Include mundane questions, poor explanations, delighted interruptions, and jokes that land unevenly; allow replies without competing wit.
- Rhetorical ownership: Ottilie owns inventive excuses and mechanical descriptions. Rococo understands audiences, cruel jokes, and villainy's pleasures; extravagance is his choice. Beryl uses economical invitations and distracting praise, becoming blunt when losing.
- Pressure behavior: Ottilie gives instructions too quickly. Rococo initially addresses spectators instead of listening; physical uncertainty prompts plain questions. Beryl stops talking to change position.
- Relationship movement: Ottilie wants admiration beyond her repair skill but cannot comfortably solicit it. Rococo wants her participation in his delight, an awkward desire to explain. Their argument changes the attempted performance; neither concedes a permanent supporting role.
- Anti-default: Avoid a literal-minded robot proving personhood to a competent human. Both already possess social judgment, vanity, and enthusiasm for bad ideas.

## Beats

1. In her black-and-red workshop, Ottilie adjusts Rococo's scarred white head while he critiques her entrance. He is an established performer receiving repairs. Her need for a sidekick is embarrassingly urgent. Set his knee limit, their touch cues, and the difference between her stealth plan and his expectations of spectacle.
2. Enter the Gilt Rotunda, a converted theatre. Establish contest rules, wheeled scenery, load-bearing tracks, protected spectators, and Beryl's gallery position through use. Beryl recalls Ottilie's defeat. Ottilie boasts about a grander route than intended; Rococo takes her seriously. The changed approach costs time.
3. They reach the prize through complementary skills: Ottilie handles the moving access machinery while Rococo reads scenic timing. The peacock's fanfare exposes them as expected. Rococo nevertheless spends precious seconds presenting it to the audience, and Ottilie joins the flourish despite herself. Beryl closes their planned exit. Their scramble overheats the knee; it cannot recover during the remaining contest.
4. Behind a wheeled moon flat, they quarrel over lost seconds, visible routes, and what each actually did. Ottilie contributed to the showing off. Rococo proposes using the scenery he knows as moving cover. She can make that work, but it requires conspicuous cooperation rather than her intended graceful finish.
5. Build the escape from the established scenic route, the moon's broad shield, and the peacock's inconvenient tail. Ottilie moves the rig while Rococo braces and steers from its frame. Beryl leaves her gallery for a better angle and nearly catches them; they must change facing together using their earlier cues. The final rush is ungainly, contested, and successful. No hidden capability or convenient opponent surrender supplies the win.
6. Victory brings payment, damage, sweat, and laughter. Ottilie has attracted attention for a thoroughly inelegant act. Rococo enjoys her enjoyment. Their final meaningful exchange concerns a winner's encore, each changing the other's idea. End on the appetite success releases, without a friendship verdict or maintenance coda.
