# The House Behind the Bakery

Leni saw the house while carrying six rolls of brown paper home from the bakery. It stood across the narrow lot where the bakers stacked their empty flour barrels, and it had a porch that ended so close to the barrels she wondered how anyone opened the door. Snow lay on its three steps. A woman in a blue apron was beating a rug over the rail.

Leni stopped. Yesterday she had watched Mr. Ors put a barrel in that very place. She could still see it, half hidden behind the porch.

The woman shook the rug, sending a small white storm into the air. She went inside without looking at Leni.

Leni had heard of the winter house. People said you could walk the length of Bell Street one day and find it between buildings you had known all your life. It came back with the hard frosts and was gone by the time the crocuses were up. To see it, you had to miss someone who lived there. Leni had imagined a much taller house, with shuttered windows and somebody waving from one of them. This one had a bent rain pipe and a pair of boots set out to dry.

She set down the rolls. Through the front window she saw a lamp, a table, and the pale backs of three heads bent over a game. None belonged to anyone she knew. The upper windows gave her only reflections of the bakery and the white sky. Then she saw a paper bird hooked by one wing to the curtain cord.

It was made from the cheap brown wrapping paper Rosa Kett used at her shop. The wings were too wide for the body. Rosa liked to make them that way so they would tumble instead of flying straight. She and Leni had once filled the alley with a whole migrating flock of them, to the annoyance of Mr. Ors, who had to pick them out of his dough cart.

Rosa's shop had been shut for nineteen days. Leni knew because she had put a mark on the kitchen calendar each morning, though she told her mother she was counting the days until the school holiday. There was no sign in the window, only the bolt drawn across the inside of the door. At first Leni had thought Rosa was ill. Then someone said she had gone away for the winter, and everyone spoke as if that settled it.

Leni picked up the rolls and went home. She could have crossed the lot and knocked. She could also have stood on the baker's barrels and looked in the upper windows. The second idea seemed so rude that it made the first one feel rude as well.

At supper her mother was cutting the burnt edges off a pie. Leni told her about the porch and the boots and the paper bird.

"You're sure it was the house?" her mother asked.

"The barrels are behind it. I didn't know a house could be in front of something that's still there."

Her mother put down the knife. "I don't know how it fits. I've never seen it."

"Rosa's inside."

"Did you see her?"

"One of our birds was there."

"You made hundreds of those."

"Not with wings like that."

Her mother looked at the calendar on the wall. There were nineteen blunt pencil strokes in its margin. She must have noticed them before, but she had not said anything.

"I can come with you tomorrow," she said.

"Would you see it?"

"I don't know. I can see you."

Leni was old enough to know when her mother was trying to be kind and young enough to want a real answer anyway. "Why didn't Rosa tell me she was leaving?"

"I don't know that either."

Leni picked up a piece of pie crust and ate it. Her mother was not concealing a good explanation. That made the question harder to put down.

Before the shop closed, Leni had gone there on Wednesdays when her mother worked late. Rosa repaired books, boxes, umbrellas, and anything else a customer could carry through her door. The first time Leni came, Rosa had handed her a heap of torn envelopes and shown her how to fold birds. Leni had come to expect Wednesdays. Rosa had come to expect them too, or seemed to: she always moved the jar of paste away from Leni's end of the bench and left room there for her elbows.

In autumn, other children had begun to wait there. Some days there were five or six. Rosa made them clear a path to the counter and put away every sharp tool, even the little knife she used to lift old glue from a book's spine. She answered their questions while she worked and let them use the torn envelopes. Leni had sometimes wished them gone. She had not thought Rosa might wish it too.

The next afternoon, her mother walked with her to Bell Street. They passed the bakery and stopped at the lot. The house was there for Leni, rain pipe and all. For her mother there was only a row of barrels with snow between them.

"The door is just past the red one," Leni said.

Her mother looked where Leni pointed. "I believe you."

"Come closer."

Her mother stepped into the lot. Leni saw her skirt brush the porch rail; her mother flinched and put out a hand, finding wood she could not see. She drew back at once.

"It's there," Leni said.

"Yes."

For a moment they stood very still. Someone opened a window upstairs, shook out a cloth, and closed it again. Leni's mother followed the sound with her eyes but could not find where it had come from.

"Stay here," Leni said. "I'll ask if she's in."

The woman in the blue apron answered the door. Close up, she was younger than Leni had thought. She held a saucepan by its cold handle, and a green thread was caught in the cuff of her sleeve. The hall behind her smelled of onions and wet wool. Shoes lined one wall, none paired neatly with its mate.

"Is Rosa Kett here?" Leni asked.

The woman glanced past Leni at the lot. "Who's with you?"

"My mother. She can't see the door."

The woman shifted the saucepan. "Rosa's here, but she isn't receiving anyone this afternoon."

"Could you tell her Leni came?"

"I can tell her."

Leni waited. The woman remained in the doorway, waiting too. From deeper in the house came a rough, broken series of notes, as if a kettle were trying to sing through a crack.

"Is somebody making that noise on purpose?" Leni asked.

The woman smiled despite herself. "Rosa is."

She shut the door gently. Leni stood on the second step for another breath, trying to fit that noise to Rosa, who could make a sheet of paper lie perfectly flat with one stroke of her palm. Then she went back to her mother.

"She's there," Leni said. "She doesn't want visitors today."

Her mother nodded. She did not ask Leni to knock again. At home she put the pie dish in hot water and, while she scrubbed it, asked whether Rosa had looked well. Leni said she had not seen her.

"I'm glad she's warm," her mother said.

Leni almost said that she was the one who missed Rosa, so she ought to have been let in. It sounded ugly before it reached her mouth.

That night she tried to make a bird. The first had wings that buckled before she could throw it. The second had a neat, hard beak and fell straight down. On the third she folded one wing wider than the other, trying to remember Rosa's fingers. It crossed the kitchen, struck the pie dish on the draining board, and dropped behind it. Leni left it there.

On Thursday the house was visible from the far end of Bell Street. Leni had not expected that. She had supposed the sight of it might go away when she became angry. It seemed rude of the house to stand there unchanged, with its crooked pipe and ordinary smoke.

The bakery boy was carrying flour sacks from a cart. When he passed the lot, he took a careful step around the end of the porch. He did not look up.

"Can you see it?" Leni asked.

"See what?"

"The porch."

He glanced over his shoulder. "I know where it is. I've knocked my knees on it often enough." He balanced a sack against his chest. "Mind the third step. It's loose."

That was another thing Leni had not imagined: people who never saw the house learning its shape by bumping into it. At school the children had told stories about relatives appearing at its windows, and about its doors opening to people who cried hard enough. Nobody had mentioned Mr. Ors having to leave a space for it among his barrels.

Leni went home, fetched the bird from behind the dish, and wrote her name on one wing. On the other she wrote, *I saw the house. You can tell me not to come.* She had room for three more words, so she added, *But I miss you.*

The woman in the apron answered again. This time she had flour on both hands. Leni held the bird by its beak so the woman could take it without touching the writing.

"For Rosa. You don't have to ask her to see me. Just give her this."

"All right."

"Is she still making that noise?"

"Most afternoons. The rest of us have asked her to take a break at supper."

Leni tried to imagine several grown people telling Rosa to stop doing something. She had never seen it happen.

The house stood in the lot the next day and the next. Leni did not visit it. She noticed it on her way to school, when smoke came from the chimney. She noticed it at dusk when the upstairs lamp made a bright square in a window. Once the woman in the apron came out with a shopping basket, and the bakery boy held the lot gate for her while looking directly through her front door. The woman thanked him. That seemed to be how the house worked: it could be hidden and still require flour, onions, and someone to carry ashes out in the morning.

On Monday her mother found a bird on their doorstep. It was made from blue paper, with one wing absurdly long. Across it Rosa had written, *Wednesday. Four o'clock. If you like. Come alone; I can't fit a crowd up here.*

Leni read the note twice before she showed it to her mother.

"Are you going?" her mother asked.

"She wants me to."

"She said if you like."

Leni gave her a sharp look. Her mother said, "Yes, I know you like. I'll walk you to the lot."

On Wednesday, the third step did rock under Leni's foot. She pressed its edge down before her mother came near it, though her mother stayed beside the red barrel. The woman in the apron opened the door and sent Leni upstairs with directions: second door on the right, mind the low beam.

The hall was narrow because a folding table had been set against one wall. A man was sorting buttons there into teacups. He moved his chair so she could pass. On the landing, a coat hung from a nail with a sleeve still damp from the snow. Leni could hear the broken music again. It stopped when she knocked.

Rosa opened the door with an instrument in her hand. It was a small wooden pipe with a silver mouthpiece. Her hair had been cut badly, short at one ear and longer at the other. A round mark from the mouthpiece showed on her lower lip.

"That bird flew all the way here," Rosa said. "It hit the stove, but that's a long way for your paper."

"You didn't make the wings right on yours," Leni said.

"No. I thought I'd try it your way."

There were two chairs in the room. The bed had a red blanket and a chipped mug on the floor beside it. Little curls of shaved wood lay on a plate, along with several reeds Rosa had split while trying to make them thin enough. She put the pipe on the table and took the other chair, leaving Leni to choose the one by the window.

From there Leni could see her mother by the barrel. She was talking to Mr. Ors, who had come out with a tray of loaves. Neither looked toward the upper window.

"What is it?" Leni asked, nodding at the pipe.

"A shawm. A small one. I'm learning."

"Who taught you?"

"The woman downstairs knows two songs. After that I'll have to find somebody else."

Leni studied the split reeds. Rosa had fixed the cracked spine of Leni's schoolbook so finely that she could scarcely find the join. Seeing her fail at something made the room feel less like Rosa's shop and more like a place Leni had never been, which it was.

"Are you coming back?" she asked.

"To Bell Street? In the spring, I think. I'm paid through March for this room."

"To the shop?"

Rosa turned the shawm once between her hands. "I haven't decided."

Leni's chest went tight with the effort of keeping her voice steady. "You could have told me before you left."

"I should have." Rosa set the pipe down. "I told myself I would say goodbye on Wednesday. Then I closed the shop on Tuesday, and I let myself go without doing it. I'm sorry, Leni."

This was a plain answer. Leni had imagined a secret that would explain nineteen marks on the calendar and the locked shop. She did not know what to do with a plain answer.

"Did you leave because we were there?" she asked.

"Not because of you." Rosa rubbed a splinter off the edge of the table. "I kept saying yes when people asked me to mind their children. At first it was an hour now and then. By autumn, I couldn't open a book with a knife unless I knew where six small hands were. I should have said no before I was cross with everyone."

"You were cross with me?"

"Sometimes. You know how you used to put your feet on the paste shelf?"

"I stopped."

"After I asked you forty times."

Leni felt a laugh escape, though she had not wanted to laugh. "Maybe thirty."

"Thirty-nine, then." Rosa's mouth moved but did not settle into a smile. "I missed you too. And I wanted the room to stay empty when you went home. Both things are true."

Leni looked out at the lot again. Her mother had gone into the bakery to get out of the wind. The red barrel stood alone under its cap of snow.

"I don't want you to be cross when I come," Leni said.

"Nor do I."

"Can I come if I ask first?"

"You can ask. I can say no. Some days I will." Rosa lifted the shawm. "Today you can stay until half past four, if you want to hear the part I know."

"Will it sound like it did through the door?"

"Probably."

Rosa blew a short phrase. The first two notes ran together. On the third, the instrument made a sound like a goose backing into a fence. Downstairs somebody called, "That one's getting closer!" Rosa leaned toward the stairwell and called back, "You said that yesterday!"

Leni laughed properly this time. She had never heard Rosa answer another adult in that loud, delighted voice. Rosa tried the phrase again. Leni watched the bare place on Rosa's lower lip fill slowly with color, and she listened until the right note came, quite small and clear among the bad ones.

At half past four Rosa walked her downstairs. The woman in the apron was kneading bread at the folding table now; the man with the buttons had gone. Before Leni left, Rosa gave her a split reed.

"It's no good for the pipe," she said. "You can make something from it, if you like."

"What?"

"I don't know."

Outside, Leni showed her mother the reed. From the lot, the house had its lamp lit already. Its third step had gone crooked again after they walked down it. Leni wanted to go back and set it straight, but Rosa had said half past four, and it was half past four. Her mother took her hand, and they went home by the bakery. The reed was light enough that Leni almost lost it in the wind. She closed her fingers around it and kept walking.
