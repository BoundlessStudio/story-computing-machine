# Opus review: The First Call

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — at the cord decision, the talk about carts never separates the belled wagon from the refugees' handcart, so "That cart was loaded" misleads the reader about why the sergeant cuts the cord.
- Prose: PASS — lean and concrete; only minor trims (theme restated by the narrator, negation clusters in the climax, one drain).
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the burning-wagon sequence that traps the refugees can't be pictured, and several smaller facts (pitch-soaked reeds, the final direction, the realization's evidence) need a clause each.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, Dialogue: PASS; partly disagree — most dialogue is sound, but the decision-beat referents and the post-cut staging fail.

The problem here is not the collection's usual house-voice convergence. The three survivors talk in clipped field shorthand, which suits smoke, blood loss and a burning fuse, and their lines separate by job: the scout reports and hedges, the surgeon reasons in bodily limits and dry asides, the sergeant asks and orders in one to three words. The rooster's wordless consent (beak on the wrist, closing and opening the wing) is the best dialogue in the story. The failure is Ground. At the one decision that matters, the talk uses five names for at least two vehicles, and the surgeon's reply to "Could still be a fire cart" reads as confirming that the handcart was the fire cart. The edit pass should make the vehicles and fires legible in a few words, keep the clipped register and the silences, and add no line that explains the cut or states the theme.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt promise: a rooster in battered armor is laughed at; years later the last surviving soldiers see an enormous feathered warrior come out of the burning field with arrows in his back and dawn behind him, and realize he was never anyone's mascot. L181 carries that image almost verbatim; keep it.
- Length 2,500–4,000 words (body is about 2,950 now); broadly accessible; wounds stay non-graphic. Active craft profile is prospective-2026-08-21 (the rewrite request's constraints); the rewrite request covers all dialogue.
- The rooster's rule as written: once, at first direct dawn light, by his choice, for nine minutes; no flight, no healing; arrows keep their size; heat during, cold after; the armor unfolds mechanically. Keep the timing chain: seven minutes to sunrise, six-minute slowcord with notches, nine-minute change timed on the quarter-hour sandglass.
- No proper names anywhere; the kingdom and causeway are unnamed. `universe/locations.md` has a LOCKED "Ninepin Causeway and Reedmarsh" with similar imagery (raised causeway over black marsh water, wagons blocking the road); do not add names or details that suggest a link.
- The rooster never speaks. Consent runs through gesture and must stay that way.
- Keep the silences that carry decisions: the scout's non-answer at L125 and "No one asked whether that was a new order." The sergeant keeps the unburned cord and the coal; the cord later lashes the litter.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Two vehicles, one "cart" at the cord decision

- Where: L79–L133, the three survivors on the east road deciding whether to fire the causeway
- Text:
> "If it's the fire wagons, four is enough."
> "Smoke moved." He listened. "Harness again. No marching feet."
> "Three uprights," the scout said. "Certain now. One pushing the wheels."
> "Could still be a fire cart," the sergeant said.
> The surgeon said, "That cart was loaded."
- Problem: Ground/referent. The scene has at least two vehicles: a horse-drawn wagon with harness bells (L69, L91, L127) and the refugees' handcart (L117, L165). The dialogue never separates them, and "the fire wagons" (L79) arrives with a definite article the story has not set up; the order at L71 mentions only "the enemy's wagons". The sergeant's "Could still be a fire cart" is about the handcart the scout just described. The surgeon's "That cart was loaded" comes next and picks up her word, so the listener, and the reader, most likely take it as "the handcart you worried about was a fire cart, and it has just gone up." On that reading the handcart group is dead and the cut at L133 is a tactical response to a spent threat. L165 then contradicts it. The story's decisive choice loses its motive at the moment she makes it.
- Fix: Keep "Could still be a fire cart" and the scout's silence; they are the best pressure in the scene. Establish the enemy's pitch or fire wagons once in narration near L71 so "the fire wagons" has a referent. Let the scout hear the bells as separate from the pushed wheels (at L91 or L117). After the crash at L127, the reply should identify the burning vehicle as the belled one and tell the sergeant the handcart is still out there, so her cut follows from what she has just learned. That is a report, so it fits the scout; if the surgeon keeps it, make it name the wagon. Illustrative only: "That's the bells. The handcart's still coming."

### D2 · Minor · Retreat stakes left in shorthand

- Where: L81–L93, the same decision exchange, before the cord is lit
- Text:
> "Can you cross?" the sergeant asked.
> "With a shoulder, perhaps sixty paces. Not quickly.
> "By the time you're certain, the cord won't give us six minutes."
- Problem: Ground/referent. The causeway "ahead" is the only crossing the scene has named, so "cross" first reads as crossing toward the enemy; its real object, the low ridge, arrives only in L85's narration. "Sixty paces" has nothing to measure against because the ridge's distance is never given, so the reader can't tell whether lighting the cord strands the surgeon. The cord burns six minutes whenever it is lit, so the surgeon's six-minute line works only if the reader combines seven minutes to sunrise (L69) with the fire-before-dawn order (L71) and concludes that waiting would mean a shorter cord. The sergeant would follow him; the reader has to stop and do the sum.
- Fix: Point "cross" at the ridge and give the ridge a distance once in L85 so sixty paces clearly means "not enough" or "just enough". Tie the surgeon's line to the sunrise deadline instead of the cord's length. Illustrative only: "Can you make the ridge?"

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Narrator restates the story-versus-evidence point

- Where: L33, L43, L53–L55, L61, L143, the muster and the seven-year montage
- Text:
> That was trust, and because it was real, she mistook the story wrapped around it for real too.
> She could have followed the evidence then.
- Problem: Philosopher in narration. The montage already shows the gap between legend and evidence through objects (chalk not sand, pine pitch not marsh tar, both armies' fletching). The narrator then states the point again at L33, L43, L53, L55 and L61, so by L143, where the prompt needs the realization, it has already been explained. L55's last sentence is the clearest gloss: it names both the trust and her error and leaves the reader nothing to infer.
- Fix: Cut L55's last sentence and L61's first sentence; the "Instead she burnished the plates..." sentence already shows her choice. Keep L33 and L43, which compress events instead of glossing them, and keep L141–L143 as the prompt's realization.

### P2 · Minor · Negation clusters in the climax, one clashing with the next beat

- Where: L165–L175, the rescue on the causeway
- Text:
> The rooster did not look for a uniform.
> No magic closed the wounds. No flame turned aside for him.
> He did not turn toward the charred royal standard. He did not come to the sergeant.
- Problem: L167 has already shown that the arrows keep their size and still hurt him. After that the narrator keeps underlining the anti-mascot theme and the magic's limits by listing what the rooster doesn't do. "The rooster did not look for a uniform" repeats what the mule's mixed colors just showed. "He did not come to the sergeant" then clashes with L179–L181, where the scout says "He's coming" and the rooster lies down beside the milestone next to her.
- Fix: Cut L165's last sentence. Keep at most one sentence of the L171 pair. Keep "He did not turn toward the charred royal standard" (the prompt's mascot payoff), and cut or reword the sergeant clause so it doesn't contradict L181, for example by saying he didn't look for her while he worked.

### P3 · Minor · Water infrastructure as texture

- Where: L51, the winter-siege visit
- Text:
> At dusk he vanished through a drain beneath the eastern wall.
- Problem: Policy nit (style guide, "No default water infrastructure"). The drain is incidental texture the prompt did not ask for.
- Fix: Use an exit that isn't water infrastructure: a gap under the palisade, a sally port, a breach in the eastern wall.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Major · The blocked causeway can't be pictured

- Where: L137–L151, from the cut cord to first light
- Text:
> A second voice answered from farther north, where the king's retreating sappers had abandoned two more pitch wagons.
> Fire came from both sides of the road now. One wagon rolled backward without horses, its flames tilting as it gathered speed.
> "That was the east rail," the scout said. "Causeway's blocked."
> Beyond him, dim figures struggled with the handcart, trapped between the wreck and the fire advancing through the reeds.
- Problem: Confusing staging at a key beat. The reader cannot tell whose voice answers from the north (enemy scavengers or royal stragglers), who set the abandoned royal wagons alight, which wagon rolls (the enemy wagon whose horse bolted at L127, or a royal one), what "the east rail" is, or how the handcart group ended up on the far side of the wreck. The sergeant's deduction at L141 ("Damage from every banner, always carried out of a fire that threatened the same road") depends on the reader having just seen fire from both banners, and "Fire came from both sides of the road now" is too vague to support it. The rooster's whole dawn action is clearing this wreck, so the reader needs to see it.
- Fix: In two or three sentences of narration, not dialogue: name the rolling wagon's banner, say where it stops (across the far end), and put the handcart group east of it. Make the royal-side fire concrete, for example the abandoned sappers' wagons catching. Either set up the causeway's rails near L69 or change "the east rail" to a feature the reader already has. Reuse the vehicle names fixed under D1. The scout's short call can stay once its referent exists.

### C2 · Minor · Pitch-soaked reeds versus the advancing fire

- Where: L85, L95, L151, the prepared line and the fire at the far end
- Text:
> The pitch line ran from the roadside jars into reeds beneath the causeway.
> The reeds were soaked.
> the fire advancing through the reeds
- Problem: The sergeant has soaked the reeds under the causeway with pitch. Minutes later, fire is moving through the reeds. Nobody checks whether it will reach the prepared line, which would fire the road anyway, catch the rooster and the refugees, and make her cut pointless.
- Fix: Add one clause: either the prepared reeds are at the near end and this fire is in dry reeds beyond them, or the sergeant watches it creep toward the pitch line. The second option gives the countdown a real threat to use.

### C3 · Minor · The realization's evidence overreaches

- Where: L49, L61, L141, the repair visits and the sergeant's deduction
- Text:
> Yet the grit beneath his plates was white road chalk, not river sand
> Beneath them she found ash, more pine pitch, and the fine red dust of the east road.
> white road chalk, black pitch, royal feathers, enemy barbs, cage wire cut twice.
> Damage from every banner, always carried out of a fire that threatened the same road.
- Problem: Internal continuity. The deduction says every visit came out of a fire on the same road, but the river-campaign visit shows no trace of fire (chalk, a crushed hinge, an enemy barb), and its white chalk differs from the east road's red dust. Her evidence doesn't support "always" or "the same road". "Royal feathers" first reads as the rooster's own plumage; it means the king's grey-and-blue fletching (L57). The prompt's key recognition should rest on evidence the reader already has.
- Fix: Either add a trace of soot or pitch to the river visit at L49 or soften "always". Either make the chalk belong to the east road or change "the same road" to what the evidence shows. Replace "royal feathers" with "royal fletching" or "grey-and-blue fletching".

### C4 · Minor · The final walk heads toward the enemy without comment

- Where: L239–L257, the litter and the fork
- Text:
> The scout studied the fork beyond the marsh. "East branch drops more gently."
> "I saw willows. I didn't see water."
- Problem: The soldiers are at the west end of the causeway. "Beyond the marsh" and "the east branch" send them across it, toward where the enemy was shouting at L137. The exchange covers slope, water and distance but not the most obvious risk, so either the enemy has gone (never said) or three royal soldiers carry a litter into them without a word. The ending's meaning, that they follow the rooster's road instead of the king's, is good; it just needs one fact on the ground.
- Fix: One clause of narration or one scout observation placing the enemy (pulled back north, scattered by the fires) so the east branch reads as a choice, not an oversight. Keep the practical talk and "No," said the surgeon. "Go."

### C5 · Minor · Who can reach the wounds on the giant form

- Where: L187–L215, the arrow removal
- Text:
> The sergeant climbed between the unfolded back plates.
> The surgeon pressed folded linen to the wound.
- Problem: Staging. The first wound is high enough that the sergeant has to climb onto the plates of a bird taller than the causeway posts. The surgeon, who could manage "perhaps sixty paces" with help and sits against the milestone, presses linen to it and later packs the second wound. The reader can't place him.
- Fix: Either have the rooster lower the wing so the wounds come down to the milestone, or keep the surgeon on the ground giving instructions while the sergeant presses and packs. "Hold it straight. Not up. Straight." already works that way.

### C6 · Minor · The recognition comes before the prompt's image

- Where: L103, L141–L143, L181, the scout's greeting, the sergeant's deduction, the giant's return
- Text:
> "Knew our luck wasn't dead."
> The stories had put him beside their victories because that was where they had happened to see him afterward.
> The enormous rooster limped toward them with dawn at his back and three arrows standing from his blood-wet feathers.
- Problem: Prompt fidelity. In the prompt, the realization comes when the last soldiers see the enormous warrior come out with arrows and dawn behind him. The story finishes the sergeant's realization at L143, before sunrise, so the prompt's image at L181 confirms something already known. The scout's "our luck" framing at L103 is never answered. The surgeon's "May we pull this one?" does show a change, and that is the right kind of evidence.
- Fix: Optional and small: let the sense of L143 land after L181, as the sergeant watches him come in, and leave L141's evidence where it is. The scout's countdown already serves the rooster; if a beat is added, make it behavior (he keeps the glass for the rooster, or doesn't say "luck" where he could), not a statement. Add no thesis line.
