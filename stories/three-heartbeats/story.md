Ruvan let a violet petal fall between them.

Asereth stepped out of time.

The petal hung edge-on above the marble. Behind it, the human harpist's thumb rested against a string that would not sound. The candle flame had bent toward the open door and stayed there, a small gold pennant in wind that no longer touched her face.

She had one heartbeat. She turned the curved blade in her right hand, slipped its point through the petal, and caught the falling silk of her skirt with her left wrist.

Her heart struck. The candle straightened. The harp sounded. Half a petal settled on either side of her bare foot.

Ruvan made a mark on the paper beside his harp.

“Two hundred and twenty.”

“You sound disapproving.”

“You sound pleased.”

She was. The cut had been so fine that neither half had turned in the air.

He put down the pen. “You still mean to do it.”

From beyond the door came the court's gathering noise: glass on silver, the rustle of gowns, a chair moved for someone too important to move it himself. Asereth laid the second blade across her knees. Its gold guard gleamed against the brown of her hand.

“I can make the turn.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

For twenty years he had played for her. He could tell when she was counting a phrase in her head instead of listening to him. Now he put a finger on the last page of the score, where three small crowns had been drawn above a single bar.

The Crown of Air. Two centuries old, unfinished in every court performance because the approved ending took the dancer away from that bar. In the royal dancers' private books, the three crowns remained. Asereth had worn the leather pale studying them.

A stolen heartbeat could put a blade where no watching eye had seen it travel. Let the world move again, gather breath, borrow another: that was the secret beneath the dancers' impossible grace. Every theft would be repaid, sooner or later. The Crown required three thefts joined without a return between them. Every master had forbidden it. Without the returns, they said, the whole waiting debt might come due.

Nine days had been taken from Asereth at her last repayment. She had reached for her chamber door and found it locked. The flowers in her room were dead. Her father's birthday had happened without her. In the long reach of an elven life, nine days had seemed a bearable price.

Since then, two hundred and twenty marks.

“We don't know what it will take,” Ruvan said.

She stood and let the purple silk drop from her wrist. Fine gold chains settled against the fitted bodice. One had caught in her silver hair; Ruvan freed it with the quick, familiar motion of a man accustomed to untangling a princess from her own costume.

“We don't know that it will take anything tonight.”

“No.” His hand stayed on the score. “We don't.”

She could dance the approved ending beautifully. She had done it so often that the thought of doing it once more felt like opening a door onto the same painted wall.

Beyond that bar was something no living dancer had made.

“Play it as we rehearsed,” she said.

Ruvan looked at her for another moment, then gathered the pages.

The hall was full of candlelight. It ran along the curves of Asereth's swords and multiplied beneath her in the polished floor. Purple banners rose between the pointed windows. At the far end, her father leaned forward in his great carved chair, already smiling. Tiriel sat beside him with a loose dark braid falling over one shoulder.

Her sister lifted two fingers: their childhood signal for good luck. Asereth answered with a tiny inclination of one blade, too small for anyone else to read.

Ruvan played the opening.

She drew the first circle slowly. Gold embroidery followed her across the marble; her blades parted the reflection of a chandelier. When the high strings entered, she passed the right sword behind her head and let its weight carry her into the turn. Her foot found the next place without looking.

The court quieted. Even the king's smile changed. He knew the work that had gone into this, the mornings she had come to breakfast with her hair still damp from practice, the callus beneath each jeweled ring.

She loved the instant when a room ceased to contain people waiting for the next thing and became a room watching this one.

Ruvan's left hand climbed. Her blades climbed with it.

At the bar before the three crowns, she should have turned outward, taken four running steps, and finished facing the throne.

Instead she planted her left foot.

Ruvan saw. His right hand struck the first chord of the forbidden measure.

Asereth sprang.

On the first stolen heartbeat, the hall stopped.

Her father's hand hung an inch above the arm of his chair. Tiriel's braid had lifted from her shoulder. A drop of wine stood free of a cup at the edge of Asereth's sight. She brought the left sword under the right, passing the two curves so closely that the gold guards touched. The shock went into her wrists. No answering ring came from the hall.

She could return now. She could land on the approved side of the figure, and Ruvan would find her.

She took the second heartbeat.

Her knees folded beneath her. Silver hair swept across her eyes. She opened the swords into opposite arcs, one high, one low, and turned between them. There was only room for that turn. The familiar pressure gathered behind her breastbone, the small inward dragging that followed a theft.

Then came the place in the books where every diagram ended.

She took the third.

The upper blade descended. The lower rose. Her wrists crossed, uncrossed; her right foot reached for the floor. For an instant her whole body lay within the figure she had spent years trying to understand: the two crescents, the turning line of her shoulders, the open space at their center.

Yes, she thought.

Her foot came down.

The music was wrong.

She completed the movement, knees bending to take her weight, swords extended. A single low note shivered through the floor. Someone cried out.

White flowers lay around her feet. A candle had fallen, rolling toward her, and a young man in a dark coat was scrambling to catch it.

The king's chair stood beneath a black banner.

Asereth raised her head.

Tiriel was standing. Her hair was pinned high, her dark gown closed at the throat. Their father's crown sat above her brow.

For a moment Asereth thought this was some elaborate rebuke. They had seen what she intended and prepared a joke cruel enough to frighten her away from ever trying it again.

Then the woman holding the harp stood up.

She was a stranger.

An old man beside her dropped his cane.

“Asereth?”

She knew his voice before she could find Ruvan in his face. The broad forehead was there, and the slight bend in his nose. His beard had gone white. The hand braced against the harp's pillar was thin and marked, the first two knuckles swollen large.

She looked from him to the banner.

PRINCESS ASERETH. THE SEVENTEENTH VIGIL.

“No,” she said.

Tiriel came down the steps. A guard moved across her path, one hand going to his sword. She held out her palm without taking her eyes from Asereth.

“Stand aside.”

He did, and the courtiers behind him drew back to let her through.

Asereth let her swords sink. She still had the last movement in her muscles. Her left shoulder burned with the effort of it. A bead of sweat traveled down her temple, past the little amethyst Ruvan had straightened before she went on.

Her sister stopped a pace away, close enough to touch her, and did not.

“How long?” Asereth asked.

Tiriel's mouth moved once before she answered.

“Seventeen years.”

Behind her, the old harpist bent with difficulty to retrieve his cane. The young woman caught it first and put it into his hand.

“I only took three,” Asereth said.

Ruvan shut his eyes.

Tiriel crossed the remaining space and caught her sister so hard that one of the blades struck the floor.

They made her eat something. She could not remember what.

People came into the little room behind the hall, then were sent out again. A man she did not know called her Highness and wept. She recognized a woman who had once served their mother, except that the woman's daughter was standing beside her now, keeping an arm at her back.

Asereth kept turning the hilt of a sword against her palm. The ridges were precisely where they had been a moment ago.

Tiriel unpinned her crown. A shallow red mark crossed her brow.

“Where's Father?”

Her sister's hands stopped.

Asereth looked at the black ribbon tied below her shoulder. “That's for him.”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Six years ago. In the winter.”

There had been time for eleven years of him after she vanished. He had walked about, eaten breakfast, been angry with people. He had grown ill, if that was what had happened. There had been a last day.

She had been turning between two swords.

“I want to see him.”

Tiriel put the crown down.

They went through a corridor hung with tapestries Asereth did not recognize and down to the royal tombs. Tiriel carried the lamp. When they reached the new stone, Asereth stood before it for a long time, reading the dates.

In the niche above the inscription lay a small silver gull, its wings made into a clasp. She had left it wrapped in her room. She had intended to give it to him after the performance.

“You found that.”

“Two months after. I gave it to him.”

“Did he like it?”

“He wore it.” Tiriel moved the lamp. “All the time. It was rather too large for his blue coat.”

Asereth tried to imagine the gull on that coat. She could see him grumbling at its weight, then putting it on anyway. What she could not see was herself entering the room and watching him do it.

The clasp had tarnished in the creases beneath its wings.

She reached up, then lowered her hand. It belonged here.

*

Ruvan was waiting in the rehearsal room the next morning. He had a different harp, deeper in the body than the old one. A medal hung on his coat. Beside his chair stood the young woman Asereth had seen at the vigil.

“Dema,” he said. “My pupil. Once. She plays for the queen now.”

The young woman bowed. “I'm glad you're here, Highness.”

“So am I,” said Asereth, and was surprised by the fierceness of it.

She had slept badly. Every time she woke, she expected the door of her room to open on another year. She had no way to tell whether the seventeen years had paid for every mark. She had held a candle until it burned down, watching the wax travel a distance she could account for.

Ruvan had set the old score on a stand. The three crowns were still there. The paper had darkened around them.

“Tiriel wants a celebration tonight,” Asereth said.

“Yes.”

“We should finish.”

He rubbed the side of his thumb against the harp's wood.

“From where?”

“The beginning. It ought to have a beginning and an end in the same evening.”

He looked at Dema, then back at the score. “Let me warm my hands.”

She waited. He sounded the strings, adjusted a tuning pin, and tried a phrase twice. Asereth set down her outer robe. The costume had been brushed and mended where her dropped blade had cut its hem.

Ruvan began.

For eight bars she knew exactly where she was. She passed the blade behind her shoulder and took the long step into the rising line.

The line broke. Ruvan missed a note, caught the next, then lost the run altogether. She finished the step without music.

“Again,” she said.

His mouth tightened. He began at the bar before the run. This time a string buzzed against his finger, making an ugly, flat sound. He took his hands from the harp.

“My left won't cross quickly enough.”

“We can take that bar a little more slowly.”

“That bar, yes. Then there's the next, and the one after. There are six pages of it.”

She looked at his hands. He closed the left one carefully and opened it again.

“When did that happen?”

“The swelling? About five years back. It comes and goes.” He pointed with his right hand. “Dema can play it.”

The young musician had been studying the floor. Now she looked up.

“I know the score, Highness.”

Ruvan began to lever himself out of the chair.

Asereth almost asked him to stay there. But he was in pain, and Dema was waiting, and she had no claim on either of their hands.

She moved the stand so that he could get past it.

Dema sat, settled the harp against her shoulder, and sounded the opening chord.

It was beautiful. Her tone was clearer than Ruvan's had been, bright enough to lift Asereth's chin. At the first rising line, Asereth turned and found the music exactly where she needed it. Dema watched her feet without seeming to lower her eyes.

They reached the three crowns.

Asereth turned outward into the approved ending. Four running steps, the blades opening, the last note sounding as her heel touched the floor. She could still give this to an audience. Even with another ending, it was a great dance.

Ruvan struck his cane against the floor in approval.

“There,” he said. “You see?”

Dema smiled. “I would like to try the fourth-page turn again. I think we can give it more room.”

They tried it. They tried two more passages. At the end Asereth was breathing hard, her legs pleasantly unsteady. Nothing had been taken from the strength she had carried out of the old evening.

“Tonight, then?” Dema asked.

“Tonight.”

The younger woman went to tell the chamberlain which harp she wanted moved into the hall. Ruvan gathered his papers.

“What did Tiriel give you the medal for?” Asereth asked.

“Music.”

“I had guessed that much.”

“A set of songs for her coronation. Though she says it was also for enduring your family.” He stood. “Come and see the room they gave me. There's a window that opens without a fight.”

It had once been a small retiring room overlooking the western court. Now shelves filled one wall, and a smaller harp stood in the light. There were dried leaves between sheets of music, a painted cup full of pens, a child's badly carved horse. Ruvan put the old score on a shelf among a dozen others.

Asereth read the titles. Two had dates she could not fit into any remembered season. One bore a dedication to a woman whose name meant nothing to her.

“You married?”

“Eleven years ago.”

“Ruvan.”

“I know. Everyone was astonished.”

She picked up the little horse, examined its enormous head, and put it back.

He had gone to the window harp. Without opening a book, he began to play.

The first phrase took her by surprise. Two low notes, a sudden flight into the upper strings, then a rest so long she thought he had stopped. When she moved to speak, the melody darted in beneath her breath.

His left hand laid down a low chord and lifted. His right moved quickly across a small cluster of strings. The left returned, changed the whole weight of the phrase, and was gone again.

Asereth shifted her feet.

Another quick flight. She imagined a blade passing overhead, its curve answering the upward rush. But the next phrase fell steeply instead. She would have to turn beneath the blade, lower her body, change hands.

“What's that?”

“Swifts at the West Window.” Ruvan nodded toward the eaves outside. “They were nesting there. Dreadful little creatures. All mouth.”

“Play the rest.”

He did. She followed, first standing still, then letting one shoulder turn. The music kept offering her spaces she could not have prepared. A held note could bear a long balance; a brief, tumbling run asked for something sharp and low. Ruvan varied the run when he returned to it, and the figure she had imagined no longer fitted.

She laughed, abruptly, with annoyance and delight.

He stopped. “What?”

“You changed it.”

“Well, I wrote it.”

She looked at the shelf. Her own score was there, the purple binding faded almost white along its edge. Dema could play it magnificently. Tonight the room would watch, and at the last note Asereth would be present to take the applause. She wanted that so badly that she could feel the forward pull of the final bow.

She had imagined her father's hand coming down on the arm of his chair. She had imagined turning to find Ruvan grinning at her over the harp.

There would be applause. There would not be that evening.

“Play this tonight,” she said.

Ruvan looked at her bare feet, then at the swords she had set beside the door.

“You haven't learned it.”

“I know.”

“I don't play it the same way twice.”

“I noticed.”

He made an uncertain movement toward the old score.

“We could rehearse until the feast,” he said.

“Then I would spend the feast waiting for you to do what you had done at rehearsal.” She took up the blades. Their guards clicked together in her hand. “I want to see where that goes.”

He sat back. “I played to the end of that page after you vanished.” His hand made a small, plucking movement against his knee. “There was no one on the floor.”

Asereth set down the swords. “We can leave tonight to Dema.”

He watched her for a moment. “No. I want to try this.”

He pulled the harp toward him. “It may go quite wrong.”

“Yes.”

His grin arrived crookedly, much as she remembered it. He sounded the two low notes.

Dema listened while Asereth explained. Then she turned to Ruvan.

“You must use the large harp. The little one's bass will disappear in that hall.”

“I was going to.”

“And I want the chair by the left pillar.”

“You can sit anywhere you like.”

“From there I can see your hands.”

She helped him choose the harp. Later she played for the queen's entrance, and Asereth, waiting beyond the door, heard her take the fourth-page phrase they had rehearsed and carry it somewhere else entirely.

Tiriel came to fetch her herself.

“The chamberlain is distressed,” she said.

“At the change?”

“At having nothing to announce. He asked whether this new work has a name.”

“Not yet.”

“I told him you would say that.”

Asereth looked at her sister. The crown sat more easily now. Or perhaps Asereth had begun to see the person wearing it.

“You used to hate sitting through the long dances.”

“I hated you asking afterward whether I had counted the turns.”

“You never had.”

“I was watching you.”

Tiriel took her hand, squeezed it once, and led her into the hall.

The vigil banner had been taken down. Candlelight filled the pointed windows with reflections. Ruvan waited beside the dancing floor, the large harp resting against his shoulder. He wore his medal. Dema sat by the left pillar with her hands folded tightly in her lap.

There were faces Asereth knew among the court, and many she did not. A place had been kept for her at the high table. She saw it, and the queen's chair beside it, and the empty chair where their father would once have sat.

At the edge of the dancing floor, Asereth stopped with one heel lifted. The marble shone as it had beneath her last landing. She could not make herself put her foot down.

Ruvan rested his hands on the harp's pillar. One finger tapped against the wood, stopped, tapped again. She watched until she could feel the interval in her own breathing.

Then she lowered her heel and walked to the center.

For a moment no one seemed sure whether to applaud. She lowered her head to Ruvan instead.

He played the two low notes.

She let the right blade move first, slowly, almost level with the floor. The left rose behind her. When the upper strings flashed, she turned beneath that arm and sent the lower blade through the candlelight.

Ruvan waited.

She held the balance, one foot lifted, the swords far apart. She could hear the small unevenness of her own breath. Then the music entered again, a quick series of notes that seemed to scatter in opposite directions.

Her body chose the old fourth-page turn.

The harp went another way.

She had already committed her weight. She knew a dozen positions she could reach within one stolen heartbeat. None told her where this new music was going. She needed the next note; Ruvan had not chosen it yet.

Her foot slipped.

She folded into the slip, left knee bending nearly to the marble. The right sword swept out to clear the fall of her skirt; the left passed over her head. Someone drew a breath near the front of the room.

At the bottom of the movement, she brought the blades together.

The bright clash cut across the harp's low chord.

Ruvan looked up.

He answered the sound, finding it among the high strings.

Asereth rose into the answer. She passed the swords behind her back, changed their places, and brought them forward again. A second meeting rang lower than the first.

Ruvan echoed that too. Then, before she could decide what she would do, he gave her a short, teasing run.

She pursued it across the floor.

Her bare feet struck between his notes. Silk lifted from her knees. She made the next blade figure high and narrow, crossing her wrists above her head; the sound traveled up toward the windows. Ruvan's left hand moved, placing a dark chord beneath it, and she heard how the figure might open.

This time she did not turn where he expected.

His eyebrows rose. The next phrase broke off into a few quick plucked notes, searching. She held her arm extended, the long curve of the sword carrying the light, and waited until he found her.

Then they went on.

The speed came in fierce little flights. She leaped through one, landed low, and let the two blades open on either side of her while a single string sounded. At another, she stood almost still, turning only her wrists, drawing the candle flames into the curved steel. She let each reflection travel from hilt to point before she turned again.

Ruvan bent over the harp. Once his left hand faltered. He lifted it clear, and the right began a thin, insistent figure. Asereth answered with the tap of one foot, then the other. He laughed through his nose and put the low notes beneath her steps.

She had never heard him laugh while he was playing for the court.

They returned to the opening phrase. She recognized it this time, but he had left its end suspended. Her arms were trembling. She wanted one more passage, one more quick flight across the shining floor, and knew that wanting would not give his hands another page.

She drew the swords inward.

Ruvan saw her begin to turn. His right hand followed her, note by note, as she lowered one blade and lifted the other. The skirts settled around her ankles. She finished facing him, the two curved points almost touching above the floor.

He sounded the last chord.

In its fading she could hear a string still trembling under one crooked finger. Beyond him, Tiriel sat very straight in her chair. There was no hand descending on the arm of the chair beside her.

Ruvan lifted his hands from the harp.

Asereth lowered her blades and listened to the end.
