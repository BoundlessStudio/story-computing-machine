# The Unrepeatable Working

The tile on the hearing table had been sealed in three kinds of wax, as though it might split again out of spite.

It had already done the interesting thing. A hair-thin opening crossed the stone precisely along the line I had mapped, neat enough to admit light and too narrow for the board's smallest gauge. My working had shaped it. My power had held it. Melek had spoken the release phrase.

The chair rested one finger beside the unbroken seals. “Who cast the working?”

“I did.”

“Who said *Parting Line*?”

“Melek did, but the phrase only released my pattern.”

“Then Melek cast it?”

“No.” I heard the trap after I stepped into it. “The release was theirs. The working was mine.”

The chair looked to the two board members taking notes. Neither rescued me with a better noun.

I had brought six pages of better explanations. The brass listening tube might have resonated with a particular overtone in Melek's voice. The crowded bench might have altered the contact geometry. Their breath might have disturbed the chalk dust in a useful way. I presented all three theories, in descending order of elegance.

“Which did you test?” the chair asked.

“The room was sealed before I could test any of them.”

“So none.”

“Sealing the room does tend to impair research.”

Nobody wrote that down. The board was capable of humor but preferred it submitted in advance.

The chair turned the tile so the split caught the window light. “An unfinished working that can be released by a nearby voice would change how we safeguard every teaching room in this college. A false report could do nearly as much damage, particularly when it names a records technician as the unexplained cause.”

“I did not name Melek as the cause.”

“You named them under accidental influences.”

That phrase was mine. It looked worse when returned to me.

“By the next bell,” the chair said, “you will show the board the conditions under which this recurs. Untouched tiles. Board-kept apparatus. Predicted controls. No more than seven live holds between both shapers.”

“By the next bell I can offer a hypothesis.”

“We have six already.” The chair tapped my pages. “You may test the effect, or withdraw the incident report. If it recurs, we can investigate why. If you cannot explain why, the recurrence will remain real. If it does not recur, your explanation will not improve it.”

I disliked the distinction mostly because it was exact.

“Seven holds,” I said.

“Fewer if your hands lose precision. Melek will set their own limit.”

The chair broke none of the seals. I was dismissed with my discovery sitting on the table, grammatically ownerless.

* * *

Melek's notes were better than mine in the way records technicians' notes were always better: they contained what had happened rather than what ought to have mattered.

We stood on opposite sides of the sealed workbench while an attendant watched through the open door. Chalk marks from the accident remained under a sheet of glass. The flat brass contact lay where we had both crowded against it. The listening tube curved across the bench to a narrow horn, its shutter fixed open with sealing cord.

“You were here,” Melek said, pointing to a scuff at the bench edge. “I was beside the ledger. You turned the timing bead. Then I called.”

“Yes. The question is which acoustic property let your voice enter my working.” I slid my draft protocol toward them. “If you stand at the horn, I can separate direct sound from conducted sound.”

Melek read the first column. “You have me listed as witness.”

“You witnessed it.”

“And as vocal control.”

“That is the function I need you to perform.”

They took their hand off the listening tube. “Then your control can perform it.”

I tried reassurance first. “Nothing drew power from you. I would have felt any transfer into the pattern.”

“I didn't say it did.”

“The board has required your testimony. Participation is only the practical extension of—”

Melek closed their notebook and began wrapping its cord.

Rank, I discovered, was a tool that could be dropped on one's own foot.

“Wait.”

They did, though they did not put the notebook down.

“What have I left out?” I asked.

“You asked me to read the phrase back to you.”

“Because my hands were occupied.”

“You said, ‘When I turn the bead, say the release exactly.’ I asked whether you meant both words. You said yes. I said I would.”

I remembered the exchange. I had omitted it because it was ordinary laboratory coordination, like asking someone to move a lamp.

Melek pointed beneath the glass. “We were both touching the plate. I moved the ledger to make room. You turned the bead with your other hand.”

“Shared contact, instruction, answer, timing.” I began writing the variables in the margin. “And the tube. Present sound. Possibly breath.”

“Possibly several things. Not necessarily any one of them.”

There went my elegant overtone.

I redrew the protocol while Melek watched. The first column became **participants**. I put my name on the first line and theirs on the second. Beside each attempt I wrote who would hold the pattern, who would speak, what would be changed, and what failure should look like. A collapsed *Parting Line* left the tile whole and my fingers numb for several minutes. I wrote that down. Melek added that repeated phrases through a dry brass horn had roughened their throat after the accident. I wrote that down too.

“Three fresh calls,” they said. “I stop after any one of them if I choose. Not during an open pattern unless there is danger.”

“Three will not complete the full transfer test.”

“Then choose what you most need to know.”

I disliked that limit for the same reason I had disliked the chair's distinction. It moved uncertainty onto my side of the table.

“Three,” I said. “Between attempts, you decide again.”

Melek unwound the notebook cord.

We predicted the failures before the board could call them excuses. An echo-vial replay should not release the working if a present living speaker mattered. A live but unappointed phrase should fail if our first exchange had done more than organize us. A call after eight bead-drops should test the brief readiness I remembered holding. A shut listening path should fail if the brass carried sound rather than power. Only then would we combine the witnessed conditions.

At the bottom I wrote: **Recurrence does not identify cause.**

Melek read the sentence twice. “Keep that one.”

* * *

By the next bell, the board had turned my workroom into a place where even the dust had witnesses.

Untouched palm-sized tiles sat in a numbered rack. The board inspected the listening tube, the flat contact, the white timing bead, and the echo vial, then resealed every joint. The brass was inert: useful for marking geometry and carrying nearby sound, incapable of choosing a line or supplying a spark. I said so for the record before anyone could promote it to accomplice.

The chair pointed to the first tile. “Ordinary control.”

I set my palm over the stone and traced one short opening through its grain. Living power came up my arm, cold and familiar, whitening the mapped line beneath the surface. I held the last gap open and spoke the designated phrase myself.

“Parting Line.”

The tile opened with a small, dry click.

The chair gauged the split and recorded it. My established working functioned. I could shape it and call it alone. Nobody applauded; a control that behaved properly was expected to have the decency not to seek attention.

For the echo-vial test, I prepared a fresh tile while the vial repeated my recorded phrase into the open tube. The sound was exact, including the slight impatience with which I had made the recording. The white line under the stone trembled, then went dark. The tile remained whole.

Cold numbness filled my thumb and first two fingers.

“Stored sound failed in this arrangement,” the chair said. “Nothing more.”

I flexed my hand. “Agreed.”

For the next test I did not appoint Melek. They stood at the horn, hands clear of the plate, and waited until I turned the bead.

“Parting Line.”

Their living voice traveled cleanly through the tube. My open pattern folded shut without releasing. Whole tile. Numb fingers.

Melek made one mark beside their limit.

The late-call control belonged to the chair. We touched opposite edges of the plate. I asked the chair to speak for that pattern; the chair agreed. When I turned the bead, the eight white drops fell through their glass channel one by one. The chair waited for the ninth.

“Parting Line.”

The pattern had already begun to collapse. The fresh voice reached brass, stone, and nothing open enough to answer it.

“Late failed,” I said through my teeth.

“Under this count,” the chair corrected.

“Under this count.”

For the blocked-path control, Melek and I both touched the contact. I asked them to call for this one pattern. They said they would. At readiness I turned the bead; the chair closed the listening-tube shutter between us.

Melek spoke their second fresh phrase into the horn.

I heard only a dull blur through the shutter. The shaped line vanished from the tile. This collapse reached my wrist, leaving my hand as precise as a mitten.

The chair called a pause.

“I can continue,” I said.

Instead of answering, the chair put the smallest gauge between my fingers. I dropped it on the first attempt and held it on the second.

“One hold after the full-condition test,” the chair said. “If that succeeds, another shaper uses the last.”

Melek swallowed and took water. “One fresh call left.”

“The echo proves the brass needs living breath,” I said. “The plate may tune that breath through sympathetic—”

“No,” Melek said.

The room became very still.

They touched the two whole control tiles, not the apparatus. “A stored phrase failed. An unappointed living phrase failed. A late phrase failed. A blocked phrase failed. We haven't separated breath from attention, sound, touch, timing, or the appointment. We may not be able to.”

Every eye in the room turned to me. I could have objected to a records technician correcting my magical analysis. The evidence did not offer the same objection.

“Melek is right,” I said. The admission cost less than the numbness and felt less pleasant. “The predicted substitutions failed. The cause remains undivided.”

I placed my unreliable hand flat on the next tile. “I request the full-condition attempt.”

* * *

Melek met me at the brass contact for their third call.

“This pattern only,” I said. “One line through this tile. You speak after I turn the bead, before the eighth drop completes. You may step away now.”

“I accept this pattern,” they said.

Their fingers warmed one edge of the brass while mine marked the geometry from the other. The plate contributed no power. It merely gave our hands one exact place to be while we did what we had agreed to do.

Melek moved to the open horn. I mapped the line, fed it power, and held it unfinished. The white bead turned.

“Parting Line.”

Their words reached me through the unobstructed tube before the third drop.

The pattern closed.

The tile split along my mapped line with a sound like a fingernail drawn once across slate. Light appeared where there had been stone. My power had chosen the target and made the opening possible; no releasing sound had left my mouth. Melek's hands were empty; no shaping light had touched them. Yet without their present act, every matched control said the tile would have remained whole.

For one excellent second, nobody spoke.

Then the chair measured the split.

“You two may share an unrecorded peculiarity,” the chair said.

“The procedure succeeded exactly as predicted.”

“Once. With the original pair.”

“Changing the shaper changes the pattern, power, handling, and target discipline at the same time.”

“Yes.” The chair slid the final untouched tile forward. “That is why we change the shaper.”

I kept my annotated sequence under my palm. Methodologically, my objection was respectable. Privately, I could feel my name detaching from the only new magic in the room.

Melek set down the horn. “Without transfer, it remains a report about Dorram.”

“It is my working.”

“Then call it yourself and report nothing new.”

The chair held out a hand for my notes.

I gave them over.

The chair read my sequence without improving it, which was the first kindness the board had shown me. At the contact, the chair asked Melek to serve for one pattern under the same conditions.

Melek did not touch the brass. “My three calls are spent.”

The chair withdrew their hand at once.

The room waited. My demonstration had reached the edge of another person's throat, and no amount of standing could turn that into my property.

“If the form names both functions as participants,” Melek said, “and if this is the final call today, I will make one more.”

The chair looked toward the record sheet. “Agreed, subject to the ruling's terminology.”

“Before the attempt,” Melek said.

The chair struck out **vocal control** and wrote **participating caller**. Melek read the change, drank again, and touched the plate.

The chair's shaping entered the stone in short blue segments, unlike my continuous white trace. The line itself curved slightly around a dark inclusion I would have cut through. Different judgment, different living power, same bounded working. The chair held the incomplete pattern and turned the bead.

Melek spoke their final phrase. It came rougher now but complete.

“Parting Line.”

Blue joined blue. The second tile opened around the inclusion, exactly within the chair's mapped boundary.

I had done nothing. I had not powered, shaped, targeted, appointed, spoken, or released. My notes lay open on the table between two other living people and had worked better without me than my theory ever had.

The discovery became mine in a form I could not own alone.

* * *

The board's ruling began badly enough to be plausible.

“Dorram,” the chair dictated, “discoverer and responsible practitioner. Melek, living acoustic control.”

Melek capped the ink and pushed the unsigned witness line away.

The chair stopped dictating. “The phrase distinguishes you from the echo vial.”

“The successful working distinguishes me from it.”

“You supplied no shaping or power.”

“I have not claimed either.” Melek stood. “Write the report you prefer. Do not put my name beneath it.”

The offered wording would have repaired my standing almost perfectly. I would be the discoverer of a new method in the college records. Melek would be thanked in the apparatus section. Future readers would see one mind and one useful noise.

I looked at the chair's split tile.

“Acoustic control is false,” I said.

Melek paused but did not sit.

The chair folded their hands. “Offer a more exact classification.”

I wished, briefly and intensely, that accuracy came with its own vocabulary.

“The person holding power, pattern, target, and direction is the shaper,” I said. “The appointed person supplying the live release is the caller. They are not equal contributions in kind. They are both necessary contributions in the paired result.”

“Under the tested conjunction,” the chair said.

“Under the tested conjunction.”

“And either role may be occupied by the same practitioner, as your control showed.”

That provision restored something familiar. “Yes.”

The chair revised the ruling in wet black lines. Paired research would continue only under board supervision until its limits were better understood. Every paired test would name shaper and caller. Training and independent accreditation would still require a practitioner to shape and self-call, because the college knew how to examine one person's competence and had one afternoon of evidence for examining two.

“A caller may be essential to a paired working,” Melek said, reading over the chair's hand, “and still be absent from the definition of complete competence.”

“Complete independent competence,” the chair said.

I considered the failed tiles, Melek's rough voice, and how little we knew. “For now, that is defensible.”

Melek looked at me, not the chair. They did not agree. They also did not leave.

The final finding occupied less space than my smallest theory: a single *Parting Line* could be divided between a living shaper and a living caller under the witnessed appointment, contact, timing, and sound-path conditions. Stored sound and the tested broken conditions had failed. A second shaper had repeated the result. Why the conjunction worked, whether every element was necessary, and how far it extended remained unresolved.

The board accepted the new effect without pretending to understand it. Institutions managed courage best when it could be written as caution.

* * *

In the archive, an attendant boxed the two split tiles beside the four whole failures. The echo vial went into a padded compartment. The timing bead went into another. Last came the flat brass contact, cooled now that no living hand touched it.

Melek and I reviewed the final form at a narrow desk. Where the old form had offered one line for **practitioner**, the new one had two ruled columns.

I signed under **shaper**.

Melek inspected every page before uncapping the ink.

“I should have recorded the appointment in my first report,” I said.

“Yes.”

“I treated your answer as laboratory furniture.”

“You treated it as something that could matter only after you explained it.”

That was worse, being more precise.

“I was wrong.”

Melek signed under **caller**. “The record is right now.”

It was not forgiveness. It was something the archive could use.

The cause line still contained my overtone hypothesis, cramped into the available space with two qualifications and an arrow into the margin. I crossed it out. Underneath, I wrote **unresolved** and left the rest blank.

At the bottom of the form, the college had already pressed its separate seal for independent competence: shape and self-call. I noticed it. I let it stand.

The attendant closed the box. Through its glass lid, the brass contact lay dull between two living names, while the line that should have explained us remained open.
