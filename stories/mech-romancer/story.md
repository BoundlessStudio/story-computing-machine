title: An Engagement at Sundown

"No, buddy, you've gotten your genres mixed up. I can't raise the dead, I'm a Mech Romancer."

Sir Talven looked at my trade card. Then he looked at the six skulls he had arranged around us.

"The man at the inn said you could put the fire back into old soldiers."

"Usually over dinner."

Beyond the cemetery, the town was shutting its gates. People ran beneath the portcullis with bundles and indignant chickens. Across the valley, something taller than the church had reached the edge of the enemy camp.

Talven swallowed. "Can you seduce that?"

"I introduce lonely mechs to other lonely mechs. What happens after that is their business."

"We haven't got another mech."

Something enormous cleared its throat above us.

Talven shut his eyes.

I turned. What I had taken for an equestrian monument without the horse stood beside the cemetery wall, one hand resting on the hilt of a sword. Afternoon light caught its brass crest and faded red plume. Pigeons had reached everywhere else.

"We haven't got another *siege* mech," Talven amended.

"Magnificence," said the monument.

"Bessa Nix. How do you do?"

"Poorly. He has been setting those skulls out for forty minutes."

Talven had been sent to find reinforcements. He was a junior knight, and his marshal had included the dead in the instructions largely to impress upon him how little choice he had.

Magnificence, Talven explained, had been the finest parade champion in the western kingdoms. He had retired here fifty years ago. His guns had gone to a museum. His sword was ceremonial.

"It cuts," Magnificence said.

"Of course," said Talven.

"Quite well."

A file of mounted archers hurried past us toward the gate. Their gray-haired sergeant slowed to salute Magnificence. Magnificence lifted one silver hand in return.

Across the valley, the enemy mech bent to hitch a string of gun carriages to her towing harness. She had shoulders like a pair of fortified barns. Magnificence watched her for a moment, then looked down at his sword.

Beside the gun carriages stood a round, ribbed object on a thick metal stem.

"New kind of mortar," Talven said, following my gaze. "The scouts don't know what it fires."

I opened my book.

"Height?"

"Ninety feet," Talven said promptly.

"Eighty-two feet, six inches," said Magnificence. "And that includes the crest. If you must exaggerate, do it about my fencing."

"Interests?"

"The defense of this town," Talven said.

I closed the book on his fingers.

"His interests."

Magnificence looked at me properly. His eyes were two small amber lamps behind a narrow silver visor.

"Music," he said. "Good tailoring. Dancing."

"Dancing?" Talven said.

"I led the coronation procession for three kings. Fifty years later, people still ask me to demonstrate a marching turn. They put a brass band in front of me. I turn left. They applaud."

"What would you rather do?"

He took his hand from the sword. Beneath the pigeons, his fingers were beautifully jointed.

"Dance with somebody who could turn me."

I looked across the valley again.

"Have you met her?"

"No. New commission. I am told she is formidable."

"Would you like me to find out anything else?"

He brushed a pigeon from his shoulder with one precise finger.

"You might ask whether she likes music."

Talven made a noise suggesting that he had paid a great deal of money for an extremely unusual form of surrender.

"Lend me your horse," I told him.

* * *

The white handkerchief I carried was embroidered with entwined initials. It had survived three broken engagements and was very good at negotiating a temporary peace.

The enemy commander let me through to Tenderness because I said I had come to discuss an engagement. He wanted to know whether Magnificence was as quick as his reputation suggested.

"I'll ask," I said.

Tenderness was sitting on the slope behind the siege train. She had two massive walking legs and, unfolded behind them, two additional bracing feet: wide iron spades planted in the earth. Her round waist joint supported a broad torso and two arms ending in formidable metal hands. Gun smoke had blackened her blue paint.

She was hammering something with the butt of a spare axle.

I had seen the object from the cemetery. Talven had called it a new kind of mortar.

It was a flower.

The petals were beaten from shield plates, curled at the edges and gathered around a center of little bronze nuts. It was larger than the inn where I had spent the night.

"A poppy?" I asked.

"Peony."

"Of course."

She put the axle down.

"It's not finished."

"It's lovely."

"The middle's wrong. Real ones are messy. This keeps coming out like a turbine."

She had a low, blunt voice. I liked her immediately.

"Who's it for?"

"Nobody."

Behind her, men were stacking shot beside the guns. A carpenter tested the wheels of a ladder wagon. At the camp's edge, the commander had a map spread over a drum. This was not a misunderstanding that could be cleared up by identifying a flower.

"Do you have to attack the town?" I asked.

"At sundown. Unless the commander gets his engagement."

"He's rather misunderstood that."

I showed her my trade card.

Tenderness read it. She looked toward the town.

"Oh."

"He's eighty-two feet, six inches. Including the crest."

"He's very shiny."

"He's very aware of it."

Her shoulders made a small, complicated movement.

"I don't think I'd suit."

"He asked me to find out whether you liked music."

"Everyone likes music."

"My last client threw an orchestra into a haystack."

"Was it playing?"

"Not afterward."

Tenderness gave a short laugh. A flock of rooks rose from the slope behind her.

"I like the quick ones," she said. "With the fiddles."

"And dancing?"

She looked down at her feet.

"I march."

"That's not what I asked."

"Look at the knees on me. I can't do that crossing-over thing. I'd knock him flat."

I walked around one of her planted spades. Its hinge allowed it to fold against her calf for walking. Above it, the circular joint at her waist was scratched all the way around.

"Can you turn on that?"

She twisted. Her torso went smoothly through half a circle while the bracing feet stayed planted. She looked down at me over what had previously been her back.

"Either way," she said.

I thought of Magnificence's precise fingers, his perpetual marching turn.

"I'd like to describe you to him. Truthfully. Including the knees."

"And the feet."

"Especially the feet."

She picked up the peony. Her great thumb adjusted a petal by less than an inch.

"Would he laugh?"

"I don't know him well enough to promise he won't. I do know he's asked me for something he hasn't had in fifty years. He's taking a chance too."

Tenderness considered the flower.

"All right," she said. "But tell him I made this. Don't let him think I bought it."

I rode back with two hours to sundown and a list of practical requirements. Talven met me outside the gates.

"Well?"

"She likes fast music. She makes sculptures. She is twice his weight, has four feet when she wants them, and thinks he'll laugh at her."

Magnificence leaned over the cemetery wall.

"Why?"

"Because she doesn't have parade-champion knees."

His visor tipped toward the enemy camp.

"I have been dancing with parade-champion knees for a century. Tell her I'd welcome a change."

"You can tell her yourself."

Talven caught my bridle before I could turn away.

"Does she know this is meant to stop the attack?"

"She knows I'm arranging a date. So does he. Neither has promised to like the other."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I imagine we'll both wish you'd hired the other sort of romancer."

He stood still a moment. Above him, Magnificence was trying to polish his own crest with a handkerchief too small to see.

Talven let go of the bridle.

"What do you need?"

* * *

There was an old tournament ground on the valley floor. It was broad, level, and empty except for a reviewing stand and two armies who would have preferred something more straightforward.

The marshal objected to keeping his men outside the lists.

"Would you like to stand under the competitors?" I asked.

He reconsidered.

The enemy commander objected to the prohibition on weapons.

"For an initial meeting?" I said. "Good heavens."

He said he had never heard of such terms.

"How many engagements have you arranged?"

He allowed that his experience was chiefly of sieges.

"Different specialty," I said.

They agreed that the two mechs would meet alone at the bell, and that nobody would interfere. I made each commander say that part where the other could hear it. The marshal studied Magnificence's slender ankles. The enemy commander studied Tenderness's shoulders. Both looked satisfied.

Talven looked as though he might be sick.

He had, however, found six musicians willing to play behind a stone wall. We put them there. He borrowed a horse cart full of red ribbon from a festival committee. We used all of it marking the edges of the ground.

The reviewing stand had to go. Tenderness carried it to the hillside, with the enemy commander still on it.

"Better view," she told him.

Magnificence made three attempts to stand casually beside the entrance. On the fourth, he nearly leaned on the town's entire cavalry.

I waved them farther back.

"You're good at this," Talven said.

"Last spring I arranged a picnic for a tunneling mech and an observatory. This is practically indoors."

The bell rang.

Magnificence walked into the lists. He had removed his sword. Someone had climbed a ladder to tie a red sash below his crest. It streamed behind him in the evening wind.

Tenderness came from the other side, carrying the peony in both hands.

The armies cheered. They thought it was a mace.

Magnificence stopped in front of her. Tenderness stopped farther away than she needed to.

"I made you something," she said.

She held it out at arm's length.

He took it carefully. His thumb found a curled steel petal.

"You made this edge?"

"With an axle."

"It's exquisite."

"The middle's a bit—"

"You've turned every one of those little bronze nuts a different way."

"Took ages."

"I like the middle."

For the first time that day, Tenderness had nothing to say.

On the hillside, the enemy commander cupped his hands around his mouth.

"Stop giving him weapons!"

Magnificence turned the flower in his hands.

"I haven't anywhere to put it."

"You can put it down."

He looked at the dusty ground.

"Will you hold it while I spread something?"

Together they laid his red sash on the earth and set the flower upon it. It took them a little while to get it properly balanced. Nobody cheered during this part.

Behind the stone wall, the fiddlers began.

"You had better begin too," I called, "before they lose their nerve."

Magnificence bowed. It was a wonderful bow, smooth and deep, one hand behind his back.

Tenderness tried to copy it. Something clanged inside her left knee.

"Oh, blast."

"That doesn't matter."

"Easy for you to say. You've got very good knees."

"Thank you."

They took hands. Magnificence began a step. Tenderness attempted the corresponding one. His foot was already where hers needed to go.

He stopped at once.

"Sorry," she said.

"My fault."

"It wasn't."

The marshal shouted something about getting around her flank.

Magnificence looked down at Tenderness's feet. Then at the two bracing spades folded behind them.

"Show me how you like to turn."

"Not much of a dance."

"I'd rather see it than discuss it."

She lowered her braces. The iron edges sank into the turf. Holding his hands, she turned her upper body to the left.

Magnificence stepped with her. She stopped. He took another step to keep his balance, then looked back at the curve his feet had made.

"Again?" he said.

She turned farther. He crossed one foot behind the other and came smoothly around her planted legs.

"Like that?"

"I think so."

"Then lead."

Tenderness gave him a sudden quarter-turn. His red crest feathers swung out. The fiddlers caught the rhythm and played faster.

He laughed.

She went the other way.

He followed her, silver feet slipping between the marks left by her spades. When she came to the end of a turn, he bent one knee and swept low beneath her arm. She tipped her great blue head to watch him come out on the other side.

"Oh," she said. "You can do that."

"I can."

"What about this?"

The next turn carried him right off the ground.

Only a few inches. Enough that both armies went silent.

Magnificence landed laughing, and this time he turned her. She retracted one brace, pivoted on the other, and set it down farther along the curve. A broad new arc appeared in the grass.

They had begun to travel.

"Give them room!" I shouted.

Talven rode along the ribbon, waving the cavalry back. Nobody argued. The peony sat in its patch of red silk, and around it the two giants circled, trying things.

* * *

The enemy commander lost patience first.

"Tenderness! Fall in!"

She glanced toward the hillside. Her next step faltered.

Magnificence felt it. He stopped.

"I can let go," he said.

She looked at their joined hands.

"Don't," she said.

The marshal rode into the lists with four guards. On the opposite side, the enemy commander was scrambling down from the reviewing stand. He pointed to the siege train.

Men ran toward the guns.

Talven pulled up beside his marshal.

"Sir. You agreed. No interference."

"I agreed to single combat!"

"So did I!" shouted the enemy commander.

"And I arrange introductions," I shouted back. "We've all brought different books."

The first gun carriage began to turn. Its black muzzle lifted toward Magnificence.

Tenderness looked at it. Then she looked at Magnificence.

"There's something I need to do."

"May I come?"

"Mind the wheels."

She folded her braces and took his arm. They walked together toward the siege train.

I ran ahead, waving both hands.

"Away from the guns! All of you! This is not a good place to stand!"

Talven put his horse between the gunners and their carriages. The men looked past him at Tenderness approaching without the towing harness they had strapped on her that morning. They left the guns.

As Tenderness bent over the first gun, the marshal wheeled toward his horse archers.

"Loose on the blue one!"

Magnificence turned his head.

"Marshal," he said.

The gray-haired sergeant's bow dipped. He looked up at Magnificence, then lowered it altogether. The men beside him eased their strings.

"I am taking a lady home."

Tenderness leaned toward him.

"Which home?"

He looked at her. She looked back.

"Ah," he said.

She laughed, a great low clangor that rang across the valley.

She took the front of the first carriage. Magnificence took the back.

"Ready?" she said.

"Your lead."

They lifted it together, turned, and stood the great siege gun on its muzzle in the soft earth.

Tenderness gave it a little pat.

They did the same to the next one. Then the next. Magnificence learned to catch the carriage as she raised it; by the fourth, the turn was almost part of the music. Four enormous bronze guns stood nose-down along the ridge, their useless wheels revolving gently in the air.

The enemy commander made a sound I could hear from two hundred yards away.

On the far side of the ridge, an officer began turning the ladder wagons away from the town. Without their guns or their mech, his men had no business approaching its walls. The retreat spread along the column, untidy and quick.

The marshal watched them go. He took off his helmet. Put it on again.

He did not repeat the order.

Magnificence offered Tenderness his arm once more.

"I should like to continue this," he said. "Somewhere less crowded."

"Yes," she said. "So should I."

They went back for the flower.

Talven rode up beside me. His helmet was crooked; a length of red ribbon had caught around his spur.

"Is that it?"

"For tonight."

"What do I tell the marshal?"

"That he owes me half my fee. I'll send the other commander his share."

Talven looked at the four upended guns.

"He may not think that's fair."

"He's received half a battle at considerably less than half the usual cost. I'll put that on the invoice."

Across the tournament ground, Tenderness picked up the peony. Magnificence shook the dust from his sash and draped it over his arm.

"Tomorrow?" he asked her.

"I can do tomorrow."

"Somewhere level. With room."

"I know a place."

He took the flower from her. Then they stepped over the fallen ribbon and set off through the evening, her hand resting in the crook of his bright, slightly dented arm.
