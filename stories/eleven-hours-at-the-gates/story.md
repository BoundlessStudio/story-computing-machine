# The Weight of the Gate

The invasion began at dawn. One of the farm towers went dark, then another, and in the light between them the first ranks of machines came walking into the basin. The watch called down to the armory with their speed and the hour they would reach the gates. Eleven hours exactly. They had the length of the enemy's stride to a finger's width by now.

Neris had her cheek against a cold breastplate and a hand full of grease somewhere the old suit used to have a hip. She asked the watch to send her a piece of the scout they'd brought down the evening before. By the time it arrived, she'd freed the hip enough to turn it. The chalk mark she made on the joint was the first evidence in forty years that the armor could be persuaded to stand again.

The scout's plating came up in the apron of a man who'd been driving wagons since midnight. He dumped it on the scarred bench, shook out his apron, and stood there yawning while Neris tried the suit's arc gun. It made a dark little flower on the plating. He touched it with the tip of a finger.

“That's cooled already,” he said.

“I know.”

She left the gun on the bench. Through the cracked upper panes she could see the farm wagons crossing the stone road into Cairn End. It came straight out of the earth on the far side of the cleft, but on this side it rested on a hinge. When she was small her foster father would point out the ironwork and describe how the road used to go up like the lid of a box. It had stood between the old gates and the army for nine days. On the tenth day a blow split the drive and the road came down. Neris's mother, Linet Tann, had died defending the gates on that day. The city had paid for a likeness of her in clean white armor, with a red stripe so fresh it looked wet in rain. The actual stripe went under an ugly buckle where the suit had been struck.

The farm wagons came over with people piled as high as hay. Every other human stronghold had fallen by winter. There was nowhere for the wagons to go after Cairn End.

When the suit went down, the blow had jammed it with one leg folded and the right hand curled into the chest. There were still pencil notes on the armory wall from people who'd tried to get it to stand. Now and then someone had got it enough power to ask for a pilot. The little lamp on its collar had always answered the same way, with a flat red bar. Neris had grown up watching people swat that lamp with the heel of a hand.

By the third hour the legs could move on the rig. The suit hung with its head off under a pair of long yellow lamps. Neris lifted the helmet onto a fold of clean cloth and pulled the diagnostic lead across her bench. Her hair was coming out of its knot and her glasses kept sliding down her nose. She pushed them back with her wrist and plugged the lead in.

“Neris,” said the helmet. “Daughter.”

Her hand stayed on the plug. The terminal went on tracing a steady cyan pulse.

“Who is this?”

“Stave.”

She had seen the name on the suit's old service slips under INTELLIGENCE. The armory never stocked anything for it. All its entries were dates and a dash.

“I have you on the pilot channel,” she said.

“Yes. It's the one you used to like.”

She could hear small noises around the words, about the measure of a breath. The armor stood headless in the light. Behind it, the farmhouse bell the gate crew had borrowed for the evacuation rang three times. The drivers would be making another turn across the road.

“I can bring it all up now,” she said. “I'm going to have to get inside.”

A flat red bar came on at the collar.

“What is it you think is going to happen to me?”

“Your mother used to say we ought to go home at the same hour every night so you'd learn to go to sleep. You learned to wait for us in the dark. We walked you between the shutters until you fell asleep. She used to nod off, too. I'd take the weight of you both for a while.”

Neris sat on the corner of the bench. As a girl she'd counted the birthdays her mother had missed and had never known how many of the days before them the woman had been home for. It had occurred to her to ask for the dates of the old battle and the disposition of the armor. She'd never asked anybody who had put her to bed.

“One Shoe,” Stave said.

Neris looked at the helmet. That had been embroidered in little blue letters on the lining of the wool coat she'd come to her foster father's in. He hadn't known what it meant, either.

“The right one,” said Stave. “Always. You'd work that foot at me till I took it off.”

She picked up the cloth and wiped one side of the visor. Cleaned, it was the blue of a gas flame. She had spent half her working life getting an expression out of a needle or a motor bearing. The helmet gave her nothing she knew how to read.

“There’s work I can do from here,” she said. “Will you move if I ask you?”

“Within the shop.”

She plugged the helmet into the collar and helped Stave set its feet down. The left boot sank a little in an old crack in the floor. It took them two hours to get the boot to take any weight. When it finally did, Neris heard a tick down in the stone, and the iron shelving on the other side of the shop shivered.

She had read about the weight field in the old books. With both feet on sound stone, the suit could spread a load into the whole ground underneath it. The books showed a pilot holding a shattered ceiling while crews went in and out. Neris had read the measurements and imagined a printer getting a decimal wrong.

“Put a hand under this edge,” she said.

Stave got the fingers of one glove into the crack. For a moment the floor of the shop lay quite still. Then the edge of the slab came up and the grease that had been running into the crack began to run the other way.

“I felt that at my shoulder,” Stave said. “I'd forgotten the shoulder.”

Neris took three steps toward the window. She could see a length of the road from there and at its city end the grate in the wall where the hoist had been. She went on looking at the grate till the edge of the raised slab settled behind her.

“How far from here to the old hoist cradle?” she said.

“Down a stair. Why?”

“The road is balanced on that hinge. The rest of it comes up if we can get it started.”

“We?”

She pulled up a drawing of the gate on the terminal. Below the hinge was the empty cradle, which she had last seen full of masonry dust, with bedrock underfoot and a maintenance stair up to the city. She swung the drawing until she could look up at the underside of the road. Two stone sockets still marked where the old hoist had pressed against it. They were just wide enough for a pair of gloves.

“Without a pilot,” said Stave, “I can lift something till it starts to turn in my hands. Then I have to put it down. I can keep a field even across the shoulders. I need a pilot for the hands. If you lose either grip under load, the field collapses.”

“Show me in here.”

For a time there was only the noise of the fan on the terminal. She thought she heard the watch counting the march on its open line. Then came a click from the breastplate. The seams opened to show a lining the pale brown of an old boot.

“The test circuit ends at the shop door,” said Stave. “I can take you out of any move you make.”

Neris took off her glasses and put them in her shirt pocket. She backed into the suit. As it closed she felt a rough place in the lining just beneath her left ear where Linet's hair must have rubbed it. She'd come into the armory barefoot in the summers when the old men were out and stood under the hanging suit to see whether she could touch its knees.

“Whose movement is this?” she said when her right arm lifted.

“Yours. You moved your little finger.”

“I've got a twitch there.”

“I can feel it.”

The helmet made her voice immediate, as though she were talking to someone with their head right next to hers. As Neris walked toward a slab they'd once used as the footing for a rolling mill, Stave shifted her over each foot in turn. She could scarcely tell when it moved her. It was like walking with someone who knew how to carry the other end of a table.

At the slab, Neris set both hands under the edge. The field came on with a buzzing she felt in the fillings of her teeth. The slab lifted far enough for her to see the pale underside. Then something crumbled under her right toe. She got the other foot forward and began to find her balance. She was still finding it when the suit seized up around her. Her hands came away. Stave took a long backward stride and the slab came down with a noise that emptied the hanging tool rack.

“Open,” Neris said.

It opened. She stood with her hand against the breastplate, trying to get enough air to speak.

“I had a foot down. You would have felt it in another second.”

“I felt the slab turning.”

“You could have held me while I got the other arm under.”

The light in the visor went and came back. In the quiet she could hear the men at the gates beating something into place, with a hammer large enough that they hit twice and rested.

“There was a slit for the chains in the old gate,” Stave said. “We went back and found you in the watchroom. Linet was hurt so badly by then I had to take every step for her. She got you up the last of the way with her good hand. Then her hand went slack and you started sliding back into the glove. I pushed you up. There were hands at the slit and they took you.”

Neris thought of the bent right fingers, curled against the blow. She had worked them apart one at a time.

“I kept my hands up after that,” Stave said. “I thought they'd reach in again for her.”

Outside the shop a man shouted for someone to get the last wagon through. Neris went to the window. The wagon bed was bare all except for a woman holding two empty chicken baskets on her lap. It crossed above a pale greenness that stretched along the floor of the basin. The winter grain was three weeks from cutting. From the window she could still see patches of it rise and flatten in the wind.

The watch called two hours.

Neris got the arc gun off the bench and set it on the scrap scale. She brought over the other weapons from the rack behind the suit. Without them there was room in the seat of the shoulder for the full travel of the arm. She had spent all morning freeing metal so those guns could turn.

“We can stand in the cradle,” she said. “My fingers in the old hoist sockets. If it's too much, you'll feel it first. Tell me. Give me a chance to get my feet under me, and I'll tell you when to let go.”

“They'll be right above us.”

“I can see that from here.”

Stave's gaze followed hers to the road. The gate crew had walked out as far as the hinge to furl a flag that had hung from the wall since Linet's battle. Wind from the cleft made the faded red cloth stiff in their hands. The woman from the wagon had been let through already. She stood in the shadow of the gate with the baskets and called up to them that it would tear if they pulled it like that.

The red bar went out at Stave's collar. Neris was used to the click from before. This time the suit took a step and set both heels down in front of her. When it opened, the cradle light was green.

“Take us down the stair,” Stave said.

The gate crew had to clear a way through the cradle. By the time they called up that it was ready, the watch had stopped counting in minutes.

At the appointed hour they heard the front of the army reach the gates. A succession of blows went from one side of the wall to the other. Dust dropped in thin curtains into the hoist cradle. There was a view of the far lip of the cleft through the gap beside the hinge. Sand came down in little streams as row after row of feet knocked it loose. Stave pointed the helmet up, and Neris saw the road was shaking.

The hoist had been a great iron thing. Its mounting holes were spaced wider apart than she could reach without feeling the lining drag at her back. Neris put the heels of the suit's hands into the two sockets left in the road. Above her something made a sound as sweet and insistent as a spoon skipping on a dinner plate. She heard the watch yell for everyone to get off the gate platform. Another blow came down. Through the stone into her hands she felt the machines run.

“Sound stone,” Stave said.

Neris tipped her head back and pushed. The field buzzed in her teeth as the load found the rock beneath her. Pressure drew her shoulder blades toward each other. She bore on it until a strip of light appeared under the far end of the road. The whole basin was there for a moment. She could see it between the road and the brink, down past shelves of stone to the strip of grain furred green above the dark earth. A machine running to make the road hung from the raised lip till the next one hit it. Both went down into the cleft in the sun.

There was a blow so close that Neris knew it had broken the gate. The left socket jumped. The heel of her hand came free and she caught its lip with two fingers. She heard the first of Stave's retreat in the whine of the hip she had freed at dawn.

“Hold,” she said.

All the weight came onto her fingers. For an instant the field pulsed in her teeth. She felt the armor gather itself against the pull. Stave left the other hand where it was and took her weight, the way it had all those nights when there had been only a child to carry. Neris could move the slipped hand just enough. She got a third finger in, then the heel of the glove. She felt Stave feel it, and the two of them rose.

The far end of the road went up over the last light. The machines came sliding down toward the gate in a single dark layer, then peeled away at the edges where the stone was too steep for their legs. The road's upper end struck the wall. For a moment it rang, and afterward the hinges tore and the road settled between the stone shoulders on either side of the gate. Pieces tumbled into the cleft so slowly she could follow them with her eyes. Far off, where the road had used to lie, the approaching machines drew even with the brink and stopped. Their backs went on moving. They were turning to make room for something farther down the column. The whole length of the cleft lay between them now.

In the cradle the whole front of the suit was gray with dust. Neris could see the ridge of each of its knuckles sticking out of the sockets. The road held of its own weight.

“Ready?” said Stave.

She let her fingers uncurl. The suit let its hands down.

The stair was choked with fallen stone. Far above, between the road and the wall, there was just room to see someone lie down and look in. The gate crew had brought ropes. A voice called down to ask how many were alive.

Neris put her hand flat to the inside of the breastplate.

“Two,” she said.
