# Promised experience

The request supplies a speaker's courteous offer of a consequential choice: “civilized folk, or uncivilized,” with a stated preference for the former. It leaves genre, scale, identities, relationship, sincerity, and outcome open. My inference is that the pleasure lies in controlled menace and the eventual discovery of what the speaker can do; dry humor is available, not required. The offered choice should shape the encounter and its payoff rather than merely decorate a different plot.

# Provisional direction — Room Enough

A brisk fantasy confrontation, told close to Breska, a giant mercenary captain, in Lord Haverel's low-beamed feasting hall. Her captured company is held on the musicians' gallery. She comes to ransom them. Haverel wants a giant for his next campaign and will release the company only if she enters his service. The central relationship is between two practiced fighters negotiating from incompatible kinds of strength; this is the writer's choice, not a requested relationship. Breska prefers terms because battles make frightened people do irreversible things, including people she would rather bring home alive. That preference will remain credible even when her restraint ends.

1. Breska must crouch to enter the hall. She sees her company above, crossbows guarding the gallery stairs, a long trestle table between the prisoners and the lord, and roof beams too low for her long sword. Haverel has arranged the meeting in a room built to make her size a disadvantage. She notices the deliberate arrangement before he speaks. Her people surrendered after losing a battle; there is no supposedly trustworthy prior promise for her to ignore.
2. He refuses the ordinary ransom she brought and offers the exchange he really wants: their liberty for her service. Breska delivers the user's line verbatim. Her civilized proposal is single combat to submission, witnessed by the hall: if she wins, all her people leave with her; if he wins, she serves for a year and her people still go free. His alternative is to keep them and face her attempt to take them back. Haverel is an excellent swordsman, and the confined hall favors him. He accepts the prospect of a valuable victory without a costly fight against the whole giant. Both explicitly accept that a combatant who yields is no longer to be struck.
3. The duel delivers a genuine contest. Breska must use a short blade, move on bent knees, and meet a quicker opponent who knows his floor. Her size helps and hurts; she takes a non-graphic wound. She wins by recognizing his habitual retreat around the trestle table and using its end to close his escape, finally disarming and holding him. This is earned observation and fighting skill, not a new magical ability. He yields where everyone can hear him.
4. When Breska releases him, Haverel orders the gallery guards to shoot her. She has already watched their positions. She tips the long table up to intercept the first bolts and drives it across the hall, forcing the armed men at the stair foot back. The action makes the difference between a bounded duel and an uncontrolled fight tangible. She tears down a beam where it blocks her movement, breaks the crossbow line and reaches the gallery, freeing her company. No mass killing or surprise power is needed. Her people act in their own escape, helping one another out of their bonds and guarding the stairs once reached.
5. Haverel tries to rally his remaining men from behind them. Breska brings the fight back to him; now she can stand in the space she has broken open. His men can see their lord broke his own surrender and that she has spared men who put down their weapons. They choose to give ground rather than protect his last gambit. He asks whether he can surrender again. She allows it, making her original preference a continuing choice with a real cost: she forgoes revenge and takes her people home. She does not require an apology or convert him. The closing physical image is her company passing through the hall's ruined entrance together, with Breska last and able to stand upright.

The strongest possibility is the change in the room itself: a place arranged to constrain a courteous giant becomes evidence of what her restraint had spared. The largest risk is an over-neat second victory after the duel. The betrayal and rescue should therefore accelerate into a single short, dangerous release, with a cost carried by Breska's wound and the destruction, rather than a second full contest or a lesson speech. The final mercy must not imply that Haverel has earned trust; the prisoners leave under their own protection.
