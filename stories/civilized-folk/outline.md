# Promised experience

The request supplies a speaker's courteous offer of a consequential choice: “civilized folk, or uncivilized,” with a stated preference for the former. It leaves genre, scale, identities, relationship, sincerity, and outcome open. My inference is that the pleasure lies in controlled menace and the eventual discovery of what the speaker can do; dry humor is available, not required. The offered choice should shape the encounter and its payoff rather than merely decorate a different plot.

# Provisional direction — Room Enough

A brisk fantasy confrontation, told close to Breska, a giant mercenary captain, in Lord Haverel's feasting hall. A low painted timber ceiling hangs beneath its older stone vault. Her captured company is held on the musicians' gallery at one end. She comes to ransom them. Haverel wants a giant for his next campaign and will release the company only if she enters his service. The central relationship is between two practiced fighters negotiating from incompatible kinds of strength; this is the writer's choice, not a requested relationship. Breska prefers terms because battles make frightened people do irreversible things, including people she would rather bring home alive. That preference will remain credible even when her restraint ends.

1. Breska must crouch to enter the hall. She sees her company above, crossbows guarding the gallery stairs, a long trestle table between the prisoners and the lord, and a timber ceiling too low for her to stand or swing her long sword. The exposed stone vault above the gallery establishes that the painted ceiling over the feasting floor is a suspended furnishing, not the roof's support. Haverel has chosen the room and arranged the meeting to make her size a disadvantage. She notices this before he speaks. Her people surrendered after losing a battle; there is no supposedly trustworthy prior promise for her to ignore.
2. He refuses the ordinary ransom she brought and offers the exchange he really wants: their liberty for her service. Breska delivers the user's line verbatim. Her civilized proposal is single combat to submission, witnessed by the hall: if she wins, all her people leave with her; if he wins, she serves for a year and her people still go free. His alternative is to keep them and face her attempt to take them back. Haverel is an excellent swordsman, and the confined hall favors him. He accepts the prospect of a valuable victory without a costly fight against the whole giant. Both explicitly accept that a combatant who yields is no longer to be struck.
3. The duel delivers a genuine contest. Breska must use a short blade, move on bent knees, and meet a quicker opponent who knows his floor. Her size helps and hurts; she takes a non-graphic wound. The prospect of yielding briefly becomes real, along with its cost: a year of her remaining life serving this man's conquests, rather than choosing where to go and whom to fight for. Her freedom matters independently of the company, whose guaranteed release makes the bargain worth this risk. She wins by recognizing his habitual retreat around the trestle table and using its end to close his escape, finally disarming and holding him. This is earned observation and fighting skill, not a new magical ability. He yields where everyone can hear him.
4. When Breska releases him, Haverel orders the gallery guards to shoot her. She has already watched their positions. She tips the long table up to intercept the first bolts and drives it across the hall, forcing the armed men at the stair foot back. She breaks through the painted ceiling over the empty middle of the floor, scattering its panels and opening space to stand beneath the intact stone vault. The ceiling does not carry the gallery; her captives are outside the fall. With room to move she breaks the crossbow line and reaches the gallery, freeing her company. The rescue is brief in elapsed time but receives the climax's fullest physical attention: the room, weapons and distances stop protecting Haverel's side. Its consequences must feel larger and less controllable than the duel, while Breska still discriminates between active opponents and men who drop their weapons. Her people help one another out of their bonds, take the guards' weapons and secure the stair and exit. No mass killing or surprise power is needed.
5. Haverel tries to rally his remaining men from behind them. Breska intercepts him, takes his remaining weapon and physically holds him; the crossbows have been disabled or seized, and her armed company controls its exit. The remaining guards' withdrawal confirms this achieved victory rather than supplying it through a sudden moral conversion. He asks whether he can surrender again. She allows it, making her original preference a continuing choice with a real cost: she forgoes revenge and takes her people home. She does not require an apology or convert him. The closing physical image is Breska standing at full height under the exposed vault while her company passes out under its own protection. The stone entrance remains intact, so she must duck once more to leave; no unexplained ruined doorway appears.

The strongest possibility is the change in the room itself: a place arranged to constrain a courteous giant becomes evidence of what her restraint had spared. The largest risk is an over-neat second victory after the duel. The betrayal and rescue should accelerate into the decisive escalation, substantial on the page and carrying the cost of Breska's wound and the destruction, rather than becoming either a second formal contest or a summary of power. The final mercy must not imply that Haverel has earned trust; the prisoners leave under their own protection. These mechanics and proportions remain provisional choices to test in prose.
