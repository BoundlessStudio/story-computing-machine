# Outline

## Story

- Premise and central promise: During an overnight watch, an adult grandchild discovers three shabby local gods correcting the consequences of 108-year-old Grandma Valka's cigarettes, fried food, bad sleep, and cheerful risks. Decades ago, Valka beat them at a threshold card game and left divine debts uncollected; they keep her alive rather than face her return.
- Focal pressure or attachment: The narrator loves Valka's appetite for life but turns visits into supervision. Proof she is safe becomes a temptation to control her. Beneath the scolding is a request they cannot make cleanly: do not leave me yet.
- Counterforce or complication: The gods want Valka alive and less troublesome, but disagree, cheat, and cannot command her. Valka wants company without management. Her habits remain harmful; divine patches are finite, imperfect interventions, not immunity.
- POV, distance, and information limit: First-person past through the grandchild. Anxiety makes miracles noticeable and ordinary evidence suspect. The gods give contradictory accounts; Valka's recognition of one card confirms the wager but not what she intends to collect.
- Governing movement and time shape: A reverse haunting over one rainy evening and dawn. Small corrections lead from bedroom window to back step, pantry, and a crowded kitchen. Contract after Valka enters; end with private leverage, not public revelation or divine reform.
- Speculative rule or ordinary-world constraint: Worship, domain power, and local authority sustain this pantheon. Its threshold cardroom precedes the universal afterlife but is not that afterlife. A freely accepted wager binds these gods; Valka's interrupted death suspended her right to name their forfeits until she returns. Within separate domains they can divert an accident or hasten ordinary repair, but cannot erase aging, reverse death, compel choice, or guarantee her future.
- Dialogue promise: Domestic scolding and grandmotherly misdirection meet divine whisper-fights, bad alibis, wounded vanity, and the comedy of immortals with less social authority than Valka. Affection coexists with irritation; mortality grants nobody eloquence.
- Dialogic medium: Speech, overheard whispers, failed denials, selective silence, protective gestures, and a marked playing card that becomes evidence and withheld communication.
- Dialogue engine: Questioning each god assembles why Valka survives, while their wish to recruit the narrator exposes protection's limits. When Valka joins them, what she recognizes but will not explain changes who may bargain, supervise, or ask directly.

## Voice

- Narrative texture: Brisk, affectionate first-person observation inventories bodily risks and domestic evidence, then admits impossible corrections without fanfare. Make anxious causal logic funny, never contemptuous of age.
- Conversational texture: Use interruptions, half-answers, practical warnings, whispered quarrels, and Valka answering the concern beneath a question while ignoring its literal terms. Keep wet shoes, ash, and cards beside mortality.
- Rhetorical ownership: The narrator owns lists, bargains, and self-defeating sarcasm; the gods divide blame, ritual precision, petty rank, poor lies, and reluctant listening; Valka owns social authority, affection, strategic misreading, and few explanations.
- Pressure behavior: The narrator speeds up, then loses fluency around death. The gods overlap and leak facts. Valka grows quieter, making everyone hear their own fear.
- Relationship movement: The narrator wants a safety promise but cannot ask Valka not to die; Valka wants trust without requesting permission to age; the gods want restraint without confessing fear. Returning the card transfers leverage to Valka and replaces surveillance with uneasy honesty.
- Anti-default: Avoid polished divine caseworkers, a wholesome fitness lesson, or a correct professional fixer. Keep preservation improvised, habits consequential, testimony inconsistent, and resolution domestic.

## Beats

1. The narrator arrives after Valka's roof-climbing alarms the family. She smokes, serves forbidden food, and wins at cards without breathlessness. Her pulse falters; someone curses in the empty pantry, and it steadies.
2. While Valka sleeps, the narrator follows petty impossibilities: rain finds one ember, a loose step tightens, spoiled fat turns harmless. Catch the road god outside; his repairman alibi fails ordinary questions.
3. The pantry yields hearth and weather gods arguing over shifts and recruiting the narrator to confiscate tobacco. Each intervention costs effort, gaps remain between domains, and nobody can make Valka obey.
4. An ordinary deck keeps them talking. Their competing boasts converge: during a brief death, Valka caught their collusion in a threshold game, changed the stakes, and won. A marked card hidden in the house proves the suspended debt.
5. The narrator offers concealment for stricter protection. Divine panic exposes the desired guarantee they cannot give. Their quarrel wakes Valka, who recognizes gods and card, asks dangerous questions, and promises neither secrecy nor docility.
6. Valka makes the narrator name the fear beneath supervision. She chooses one immediate risk to forgo, not a wholesale conversion. The narrator returns her card. Valka resumes the unfinished hand with the gods present and unable to relax; stop before any debt or future death is named.
