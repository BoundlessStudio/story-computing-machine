# Opus review: The Gods Are Afraid of Grandma

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the pantry introduction loses its speakers and referents and runs on comebacks, and the decisive card threats have no legible stakes.
- Prose: PASS — lively, concrete comic narration; only staging nits, a water-infrastructure policy flag, and stray straight apostrophes.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the prompt promise is delivered in band (about 3,100 words), but the queen is on the table and in the narrator's hand at once during the climax.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree. It missed the untrackable pantry whispers, the setup-and-comeback introduction, the unexplained card, and the card-in-hand contradiction.

This dialogue is better than the collection norm at its two poles. Valka wins exchanges by refusing other people's framing ("They have been interfering." / "I came down."). The road god's municipal pedantry works as both joke and confession: his "Underlay" correction is how he gets caught. The mess is concentrated in the pantry scene (L117–L169). There the reader cannot tell who is whispering or who "she" and "you" are. The narrator, billed as "the anxious one", meets two strangers who have appeared in a pantry the narrator just searched with deadpan setup questions and word-turning comebacks instead of alarm. Elsewhere, the house tic of turning the other speaker's last word into a retort (L19, L115, L143, L169, L269, L369) gives different people one rhythm. The climax lands emotionally but never says what the marked queen actually does, so the "burn it" and "give it back" threats are hard to read. The edit should keep the comedy and each speaker's best lines, make the pantry scene trackable, let the narrator sound frightened before sounding clever, and plant one clause about the queen.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: the [WP] premise must stay explicit: grandma is very old and physically fit despite unhealthy habits, and it turns out the gods keep her alive because they fear her. Keep the comic, broadly accessible register, a complete resolution, and a length of 2,500–4,000 words (currently about 3,100). Craft profile: prospective-2026-08-23.
- POV: first-person past, told by an adult grandchild. The only cue to the narrator's gender is "She caught him" (L119). If D1 removes it, do not add a new one by accident, and never let a "she" be confusable between Valka and the narrator.
- Story facts to keep: Valka is 108. Forty-six years ago, at 62, her heart stopped for seven minutes in a bus depot. She met three local gods at a threshold before the underlying afterlife, caught them cheating, took over the deal, and changed the stakes. The game was interrupted, and it still binds them. There are three gods: the hearth god (a broad woman in an apron, blue flame), the road god (a man in a reflective vest with a brass hammer), and the weather god (a narrow person in a yellow raincoat, never gendered; keep it that way). Their domains, costs and limits (L173, L257, L275) must stay. The deck missing a queen pays off twice, with three queens at L75 and the missing fourth at L319; keep both.
- Universe: godhood rests on worship, power and authority (universe/rules.md "Divinity", CANON). The worship lines (L177–L181) and "surrender worship, perhaps even lose the offices that made them gods" fit it; keep. The threshold comes before the universal afterlife and does not replace it (universe/rules.md "Afterlife and continuation", CANON). Keep the line saying they cannot give endless life or bar her from the afterlife. Personhood protection (style-guide, LOCKED): Valka's mortality and choices must stay real. The gods postpone emergencies; they never cure her.
- What works and must not be flattened: the road god's legal register (it is his alone), Valka's economy of speech, the narrator's breakdown at L379–L399, and the final exchange from "You want me not to leave you" through "Stay." Its directness is earned, so do not add more explanation to it.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Pantry whispers: speakers and referents unrecoverable

- Where: L117–L125, whispering behind the pantry door (compare L153)
- Text:
> From behind the door came furious whispering.
> “She caught him,” said one voice.
> “Of course she caught him,” said another. “He wore the vest.”
> “People trust the vest.”
> “People trust roads. They merely tolerate you.”
- Problem: Ground/referent and Attribution. The narrator has never been gendered and Valka is asleep upstairs, so a reader takes "She caught him" to mean Valka. Thirty lines later, "She'll wake" (L153) does mean Valka. The narration puts both whisperers behind the door, which means only the hearth and weather gods. Yet "They merely tolerate you" only makes sense aimed at the road god, which makes "People trust the vest" his line, spoken from the reader's side of the door. The vest complaint also doesn't match what the reader saw: the road god was caught by his own "Underlay" correction, not by the vest. The reader has to guess three things (who "she" is, who is speaking, who "you" is) during the gods' first on-page exchange.
- Fix: Make "she" unambiguous or drop the pronoun. Tag the vest defense to the road man beside the narrator, speaking to the door. Tie the vest jab to something on the page: the dry vest at L95, or his correction. Keep it short and whispered. Illustrative only: "The grandchild caught him." / "Of course. He stood in the rain and let the vest stay dry."

### D2 · Major · Gods' introduction runs on setup-and-comeback, not reaction

- Where: L129–L169, the pantry reveal through "Exercise it on the brine"
- Text:
> neither of whom had been there when I searched it
> “It’s your floor?” I said.
> “Which grandchild?”
> “That does not narrow it down.”
> “This is why I said we should recruit the tall niece.”
> “Rain travels.”
- Problem: Several problems land in the scene where the reader meets the gods. (1) Uniform fluency under pressure: two impossible strangers are in a four-foot pantry the narrator just searched, and the narrator's first words are a dry query about floor ownership. The anxious grandchild doesn't register fear or disbelief until "Who are you?" twenty lines later. (2) Punchline Patch: the weather god has already said which grandchild ("who hides the cigarettes"), so "Which grandchild?" exists only to set up "The anxious one" / "That does not narrow it down." (3) Inert exchange and tennis match: the tall-niece run ("She lives two counties away" / "Rain travels." / "Not usefully.") changes nothing, and "recruit" is never picked up again. (4) Four more one-line volleys (L163–L169) end on another word-turn ("Exercise it on the brine"), so the whole scene has one rhythm.
- Fix: Give the narrator a real first reaction to the impossible, such as backing up, repeating a question, or saying the obvious, before any banter. Illustrative only: "I looked in there. There was nobody in there." Cut "Which grandchild?" and "That does not narrow it down." If the family joke stays, fold "The anxious one" into the weather god's first line as a description. Cut the niece run, or make "recruit" mean something (they wanted a family member on their side), and pay it off at L291. In the rank squabble, keep "Gods," and "Some of us fill potholes." The scene can end on the hearth god's cloth-shove; keep at most one word-turn comeback between L111 and L169 (see D4).

### D3 · Major · The card threats have no legible stakes

- Where: L251 (root cause), L329–L335 (threats), L431 (narrator's reflection)
- Text:
> Before she could do so, paramedics restarted her heart.
> “So this is what you’re afraid of.” I held the card by its clean edge. “I could burn it.”
> The hearth god grabbed my wrist. Her palm was fever-hot. “No.”
> “Or I could give it back to her.”
> The card was leverage over weather, fire, roads, perhaps death’s very doorstep.
- Problem: Ground/uptake at the decisive exchange. The story never says what the marked queen does, or even whether Valka won ("If she won… Before she could do so": do what? Win? Name the forfeits?). The reader therefore can't work out why burning the card frightens the hearth god, who would seemingly be released by it, or why returning it makes the weather god swear. L431 asserts that the card is leverage without saying how. The narrator's final choice to slide it to Valka lands emotionally, as giving up control, but not as a choice with known consequences. Valka's closing "We were in the middle of a game" / "This one is mine" loses its bite for the same reason.
- Fix: Fix one function for the card in the L251 exposition and let both threats follow from it. For example: Valka won the hand and pocketed the marked queen, and while she holds it the table stays open and she can call the game back. Burning it would then wipe out what they owe her, which is why the hearth god, who is fond of her, grabs the narrator's wrist. Giving it back lets Valka reconvene whenever she likes, which is why the weather god swears. Say plainly that she won, and replace "Before she could do so". Leave the hearth god's "No." bare once the stakes are planted. Do not add an explanation in dialogue, and do not pre-empt the narrator's "But it was not mine."

### D4 · Minor · Narrator's word-turning comebacks in the opening scenes

- Where: L17–L21, Valka on the trellis; L111–L115, herding the road god inside
- Text:
> “You are making you fall.”
> “Then don't help.”
> “I have no authority inside.”
> “You’re going to discover some.”
- Problem: Both are Punchline-style retorts that twist the other speaker's words. "You are making you fall" also reads like a typo for "yourself". A grandchild watching a 108-year-old hang off a trellis in the rain would plead or instruct, not correct her grammar. "You're going to discover some" literally means "you'll find authority inside", which is not what the narrator means ("I don't care, move"). This is the same device the gods use at L169 and L269.
- Fix: At L19, give the narrator a real instruction or plea for Valka to refuse. Illustrative only: "Stay there. I'm coming under you." / "Don't help." At L115, let the narrator refuse the jurisdiction argument rather than turn it into a comeback. Illustrative only: "I didn't ask. Walk." Keep "Don't yell. You'll make me fall."

### D5 · Minor · "That one counts" has no cigarette on stage; "swore" misfits

- Where: L43–L49, dinner; L67–L73, the pantry "mouse"
- Text:
> “That one counts.”
> “Then I’ll smoke it slowly.”
> “The mouse swore.”
- Problem: Ground/referent. No cigarette has been lit at the table, so "that one" could be the rain-soaked cigarette from the roof (L13, L29) or an unseen new one. Valka's comeback depends on the reader knowing which. At L71, the pantry voice said "Oh, for pity's sake", which hardly counts as swearing, so the narrator's word describes the scene wrongly. Valka's reply ("You would too, living on what you buy") is good and should stay.
- Fix: Add a beat before L47 in which Valka lights a cigarette mid-meal, so "That one counts" means "the one you're lighting now is your after-dinner one". That beat also sets up the smoldering cigarette at L77. At L71, change "swore" to something that fits what was heard (the mouse talked, or complained).

### D6 · Minor · Legal register leaks from the road god to the other gods

- Where: L135, hearth god; L261 and L347, weather god
- Text:
> “For the relevant purposes.”
> “We did not let her finish stating the stakes,” the weather god said.
> “Those were conditional,” said the weather god.
- Problem: Identical Voices, in a mild form. Clauses and conditions belong to the road god. When the hearth god and weather god hedge in the same contract language, the three blur exactly where the comedy depends on them being different kinds of petty.
- Fix: Give the hearth god a claim by long use and housework (who has swept this floor, and for how long). Give the weather god an excuse in their own idiom, such as mood, season, or blaming the cousin. Illustrative only, for L347: "Thursdays are difficult." Keep the content of L261 but loosen its phrasing.

### D7 · Minor · Attribution and card logic in the gods' squabbles

- Where: L237–L245, the cheating accusations; L261–L271, "The paramedics did that" run
- Text:
> “You marked the queen.”
> “Because you kept forgetting which suit was dominant.”
> “You changed it four times.”
> “The paramedics did that.”
> “It was a floor.”
- Problem: Attribution. Each run has three or four untagged lines with three possible speakers, so the reader has to back-count, and the second run is only resolved afterwards by the road god's cloth (L271). "Which suit was dominant" is not how card players talk (trumps), and marking a queen doesn't obviously help anyone remember trumps. "It was a floor." is a last-word quip with no clear point: floors don't shift? This floor couldn't? The cloth-to-face reaction already delivers the reveal.
- Fix: Tag one line in each run, or give it an action beat (who accuses whom). Use the ordinary card term, and make the marking a signal between cheating partners. Cut "It was a floor." and let the road god's cloth end the run.

### D8 · Minor · Duplicate road-god interruption gag in the confrontation

- Where: L363–L371 ("Underlay") against L379–L385 ("Offices"), the kitchen confrontation
- Text:
> “On a carpet.”
> “Underlay.” The road god sounded offended.
> I turned on him. “Do not help.”
> “Offices,” the road god said.
- Problem: The same beat twice within fifteen lines: the road god corrects a word mid-argument and the narrator snaps at him. The narrator already knows it was underlay (L27, L103), so "On a carpet" partly exists to set up the callback. The second instance ("Offices" / "I will put you in the pantry.") is funnier and interrupts the outburst at a better moment.
- Fix: Cut L367–L371 and let the narrator answer "I came down" with the actual point: she survived only because something was dropped under her. Keep "Offices" and the chair-backward beat.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Opening staging: drainpipe, trellis, bracket

- Where: L11–L27, Valka's climb and fall
- Text:
> Grandma Valka was halfway down the drainpipe when I arrived.
> She had one slipper braced on the porch roof, one bare foot feeling for the iron trellis
> The rusty bracket tore out of the brick.
- Problem: The first line puts her on the drainpipe. The next puts her on the porch roof reaching for the trellis, and the drainpipe never appears again. "The rusty bracket" could belong to either, and "hung above me" doesn't say what she hangs from. The reader can't picture the opening set piece.
- Fix: Choose one route down (porch roof to trellis is the one the scene uses). Say whose bracket tears and what she is holding. If P2 changes the gutter errand, the drainpipe can go too.

### P2 · Minor · Water infrastructure as default texture

- Where: L275 (culvert), L173 (ditches), L11 and L23 (drainpipe, gutter clearing)
- Text:
> a washed-out culvert left waiting while the road god softened Valka’s fall
> The road god governed the lane, its ditches, its crossroads
> The gutter had clogged with leaves.
- Problem: Style-guide "No default water infrastructure" (CANON) applies to authorized revisions, and the prompt doesn't ask for any of this. The culvert is a clear case. Roadside ditches and a household gutter and drainpipe are domestic or roadside drainage and still count as passing references.
- Fix: Replace the culvert cost with a road-domain cost that isn't hydraulic (a pothole left to swallow someone's hubcap, a stalled truck left blocking a bend). Change "ditches" to "verges" or "hedges". Give Valka another reason to be on the porch roof (a loose shingle, the aerial, a cat) and fix P1 in the same edit.

### P3 · Minor · Pantry and kitchen staging nits

- Where: L161, L181, L203, L211, L215; the jar and crock at L63, L67, L129
- Text:
> “Gods,” said the weather god.
> The hearth god wrung pickle brine into the sink.
> Silence followed. The road god stared at his knees.
> The hearth god blocked the door.
> A crock of pickles rocked on its base.
- Problem: L161 labels "the weather god" before the domains are assigned (L173), while the same passage still says "road man" and "apron woman" (L159, L169). At L181 the hearth god wrings brine although the road god holds the only cloth (L169, L211). The road god "stared at his knees" while standing at the threshold. It's unclear which door the hearth god blocks (the narrator is already outside the pantry) or whether the scene is in the pantry (crate, flour bin) or the kitchen (sink). One broken vessel is a "jar" at L63 and L129 and a "crock" at L67.
- Fix: Keep the neutral labels until L173, or move the domain paragraph earlier. Have her wring her apron or her hands. Seat or crouch the road god, or cut "knees". Name the door (the hall or the stairs), settle where everyone is standing, and use one word for the pickle vessel.

### P4 · Minor · Straight apostrophes

- Where: L21, L187, L297
- Text:
> “Then don't help.”
> The hearth god's mouth tightened.
> “We can't.” The hearth god's voice was quiet now.
- Problem: Mechanics. These are straight apostrophes in a file that otherwise uses curly ones.
- Fix: Replace them with ’ (L21 may be rewritten under D4 anyway).

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Blocking · The queen is on the table and in the narrator's hand at once

- Where: L353, L401, L429–L435, the climax at the kitchen table
- Text:
> Valka took the queen from me, rubbed the marked throat with her thumb, and placed it face down on the table.
> Valka’s hand closed over mine, card and all.
> I slid the queen across the oilcloth to Valka.
- Problem: A continuity contradiction around the story's decisive object. At L353 Valka takes the card and lays it face down on the table. At L401 her hand closes over the narrator's hand "card and all", so the narrator is holding it. At L429–L435 it is on the oilcloth "between us" again and the narrator slides it to her. The reader loses track of who holds the card at exactly the moment the narrator's choice depends on it.
- Fix: Keep L353: Valka laying the card face down between them works as letting the narrator decide. Change "card and all" at L401 so her hand closes over the narrator's empty hand, or over the narrator's fingers on the oilcloth beside the card. If helpful, make L353 say "between us" so that L429 reads as continuous.

### C2 · Minor · "Passing her medical checks" contradicts the damage catalogue

- Where: L291 against L277–L279
- Text:
> stop asking why the woman who salts bacon keeps passing her medical checks
> blood vessels the hearth god called “an insult,”
> “We are not making her healthy,” said the hearth god.
- Problem: The story's own twist is that Valka is fit but not healthy: arthritis, cough, bad vessels and a failing liver remain, and the gods only catch emergencies. A doctor would find all of that, so she would not be "passing" checks.
- Fix: Change L291 to what the family would actually wonder about: why she keeps surviving falls and scares, or keeps coming home from the hospital. This keeps the prompt's "old and fit" puzzle.

### C3 · Minor · Weather god acts on temperature despite the stated domain limit

- Where: L173 against L275
- Text:
> although not temperature, which belonged to a cousin no one liked.
> a whole afternoon’s rain held in a cramped cloud while the weather god cooled her fever
- Problem: Domain limits are an operative rule ("They could only work within their domains"). Cooling a fever is a temperature act, and the rain held back as the cost doesn't explain how it was done. Breaking heat waves with rain (L273) is fine.
- Fix: Make it a wind or rain act, such as a cold wind through her window during the fever, and keep a matching cost. Alternatively, drop the cousin's temperature exclusion.
