# The Sleeping Future

I kept inviting Ida to breakfast because I was ten and I had read that you asked people over when they lived near you. I couldn't see how she could live nearer. I ate beside her left hip most mornings. On Westerly the edge of a pod was plenty cold through a shirtsleeve and just the right width for a book.

In the old Earth register a clerk had put her down for number fourteen, North Ferry Row, and in the margin Ida had written *Use the door by the pear tree. I can't hear the front one.* I thought it must have been wonderful to be able to add a tree when a clerk gave you an address. I'd found two photographs of a sofa being carried down the steps at the end of the Row. In the second one a boy had come out to help. There was so little of him showing over the arm of the sofa that I counted the steps twice to see where he'd got to. If Ida needed a hand up I knew where to go ahead of her and put it out.

It was seven minutes past seven, which meant the bread trolley was due to hitch over the seam in the window deck. Marl had got it down to one audible knock. If I'd had the sense to learn music I'd have written him a piece for the trolley, with two staves of silence between the rows of green pod lights and one good wallop right where he had to bring my breakfast. This particular morning I smelled the bread before I heard the knock.

I set the register in front of him when he came to Ida. “Where do you knock if their tree is gone?”

He bent to read it. This I liked about him. He had the book in memory and let me show him the page every time.

“We get Ida to the ferry at the rendezvous,” he said. “I could show you the bags she brought. You might find something with the address on it.”

I had made a study of the word *ferry*. In the pictures people loaded things on one shore and took them off on the other, and I was impatient to get amongst the things that went. I drew the pear as a kind of fat spade on my hand. It was still wet when the small familiar tremor under my elbow went away. Beyond Ida's pod I could hear the other bank running. Right beside me there was a little noise from the paper under my elbow and after that the bread, cracking as it cooled.

Marl laid the cloth from the tray over the roll he'd cut for me. “Tell me which side.”

He wanted me to keep my eyes off the board until I'd said. He'd been teaching me to hear the machinery since I'd mistaken the air fan for somebody talking in the corridor. I'd gone out in my socks and walked far enough to get the automatic lamps to light. There had been nobody in any of them.

“Ida's side. The whole left bank.”

“Bring the lamp. Set it on the floor where I can find it.”

At the forward end of the deck, under the pilot console, there was a square hatch with the handle pulled sideways from the year the deck plates buckled. The hatch opened about as far as Marl could get his wrists apart inside it. He knelt and pulled until the hinge sang, and it opened another finger's width. I got the lamp down beside him. The light rose off his cheek where somebody had put fresh finish over a dent and neglected to match the old gray.

“The standby pump is turning,” he said. I could hear it now, a hurried sound below the deck. “It needs the bypass open. The handle is on the far wall. Let's try your reach.”

He got the back of my collar in one hand and steadied the lamp with the other. There was a nut that snagged my hair as soon as I'd got my head down. I reached until my fingertips hit a metal lip. My hand slid along it and only found more of the lip. I tried to get my shoulder farther down.

“You'll feel the one we want. Someone put a rubber grip on it.”

“Is Ida warm yet?”

“We're in plenty of time if we keep going. The grip is nearly as long as your hand.”

I put my arm in to the shoulder. At last there was a thing I could get my fingers around. The first way I pulled it, it felt bolted down. I was about to ask Marl to tell me which way it went when I thought of the door with the tree beside it. I put my thumb round the inside and took the weight of my arm off the handle. It swung as easily as an unlocked door.

“Keep your hand there,” Marl said. He laid his palm along the deck beside mine. We waited until the rushing sound began to settle down into the one I knew. He backed me out by the shoulders with the light in my eyes and a few hairs still caught in the nut.

I went to see Ida. The pear had printed itself on the white rail where I'd leaned to bring my face close. There was a little sight tube by her shoulder and, as I watched, three bright bubbles went up through it and were gone. Her mouth stood open just enough for me to see where there would be breath if the glass were raised. I'd had the idea, down in the hatch where I couldn't see her, that she might have opened her eyes.

Marl straightened the book to keep it from falling off the edge. “You should have both rolls, Ansa. Heat gets out of you very quickly down there.”

“Will you cut off the bit of my hair that got stuck?”

“I can make the cut even, if you want it even. You'll have to sit on the trolley and let me turn you round.”

I said I wanted it even. I took the morning's minutes down to the last decimal place while I ate, and Marl had me sign the record. There was a whole column headed *Operator*. I wrote my name under his, small enough that a third name would have fitted.

For years after that, on mornings when I could get my elbow on Ida's pod, I read whatever I could find of the coast. I went from passenger lists to somebody's winter household accounts and instructions for holding down a canvas roof in a wind. I thought North Ferry Row would be a different place if I knew how many matches forty people used in a week. The things I could never look up were usually the things I wanted most. How hard a cold drop struck your neck if you stood under a gutter with your collar down. Whether an awake person would turn over if you sat by their bed long enough.

At sixteen I had grown tall enough to read the sight tubes at a glance and was once more having to bend because I'd learned which one could fool you from a height. On the first morning Marl let me take both banks alone, I found him in the galley afterward with flour up one sleeve. He carried the log into the aisle with me. I could tell from which way his eyes went that he was going to ask me what Ida's reading had been. He took a second look at the page and read it back to me instead.

He taught me to fly in that year, too, on the console with the drive in simulation. The end of every practice run was the point where we'd meet the ferry. I'd ask where it went from there. He always talked about what we could do here to make it easier for them to take us aboard. When I got tired of the simulation I'd listen to Ibis and the other ports calling their supply boats in. Once that whole long after-supper hour was taken up by three men who needed a fourth to get a piano through the collar.

By twenty-three I was keeping the night watch, when I could hear a pump knock from my bed and look out to see where we were by the lights on the coast. The new coastal towns belonged to the dinosaurs. I'd seen them on the survey feed replacing the North Ferry steps with a broad tram crossing. Human survey crews could still go down when the towns let them, a few at a time, and I read the notices to learn where they were allowed to land. In our charter the ferry assigned to us had the same name as a little ship I'd seen in photographs of the evacuation, a ship with heat tiles and feet for landing. Marl said the charter covered everyone in the pods. I worked out what I'd have to pack to help Ida cross a station deck and go outdoors in the same morning, down to two kinds of shoes, while we waited for the ferry's flight papers.

* * *

I was twenty-eight the night we got the drive working. The smell from the console when I brought it up was the same smell a sleeve gave off before you took it away from the toaster. The rendezvous was due that night. I'd remade two of the drive contacts the day before and had a third in my pocket in case the metal shrank in the cold. When the gauge rose right through the old stopping place, the console shuddered and I braced one foot against its base and caught the edge with both hands.

Marl thought I was slipping. He caught my sleeve. He was still wearing the apron he used when the galley burners were open, and I was in the black station jacket I'd taken in to follow the red seams of the original. The bright scrap on my breast had outlasted three jackets. At twelve I'd done a month of temperature rounds for it. I'd known precisely how handsome it would make me look when I came down a stair in front of eighty-four people, and here I was in it with my toes turned out, stopping an immovable thing from falling over.

“Did we get a place to land?” I asked. “I've got the part where we meet the ferry. I want to see the leg that goes down to the coast.”

Marl let go of my sleeve. He went to the other side of the console and slid my docking sketch away from the screen. There was the rendezvous marker, well out from Earth where I knew it from the practice runs. A second mark came up beside it, on an incoming track. He brought up its image. I thought it was a photograph of several stations laid end to end until I saw how the structures fitted round the drive.

“You're looking at the colony ship,” he said. “The ferry will carry us across to it. Westerly was built to send people away from Earth. There was never a voyage back.”

I looked for the ferry in the image and found a white shape six or seven decks up from the big drive. It gave me something to point to. I kept one finger on the corner of the docking sketch and worked up the start of a smile, because he still had the picture up with enough room on it to show a boat leaving from that spot.

“Has the ferry been refused permission to land?” I said. “I'd like to know who refused it.”

“I should have answered you years ago.” Marl lifted his hand from the image. His knuckles showed the narrow black gaps between their plates. “These people signed up for that ship while it was still being built. They were put to sleep aboard Westerly to wait for it. I was charged with getting every one of them across alive. The ship has a place for you whether you stay awake or want a new pod. I counted you when I checked the manifest this morning.”

There was something to say back to him about being counted. I couldn't lay hold of it. I knew a dozen pictures of a ferry's gate coming down and the one I could recall all the way across was a photograph somebody had taken with their thumb in it. I tried the image of the colony ship again. Under it Marl had opened the itinerary as far as the colony world. Our transfer ferry was drawn as a line the width of a finger between two things in the sky. I'd seen how space on an itinerary was managed. If there was a leg down to Earth there would be a line for it.

“You watched me make a list of the houses,” I said. “I asked you to look up where they keep the spare keys.”

“You were seven when your pod began to fail,” he said. “Once I got you warm you asked when someone was coming to meet you. I showed you the names of every person on the station and you asked me when you could go where people were awake. I told you the people here were waiting for a ferry. The next morning you set your shoes beside your bed. You asked if I'd teach you to look after Ida until the ferry came. I'd been bringing you your breakfast in bed for twenty-six days. I wanted another morning like the one with the shoes. So when you talked about getting to Earth I let you.”

His hand was still above the itinerary. “Even after you were old enough to hear the flight plan, I kept letting you think it would get you to Earth. I knew what you'd do to get the people here that far. I wanted them to get as far as the ferry.”

At fourteen I'd drawn a floor plan of the house across from Ida's, where I meant to have a bed of my own and a hook on the door for a visitor's coat. I'd rubbed out the stairs and drawn them in again so there'd be somewhere on my way past them to sit and take my boots off. Marl said it would take me longer than that, on a cold morning, to get used to wearing boots on Earth. I pulled the docking sketch out ragged from beneath my finger.

“Why did you help me draw my house? You spent the whole afternoon.”

He watched the corner of the paper tear and put his hand down on the console. “You told me I could hang my apron on the hook if I came to see you. I liked hearing that. I wanted to hear what else would be in a house where you'd ask me to come in.”

He still had the apron on. I could see a twist of the hem where he always put one hand into it to get a hot plate out. I'd made the hook pointed enough to carry the whole weight of the apron by its loop, copied from the hooks in a picture of the receiving hall. The clerk had hung a cup on one, which was why I'd been able to see how it went.

Outside, at the wide end of the window, the winter coast was coming clear. In the new tram crossing over the old ferry steps the turnstiles had been set up in pairs, one for the travelers with high crests and one for the broad ones who had to push with their hips. I'd watched them coming home so many afternoons that I thought I could have found my way from the tram toward the Row if they'd let me off. I had a feeling in my legs I could have sworn came from actually walking there. I stayed at the window until there was just the curve of a black field and a lighthouse flashing at the head of it.

Marl moved the picture of the colony ship aside. He'd set the docking sketch in front of me again with the torn corner laid next to it. “I will need you to fly the approach. I'd have preferred to have told you all this on a morning when there was time for you to ask me to leave the room.”

“I can do that now,” I said. “Would you leave the approach on the board? I need room beside it.”

He looked at the old circuit gauges when he took his hand away. He went to the galley through the far end of the pod aisle. I heard the light over the dock collar come on as he passed.

I still had my file of notices from Ibis. They'd opened a recovery ward since the business with the piano. I'd wondered how they could find enough people for so many beds until I read their notices during an outbreak of fever. They'd taken in whole families from other stations.

I found Ibis on the plot and laid its approach over Marl's drawing. I could run my finger down either fuel column and see where we'd be scraping the tank on the way into dock. I tried to write the port a message in the order I normally sent a request for parts. I got to the word *patients* and had to put in a number. It was the first time I had asked another person to make room for even one of them.

The ward wanted the pod readings before it made an offer. While they were checking them I opened the register. In the days when the receiving halls were full, the clerks had to squeeze new destinations in beside the old ones. I found a woman who'd put North Ferry Row down as her old address and Ibis as her new one. The same name was still in the Ibis directory for a dining room. When I called, she asked me to hang on while somebody brought the plates. Then she wanted to know how I knew about the hall and had I been there myself. She had a table of people from the coast in for supper. If anybody from Westerly wanted to come down and see her, she'd be very glad.

I was holding the berth offer when Marl brought the colony papers back. The port had a power berth for Westerly. If we could get there with the banks in the condition I'd shown them, the ward said it could start opening pods within weeks, a few at a time.

“I asked Ibis if they'd take us,” I said. “I talked to a woman who used to live on the Row. She's got people from the coast still coming to her for supper. We could start waking people up this month and anyone who wanted to go could have supper with them.”

Marl read the offer right down to the switchboard number, then steadied the flight papers on his hand. “I thought you'd like to know what Ida was doing the day she came aboard. She found me with the things she'd packed from the receiving hall and asked if she could go back out to buy a proper tin. She had a packet of seeds she'd saved from her pear tree. I found her a tin in the stores. Then she wanted me to find the part of her contract that said where she'd be allowed to plant them.” He set an allotment plan under the port's reply and rested his finger on a patch of ground inked round in black. “Here is her name. She asked for a piece large enough to put in more than one tree so people would have somewhere to sit.”

I turned the allotment plan around. The slope went down to a footpath that carried on past all the plots. Ida had signed outside the boundary of hers with the tail of her name curling up into everybody's path. If the trees went where her name was, anybody passing could reach them.

“She asked to go all that way for ground of her own,” I said. “I'd have to tell her I'd spent her passage. I'd have to tell her that before I could ask her to be glad to see anybody.”

“Give her the passage, then. The ship will put a whole settlement down where she can open a door and go out in the rain.”

I held the corner of the plan as if there were a draft. “Two days I went round and round the aisle this month waiting for Ida's heart to settle. Last time it took one. I can do the same thing again. The people on that ship can keep her cold another sixty years and do the same thing when her heart starts up wrong. But at Ibis I can put somebody beside her who knows what to do about a heart that's had trouble sleeping. And the dining room at Ibis has people in it today, Marl. If we send them sixty more years out, those tables will be empty before they can even ask.”

Marl looked at the offer from the port. “Some of the sleepers may have chosen never to see those people again. You can ask when they wake. By then the ship will have left.”

He said it gently enough that I nearly let him go back to preparing the approach. I wanted to tell him I could save all eighty-four. There were things I'd been counting on doing by now, ordinary things, like putting my palm on Ida's back while she found her balance. All the years I'd been watching her chest to see if she drew enough breath had given me no idea what we'd talk about afterward. She might want me out of the room. I looked again at the patch they'd fenced in for her on the paper and tried to reckon how many people might be coming along that path if she'd ever got anything to grow.

The left pump lost power under the draw from the waiting drive. I felt the fan still going over the top of the console. In the deck there was a break in the vibration which I could have put my thumb into. Marl went to his knees at the pump hatch and tried the handle before I finished handing him the lamp. It came farther now than it had when I was small. I had to climb in after my arm, flat enough that when my head cleared the nut I'd left a wisp of pale hair on it. The little metal badge dug into my chest. I felt a place where the rubber on the bypass handle had worn right through.

Marl had his hand round my boot to keep my foot braced at the opening. I told him when I'd got the handle. Being able to hear the other bank running used to be the nicest thing about a failure, because I could listen to it until I got the two sounds back together. I put my head against the side of the hatch and waited for the second thrum. When I came out he was still holding the lamp in the place I'd asked him to hold it.

We went down Ida's row together to see that the pump had caught. From the dock end I could see the lamp shining on the floor where we'd left it, and above it two courses drawn on the screen.

“I can't tell whether Ida wants to see anybody,” I said. I could see the hand she'd have to put out, the one lying along the dark fold in her blanket. “She might say she picked that plot because that was the only place she wanted to go. I don't get to say she traded it for something. I can put a doctor beside her this month and let her decide who else comes in. I want her to have that.”

We walked back to the console. The ferry had called twice while we were down at the hatch. I picked up the receiver and told Marl I was taking Westerly to Ibis. I gave the colony ship the port and berth number so they could find the people whose names they had. The ferry answered that it was already coming for us and could still reach the marker. I told it to go back.

Marl took the log from between the sheets showing the two courses. “I would have put them on that ferry,” he said. He wrote Ibis on the next line and signed for the watch. “I'll be with the banks while you fly.”

He took the lamp with him down the aisle. At Ida's pod he put his palm flat on the white rail where the pear had printed. He was looking at the sight tube when I took the port's course and lit the drive. The coast tipped up in the window and I felt the belt draw tight across my ribs. I thought of Ida in the next row with nothing to steady herself on, her left hip all I'd felt of her for years through a sheet of cold glass. When the drive fell quiet, the coastal lights had gone beyond the frame. Ibis was still too far to see and I had to turn back to the screen to know I was carrying us toward it.


* * *

We began to wake the left bank on the nineteenth day at Ibis. Ida was the first to open her eyes. Through Westerly's open collar I could see a woman from the port trying to tell her boy how to pull a sack of clean blankets around the turn. He put his whole weight into it backward. When it came loose he went down sitting on our deck and laughed. A little of the air came through with him. It always smelled faintly of somebody else's cooking at Ibis, even the parts of the port where nobody lived.

I'd got good at caring for Ida with her eyes closed. Awake, she wanted her hands where she could see them. I put down the check sheet so she could draw her left one out of the blankets. She got as far as the cuff on her wrist and stopped to wait for her breath. Her gaze climbed the red seam of my sleeve to the gold on my jacket. She touched the point of the badge with one finger.

“Westerly. When do we change ships?”

Marl pulled up a chair between Ida and the window and set a card on the seat. He'd written the date, the port name and our berth number on it to have ready when he told her where she was. He stepped back when I reached for the card.

“I want to tell her,” I said. I turned the chair a little so Ida could still see the dock and sat. “My name's Ansa. I flew Westerly here. This is Ibis, one of the stations near Earth. The colony ship sent a ferry for us and I used our drive to get us here instead. The ship's gone.”

Ida took her hand off my jacket. There was a crease in the side of her face from the way she'd been lying. The little ridges in it had gone red. She looked behind me at the window and did a little calculation with her lips moving. I'd imagined being able to tell her where she'd come out of the clouds and what to wear. I had the warmest blanket drawn right up to her waist and could see her toes pushing against it.

“Who gets my ground, then?” she asked. “I had the plot by the path. I picked it because anybody coming down would be able to reach the trees. The man who showed us the plan said you could walk that way right to the shore. I kept asking him to tell me where there'd be people going past.”

“The crew said they'll reassign the ground when they get there. I can send word about what you wanted done with it.”

“I was going to do it,” Ida said. Her hand moved toward her throat. “Can you get me the tin, anyhow? It was packed in with the clothes.”

I brought it from the drawer where I'd had her kit put. She could hold it in one hand but I had to help her get the lid off. She tipped one seed into the crease of her palm and got it back inside with an effort that made her breath catch. I thought she was going to ask for the lid. She asked where the boy had gone who was laughing earlier. I told her he'd be back when he'd fetched the rest of the blankets.

“I expect he's got supper,” she said. “Have you got anybody here I might know?”

The woman from the dining room had sent me the names of people who'd like to be found. I brought Ida the printout and she put the tin at the top of it to hold it down. At Enid's name she bent her head so quickly I had to come up beside her. Enid had put down the same room in the receiving hall as Ida. The clerk had squeezed a note beside it in red, *Two persons. Issue a second key.*

“Put Enid on for me, will you? She'll tell you I owe her for a pane of glass.”

Marl drew the cord through the handle of the chair so it'd reach. I got somebody at the switchboard and said who we'd like and held the receiver near Ida until her fingers made it round. From the pods beyond us I could hear both banks. Ida shifted the receiver twice till she could hear it clearly, and then she steadied herself on my wrist with a hand warmer than mine.

“Enid,” she said, and the hand tightened. “It's Ida from the Row. How soon can you get here?”

The woman on the other end started telling Ida where she was. I put the port card where Ida could read it. She gave Enid our berth number and told her to come straight in.
