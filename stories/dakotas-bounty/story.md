Of the three bad likenesses of Dakota that had come out of Gilt Mile, she liked the one that gave her small, sharp teeth. A million credits looked about right under a mouth like that. She drew a red blossom beneath the chin, added *BRING YOUR TRUCK EMPTY* at the bottom and paid to have that version run off by the hundred. Breck Sorrell, who paid for those pictures and most of the guns in the badlands, wanted her alive at Madder Switch at noon, when his train came in. She’d spent the week giving away his bad picture. At the garage on the salt road she sat out front with a pot of cold coffee and showed people the way to Madder whenever they stopped.

At noon the road to Madder ran out of room. Drivers were coming round the backs of other drivers with their outside wheels in the saltbush. The hot tin freight board banged against its post and the smell of slipping belts rose from the road. Dakota stood at the end of the loading platform with her hands behind her, the wind blowing her blonde hair into her mouth and flattening the red pleats of her skirt, and counted eleven empty beds. She’d wanted twelve. A man down on the road saw her looking and held up the spare helmet from the back of his motorbike. There used to be a pastry tucked inside it when he came to take her home for the night. She wanted to ask him if there was one now, which was about as unkind a thing as she could have said to him.

The man who had come up the platform steps reached for her wrist. He’d got out of a white truck with fat tires and a broad, bare bed. There was a loop of chain bright and unused around his other hand.

“You can put that on me right here,” she said, and let him close his fingers. “I just want you to have a look behind you first. Every one of those people has the same picture you do. How are you getting me down the road?”

“I’ve got friends coming.”

“Well, get your truck while you wait. Back it right up under here and I’ll step in.” She gave him the little lift at one corner of her mouth which was worth more, in her experience, than taking pains to look honest. He went down the steps two at a time. When Dakota turned, Della Quince was standing in the freight lane where she’d asked her to come. Della watched the chain go down the steps, then looked at Dakota’s bare wrist. She stood her ground and waited for Dakota to come down to her.

She had one ear of a brown coat pinched under her arm. It was the coat she kept in the cab for people she hauled who complained they were cold, one of whom had been Dakota. Back when they shared a bed, Dakota used to put it on first thing and see what Della had left for the two of them to eat. Georgia cloth, Della called it after the woman who’d sold it to her. It had been worn smooth where a chin rested in the collar. Dakota had given it back by way of a man who sold rugs, and it still had the empty threads where she’d snapped off a button getting out of it one night on the road.

“Put this on,” Della said. “We can go the way I came. Half the men at the fence have started for the steps because they saw that man touch you.”

“You came in a truck?”

“Yellow one. I left it where there’s shade of the building.”

“Empty?”

“The bed’s empty. I saved you the seat beside me. I mean to be able to see you the whole way to the pay table.”

Dakota took the coat and tucked it under Della’s arm again. “I want you to walk me over in full view. Give me to Sorrell yourself. He’s turning up with a whole trainload of people to impress and he’s left the money here with the exchange so everybody will believe he has it.”

“You could have just said that on the back of the picture you sent me. I’d have worn my good shirt to hand you over in.” Della waited a little. “What are you buying?”

“Seats. By the truckload.” Men had been loading people into cattle cars for Sorrell all summer, and Dakota had seen most of the people she needed rides for during an engine stop in the town south of here. She watched the white cab ease through the mess of other trucks. Della turned to look with her, the way she always did when there was a question of room. “Your mother’s in the third car.”

Della had been holding the coat at the collar. Her second hand came up and held it at the same place. “Tell me what you saw.”

“Her hair was pinned up with a pencil. They had her standing below the step of the car and she was giving a girl ahead of her a boost. I’d have known that voice from three cars down. She wanted the girl to put her foot a little lower so she could give it a proper hold.” Dakota remembered a man getting up in the doorway to hit somebody further down the line, and how she had gone on looking for that gray head until she found it again. She’d counted to the third car from the engine twice.

“You watched them take her aboard?”

“Yes. I waited till the train left.”

It was a long time before Della let the coat hang free of either hand. Below them a tire spun on the loose fill and the wind blew the taste of it up the lane. Dakota had heard Della looking for her mother on a radio in a cab at night. Della had been giving the woman’s shoe size, which had made Dakota want to turn the radio off and lie down with both arms over her head. She’d listened to the end and called three places she knew. All of them had wanted to know if Della was still a friend of hers. She had said yes three times.

“Breck Sorrell’s men came for her the night of the fire,” Della said. “Somebody put down her name as owing for what burned, along with twenty other names. I went over to ask how much money and got a list of the trains she might be on. Every time I saw a car like one of those I tried to picture you leaving Tansy. We’ve got about five minutes before you ask me to take your hand in front of that man. You burnt the freight shed. Tell me what you saw after you did it.”

“The tar paper got going up on the roof and for a while it came off in pieces that were still black on one side. There was an upstairs window over the tinsmith’s shop and the paper hung in its awning. I saw it catch. I’d known old Mr. Cams was upstairs. He’d wave from the window when we got in late, remember? I went down and shouted for him and I kept shouting until his daughter came out below me. I could see where she’d split a shoe at the toe trying to get him down the back stair. She had hold of him. I thought, well, she’s got him. I left her to it and went for my truck.” Dakota took her hair out of her mouth and had to hold it while she spoke. “Sorrell named a price that would let me quit. Tansy was the third place where I’d told myself I was quitting.”

Della drew in a breath to say something. Dakota was glad she hadn’t said it yet. There had been more after she went for the truck, and if Della spoke in the silence Dakota knew she would let her.

“I started the engine and sat there with our bags behind me. I’d told you to meet me at the truck when you heard the horn. I had a whole idea about how we were going to go away together that began with you getting in when I told you to. I could still see the shop door down the street. There was a chair out in the road that the daughter had got from somewhere. I put the truck in gear and drove around it. I thought I’d see your lights once I got far enough down the road. I drove right on at the turn.”

Della folded the coat in half over her arm with the sleeves left dangling. “I was already at the shop by then. I put old Cams in that chair while his daughter went back for his glasses. There were two of us carrying him when we finally got into the road. A truck would have saved us a lot of time.”

Dakota held the hair back a little longer. It was an extraordinarily ordinary thing she could have done, stayed with the truck, put their bags at Mr. Cams’s feet and waited another five minutes. There would have been room. She looked beyond Della’s shoulder at the hot boards of the platform where the end of the white truck had come to rest, neatly squared up to the lip just as she’d wanted.

“Who else is in the third car?” Della asked.

“Two women from Tansy who ran the feed shop. Others I can’t place. Most of another town in the car behind. I counted up to ninety and they brought a wagon in after that.”

“I’ll make a driver take the people in his bed wherever they ask to go. He gets paid when I see them all aboard. I’ve got a truck that’s going as far as my aunt’s place with my mother in it, if my mother can stand the trip.” Della lifted her face and looked straight at Dakota. “I want the money where I can see it until that first truck is ready to leave. Breck Sorrell would send somebody after that money before dark. By then I could be across the flats on a road he’s never heard of. I thought that part through on the way here. I’d rather be the one spends it right in front of him.”

“Have you thought what you’d do if I wanted a ride?”

“I’d have to see you coming.”

From the road came a little rush of voices, the sound people made when there was something they could all point to. Dakota looked past the chain-link at a dark engine pushing out into the light beneath the escarpment. She gave Della the pistol from the front of her skirt. Della took it and tucked it in her belt. Then Dakota turned up the lane with her arms folded behind her. She made herself wait until Della put a hand on her wrist before she took the first step.

* * *

By the time she and Della reached the exchange, Sorrell’s old engine had pulled away without its cars. It was going back the way it came. A little way up the grade lay a second track where the big engine waited to bring the train into the hills. Dakota had sat out the change at Madder often enough to know how long it took. In this heat there was time to unwrap a sandwich and eat it in the shade, if you already had the sandwich.

Prisoners for the north run stood behind the exchange in a ragged strip of shade that smelled of sun-warmed wheel grease. Men were bringing them up the steps at the far end of the platform. The doors of the cattle cars stood open along the track side, and through the one nearest Dakota she could see a good many bare feet with dark marks where the shoes had been. Across the platform, the white truck was backed up tight against the boards. A woman could walk straight across from the train door to the truck bed if she took care of the step down at the lip. Dakota had taken sacks of feed the other way once in a pair of worse boots than these.

“Third from where the engine was,” Della said. “The one they’re bringing more people to?”

A gray head came forward inside the third door. Dakota felt Della’s hand close hard on her wrist. The hair had come down since the other stop. Della’s mother pinned it up again with her pencil, in full view of everyone, with the small distaste she used to show when a visitor came and found her in her apron. The throat of Della’s brown coat hung over her arm. For a little while Dakota wanted to put the coat on and hide against her.

The exchange clerk had set a table across the platform near the steps down to the road. Sorrell came up to it with two men behind him, took the hat off his head and put it on the table as though he were calling on the clerk at home. He could have used any expression he liked and he chose the one he’d had when he was passing a plate of peach slices down a table at a party Dakota remembered, watching for the person he meant to leave with the pit.

“Miss Quince,” he said, when Della brought her forward. “I made a great many guesses about whose company I’d find her in. Somebody ought to pay you for keeping such excellent time.”

Della let go of Dakota only when Sorrell put a hand on her arm. The clerk held up a copy of the notice to read and Dakota turned her face so the sun got to the flower where her neck met her collarbone. She had drawn it on the picture a petal short. She was gratified to see that he counted. Sorrell told him to open the money box.

“I know her,” he said. “Let all those people see you pay.”

Della counted out ten strapped packets beside Sorrell’s hat, a hundred thousand to a packet. All the faces at the foot of the platform steps tipped a little her way. Dakota could hear one of the older trucks running hot in the lane. Behind her somebody in a car asked for a turn at the door.

“I paid her for her first job in a town that had just ceased to be of any commercial use,” Sorrell said. He spoke to the people on the steps, and when the train doors rattled he turned a little so the people in the cars could hear. “We counted the money on a table her friend brought out of a house. There was a fellow standing by who said, ‘Ten times that, if she’ll take another job.’ He was young. I suppose he thought it must be the price that kept her coming back.”

Dakota could still feel the surface of that table. Della had reached over and folded up one of the legs so it would fit in the truck, and there had been a minute, while Dakota collected her pay, when Della had been holding up her entire end of it.

“You could tell them what you think kept me coming back.” Dakota felt his thumb move on her arm. “Or you could let me tell them I came here of my own accord. The fellow with the white truck will get home tonight and say a woman walked right up to you. Your people in the cars will have heard it from my own mouth. They can tell from here whose hand is on my arm.”

The red dust at the toes of Sorrell’s boots was a different red from the dust of Madder. He looked down the length of the train and, when he looked back, Dakota knew he liked the thought of the story travelling farther than he did that night. She brought her hands behind her and stood as still as she had stood all morning. It was the least she could do in a negotiation to let a man notice what his own hand was doing.

“Give her room,” he said to the men close by. His thumb came off her arm a little before the fingers. “We shall hear what it took to get her to come back.”

She walked to the edge of the platform. The man from the white truck had climbed up on his running board for a better view and his chain hung out of the open window. He was still looking for the friend who was going to tell him there’d been a mistake about whose million he’d seen Della collect. She stepped down into his truck bed. He lurched so hard the whole truck moved under her feet.

“I said I’d get in,” she told him. “I’ve got some people coming with me.” She looked at the people crowding the railcar doors, then over the truck to the road. “A hundred thousand for a truckload out of Sorrell’s cars. I can pay ten drivers. Della Quince has the money right where you saw her count it. Get a load aboard and name where they asked you to take them.”

“I brought the truck for *you*,” the man said.

“You did. How much is it going to cost you to drive home empty?”

He looked all the way down the white length of his truck to the flower on her neck. The muscles jumped in his face and the bright chain slid to the ground. There had been a lot she had promised people with the same half a smile, and she doubted many of them had been this willing to buy a second thing from her.

“Clear my mirror,” he said. “I can go south as far as Tansy. Ask if they’ll have it, quick.”

Dakota stepped back up to the platform and shouted across to the nearest car. A woman with a loose sole on one shoe was already on the edge of its floor, waiting to see what to do with her feet. “South road,” Dakota called to the car. “Tansy and everywhere short of it.” She gave the woman a hand, backed up and called the ones behind. Sorrell was calling for the men he had sent back to the cars. The two closest kept their guns low. A barefoot man walked between them with both hands out for the truck. They let him go by, and they let the next one go too. A cuffed man with the print of a rifle stock in his cheek stopped on the planks and looked down into the truck.

“She says we can get home,” he called back to the car. Then he turned to Dakota. “Is your man going to take us all the way there, or have you got somebody else waiting for us on the road?”

“The man in that cab told me he’d go to Tansy. The fare’s for the ride all the way to your door if you’ve still got one.” She took the step down again to catch his elbow. A woman in the truck made room below him by taking a much smaller person up into her lap. Once there were people all the way to the sides, Dakota slapped the cab roof. The white truck pulled clear and swung broadside along the planks to get back to the road. Della caught the open cab window at the first step and put a packet through it. The driver had it in his hand when Sorrell fired.

The woman with the bad shoe folded over. Somebody put both arms out to try to catch her and took three others down. The truck rolled past slow enough that Dakota could keep a hand on the woman’s shoulder as she ran along the edge of the planks. A man in the bed got a better hold and lifted her face. There was dust on her tongue. When he put her down across his knees a wet patch showed through at her ribs, and for about three strides Dakota could see that the woman was moving her mouth with the effort of breathing.

The driver stopped where the lane dipped to the road. Dakota went down the platform steps with Della and they got the old coat up through the rail of the bed. A man put it under the woman’s head, folded with the collar out. Dakota could have hauled herself in where a passenger had made room for the woman’s feet. The driver checked his mirror and she saw him decide to go. She took her hands away. Farther along he got through the crowd at the fence and found the road. She could still see one sleeve of the brown coat hanging over the side of the bed as it went.

“The next one,” Della said. “There’s a green truck pulling up.”

Its driver had been standing by the exchange table in Sorrell’s company badge when the doors opened. Now he had one boot down out of the cab, peering under the brim of his hat at how far the white truck had got. The man beside him leaned over to take his shoulder.

“You saw him shoot into that truck,” the man said. “There’s glass on every side of us.”

“We’ve been standing in his dust a week for the money he owes me,” the green truck’s driver said. “If I go around this platform I want the same deal as the white truck. She put the money right in his hand. I could see his thumb over it.”

“Quince has nine more packets that size,” Dakota said. The truck was there if she wanted him to take her now while the men on the train still had a lot of prisoners to count. She had seen Sorrell shoot a man who went on eating for almost a minute, with a plate he kept moving closer and closer to his mouth. She’d thought there would be more of an interval today between the terrible thing and what she would have to say next. She could hear people in the cars beginning to shout out their towns. There were a few voices she knew. “I’ll show you the people that fit. Ask the first one you let up where he’s going.”

She climbed back to the platform. Della called out another fare and a third truck came up behind the green one, with room up front for young ones. The driver of the green truck stood with one boot on his running board until he saw who had come up behind him. Then he went around to the loading edge and got out to open the way into the bed himself. There was still one guard on the boards with his gun trained on the first person who tried to cross. The third driver came out of his truck carrying his own gun and two of the men on the steps did the same. The guard took a slow look down the road, where the trucks coming in with the sun across their windshields were past counting, and walked back to his train. Sorrell lowered his pistol an inch at a time. He called out the green driver’s name, and the driver took his money and left in plain view.

For a time Dakota had just the hands she could see in front of her and the noise of Della calling to drivers until her voice roughed over. An old woman got into a truck backwards, keeping an arm around the iron handle of the car until she had set both feet down. A man who had come to Madder to sell a pair of tires lifted a boy over the side of a truck and came back to see who else would go. Somebody on the posted road was tearing up a wanted print where he stood. Then Dakota could hear the big engine up the grade, taking up its place on the other track. She had seen the whole stop go that fast before with her sandwich still on the paper.

“Here’s my cab,” Della called.

Dakota could see the yellow truck now. Della’s mother was already inside with her pencil stuck back through the hair above one ear, where it would have to come out before she put her head back. Della stood with the passenger door open. There was just room beside her mother if they pulled the door in tight. At the exchange table the clerk still had his hand on one strapped packet, the last one Della had set aside for the last truck.

“You wanted a ride,” Della said. “Come on before I’ve got to ask twice.”

Dakota stepped towards the cab. She would have to lean to get into it, and Della still had a hand on the door beside the frame so it wouldn’t swing back on her. She could see Sorrell past the gap between Della’s head and the cab. He was closing one of the car doors with a man still standing in it. Beyond him the new engine was easing into the space ahead of the first car.

“Take that road you said you’d take,” Dakota told Della. “I’m still working.”

She got up to the last open door while Sorrell held it half shut. A man in the car had put his damaged foot sideways into the space so Sorrell would have to shut the door on it. He had an old-fashioned bag folded very small under one arm. Sorrell was looking down at his foot, trying to decide something before he hurt him.

“I’ll give you a minute,” Dakota told the man. “Can you take the bag out first and put both hands on my shoulders?”

Sorrell lifted his head. “I paid the million in full, Dakota. I’d advise you to get back on this train.”

The wind had blown hot grit inside her shirt. The man in the car eased his foot past the upright and the weight of his hands came down hard enough to make the little chain at her throat dig into her. She stood till he’d put his good foot down too. With some time to look at Sorrell, she found she wanted to tell him that she’d counted on him to pay in full, that in all the years she’d worked for him a fine clear price had been all she really needed by way of an excuse. He might have liked to hear it. She let him wait and watched the man with the bag find his way across the boards. The last truck driver had pulled up for him. She reached up and slid the door to. The sound went all the way through the empty car.

“Come here, Dakota,” Sorrell said. She was as close as she’d been when his hand was on her arm, and there was nothing in his face she could mistake for the man she’d once been happy to see coming for her with money. She smiled because she’d wanted a look at him standing right here from the instant she saw the bad print of her own face. He watched her step down over the bright edge of the platform into a truck that had room for her standing up. The truck bed lifted over the rut at the foot of the drive and she was high again, with the saltbush gone almost gray below her. Sorrell had people at his back who would pay dearly to know which road she was taking. Looking back, she saw dust lift from so many roads going out of Madder that she lost the one she was on.
