# The Last Firmament

For a septillion years I had been very generous with my hours. A god once used one to read me his abdication from both ends of a roll, on the belief that if he could bring the terms into contradiction he would have to be allowed to live. I let him read until the two ends met. There was an orchard behind him coming white from the middle out as it came into bloom, which made it a very easy hour to give away.

I wanted the hour after Lethra for myself. Neret had shown me where to sit, on the left side of the sill that took the evening heat. I had never sat in anybody’s window without waiting for someone to die. I was looking forward to finding out how it felt. The sky had come rather near the exact place I’d arranged to sit. It had looked bigger and higher when we made the arrangement.

I had in my right hand the scythe with which I took the god. The cold along the ridge had shrunk the wood away from the metal bands so that I could turn one with my thumb as I walked. The blade had let the soul of a world out through its frozen sea. Miles of ice moved away in silence on either side of the cut. Elsewhere, getting a nation of people out of their houses for the last time had taken a good deal of calling up and down stairs. I had come on from one city to the next until only Lethra was left. From the ridge I could see where all its roads ran out in blue dust.

A dying galaxy turned in the west amid the bright blue wrecks of others. Its long gold arm drew the edge of the sky upward. The city’s spires went with it until the iron stays pulled taut. Between the spires the sky bent down and broke the glass dome over the Stair. Red sparks sailed through the folds of my cloak. I stood where the ridge leveled, with the scythe planted beside me, and could feel through the shaft the note of iron drawn almost to the point of breaking. The black grasses lay flat along the road.

Neret’s red window was seven steps up the Stair of Late Arrivals. On the evening I took her, she made me sit on the sill to see how far down the slope you could look from there. All the travelers’ stairs came into view, and the people going up them with their bundles had gold in their hair where the heat went out through the glass. She laid her wrist in my hand halfway through telling me how much a second pane would have cost her. The pulse went first from the fingers, then from the long place inside the wrist. A thread of orange still worked its way through a large bubble in the pane after her hand grew cold.

When I told her I’d be back to sit there once I’d made the last crossing, she was already on her feet. She turned around to see where I meant. Then she said she’d always intended the cushion to go with the house.

The cushion was gone when I arrived. The stone was still warm. Higher up, when the spiral lit the whole Stair, I saw the woman I had come for.

She was at the upper landing, standing between the spires with her feet a long way apart. The iron stays above her were drawn so tight that new lengths of them showed at the mouths of the sockets. She looked like someone riding in the back of a wagon over a bad road, used to it and unwilling to sit down. I watched her draw her shawl up to her throat. Then she put it over one arm, as if she were about to shake hands and wanted her fingers free.

“I have come to take you across,” I said. “We can go by the lower road if there is a window you want to see on the way.”

“I saw you come past the lower road. The sky’s been pulling it uphill by the rooftops. The bolts in the doors there fall straight out if you open them. Were you going to walk me that way?”

“The road will serve for the two of us.” I held my forearm out, with the wrist turned up. “You can take hold anywhere along the wrist.”

She drew her hands beneath the shawl. “Put your hand away. I watched the others give you theirs when it was time for them to go. You may have arrived at the hour you expected. I intend to stay.”

She went around the foot of the spire, to where the top step ended at a drop. There the edge of the sky hung down almost to the step. When I bent to look under it, the back of my skull brushed the stone spire through my hood. The orange of my eyes reached her first. She was broad, in a red-brown dress and an apron, very grey at one temple. She had pushed one sleeve up to the elbow and set her wrist down on the stone where I could reach it.

“If you’re going to take me with that thing,” she said, “have the decency to find out how much of me you’ll be taking. Can you reach as far as my wrist from where you are? I remember how you go about this.”

I set the scythe against the spire and laid two fingers on her wrist. She took my free hand and set it on the step beside hers, with my fingers pointing out over the drop. The stone was warm at the heel of my hand. My fingers reached under the edge of the sky, into the dark behind the galaxies.

I could still tell you the weight of those two fingers on the small place in her wrist. I had been listening at wrists and temples and the cold floors of oceans for longer than the stars had kept their present shapes. You could follow a pulse to the furthest edge of a body, so long as there was life in it. This one was quick and weak. The tendons moved between my fingers and the beat went out under my other hand, through the dark behind the gold spiral, through the long blue darkness behind that, until I felt it come up on the other side of the sky. I bore down on her wrist, making sure of the next one. It went the whole way again. I was holding the wrist of the universe.

I had crossed the dark from one dead star to another, and at each one there was less left to carry. I had thought that by the time I reached Lethra there would be one woman left where everything else had ended, and all I would have to do was take her across to the others. Now I could feel the length and breadth of the living body I had meant to part from its soul. I had put my hand out for her with the same patience I had shown Neret when it was her time. My fingers were still around the woman’s wrist. I took them off one by one because I was afraid she would think I meant to close my hand.

She drew a breath that hitched at the top. “Tell me when you have it. That’s a tiring way for me to stand.”

“I came here prepared to take you out of your body,” I said. “I know how far it goes now.”

She waited until I drew my hand back from the drop. Then she went to sit in the shelter of the spire, taking pains over the fall of her skirt. Above the place where my fingers had been, a flake of the sky lifted and dropped as the edge strained upward. I picked up my scythe and fitted the blade under the flake.

“Would you move back a little?” I said. “I’d like to see what happens if I can lift this much sky.”

She gathered her skirt and got up with a hand against the wall. She moved as far as the stair rail, where she could still look in.

I put my weight against the shaft. At first there was the sound a foot makes coming out of mud. Then beyond the pale edge that I had lifted, in country so deep no light from Lethra had ever reached it, a small point took fire. It grew bright so quickly that the stone of the spire threw off its own shadow. Heat reached my fingers through the scythe. I braced the bowing shaft against my knee to gain another breath with the opening held.

The flake came down at an angle and struck the stairs. I got the blade out with both hands. The light went out and a stay caught the hem of my cloak as I came back. I left a handspan of blue cloth in the iron and stood over the broken edge with hot fingers and the scythe still in my hands.

“Oh, I used to be warm like that,” she said. She came up beside me and held her face toward the drop. “I’ve had whole skies come away from me before. I went on living in the dark until I was warm enough to grow another. I’d forgotten how far out you could feel a star starting. Did you see where the light got to this time?”

“All the way to where we stood,” I said. “I’d have had to walk for years to fetch a soul from as far out as that star.”

I put my hand where the stone had warmed. It was cold again. “Let me see if I can take that old sky from you.”

She put a hand on the stone of the spire. “Before you do, look where you’re standing. There was a bare shelf here when the ships put people down on the ridge. They’d come a long way in those ships. They brought down a great quantity of chairs, and then the whole party sat on the ground. It did them good to feel the heat in the stone. The edge of the sky kept lifting in the night and letting the warmth out, like a blanket when you kick it off. Neret came up with a pane of glass under one arm. She knew what a wall would do with the wind if we could get together the stone for it, and I showed her where to look. I helped her plant her first spire right through the edge of the sky to hold it down over the shelf. Then she built the Stair so there’d be room for the others coming up. She used to call me down to see who’d arrived. Seva, she’d say, come see what they’ve brought us this time. I’d have to leave whatever I was doing and go down or she’d keep on till the whole street could tell she wanted Seva.”

The spire shifted under her hand. She had to stop and put her shoulder into the stone before she could go on.

“Neret drew out her stairs with the end of a burnt stick. I told her she’d made the third one too broad. She took the children out of the crowd and stood them where she’d drawn it, all jostling one another with the drop right there, until I got so cross I went down the slope and found her a piece of stone big enough to set them all on. She was clever with people. She said I had made the sky wider for them, and I was very pleased with that at the time. Look down there. I’m asking if you can take away the sky and leave me that first landing. The one with the red glass.”

I moved out from the spire to look where she pointed, although I knew which window she meant. From above I could see the ironwork Neret had laid in its sill. One end of the rail had come loose. I lifted the scythe and took a step down. It was hard to give her an answer with the whole Stair in front of me and the stones all apparently in the places people had meant them to be.

I climbed onto the sill to see if there was still a join above it where I could free the spire. While I was looking, one of the stays came out of its socket. The sky took it straight up with a stone still on its end. The spire bent with the sky. The same red ran through them both where they had grown together. I could feel the whole landing lift a little under the pull. I got down from the sill to see where the city could break away from the ridge.

Below the window, at the edge of the first landing, I found what I’d missed. In the side of a broken tread I could see the course of dark stone Neret had set all those years ago. It ran beneath each of the spires in turn, between the stone hauled from farther down the slope and the bare rock of the ridge. I put the blade to that line and the edge caught, as I’d known it to catch at the cleavage of worlds.

On the long roads I had pictured myself setting the scythe down beside Neret’s window, with the blade turned out beyond the sill so it could hang at its easiest. There it was in my mind, as plain as the loose rail that lay across the window now. I heard Seva coming down the stairs. She was holding to the spire with both hands. She stopped just above the window and I saw how little her shoulders rose when she tried to get her breath.

“The cut has to start under the window,” I said. “If I run the blade along the footing, everything the spires are holding down can lift away. The landing will go with them. I wanted the window to stay. Neret took me there when it was her time. I promised her I’d come back to that seat after the last crossing, with an hour ahead of me that was entirely my own.”

“Then you’ve seen it at its best,” said Seva.

I’d sat there on a night when the arrivals came up the Stair past us with their bundles. I had spent so long wanting the hour when I would have that view to myself. Seva reached in for the catch on the window. She had to lift the frame to clear the sill, and I took the other corner so she’d have the weight off her wrists. The wood hit the wall with the same knock I’d heard from down on the road when Neret opened it for me.

“We used to leave it like this if there were people on the road,” Seva said. She put her hand to the sill where I’d meant to sit. “You could hear Neret all the way from the bend. I’d give a lot to call down to somebody from here once more. Get me out to the rock where they used to stand and look up. I want to see the whole Stair when it goes.”

The stone moved a little under our feet. I stepped down from the landing to the ridge, put out my hand, and she took hold of my wrist. I felt her grip tighten as she put her whole weight on one foot. It was a short way across, and she came over with her head turned so she could keep the city in sight.

The air there tasted of iron. I set my feet and drew the head of the scythe back against the join. At first the weight that came into my hands was the weight of a single stone. Then I could feel the whole length of the Stair bear down on the blade, from the first room cut in the rock to the white tips of the towers. There was a place just above my right hand where one of the loose bands cut into the bone. I let it stay there. I drew the blade slowly enough that I could feel each spire let go through the shaft and fast enough that the next had no time to take its weight.

The old sky opened from end to end. The stone of Lethra rose whole for the length of a breath, and from underneath I could see the ends of the spires running through the sky like roots through soil. The rim took the city up into the gold spiral. I saw the Stair on its side, all the windows full of red light, the lowest one rising last. All the way across the old heavens the galaxies went curling out of sight, drawing their long bright edges after them. Some of them came very near us as they turned. I could have counted all seven steps up to the window in their light. The place where the stairs had been was bare rock.

I heard Seva take a long breath. I turned to her because I expected to have to take her arm again. She had her head back and her hands open at her sides. The warmth I’d felt before, in the little space under the flake, was coming up around us. Beyond Seva the first star caught, and in the time I stood there watching it others caught farther out. The distances were growing before my eyes. I began to see where I would walk again when there was need for me, but for once I was there early. I planted the scythe beside me. By then I could turn all the way around on the ridge and see new stars still coming into light.
