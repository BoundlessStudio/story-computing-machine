# The Last Firmament

## Opening 1

For a septillion years I had been very generous with my hours. A god once used one to read me his abdication from both ends of a roll, on the belief that if he could bring the terms into contradiction he would have to be allowed to live. I let him read until the two ends met. There was an orchard behind him coming white from the middle out as it came into bloom, which made it a very easy hour to give away.

I wanted the hour after Lethra for myself. Neret had shown me where to sit, on the left side of the sill that took the evening heat. I had never sat in anybody’s window without waiting for someone to die. I was looking forward to finding out how it felt. The possibility that the sky might fall on the exact place where I had arranged to sit had occurred to me once or twice. It had looked bigger and higher when we made the arrangement.

As I came down the ridge I had in my right hand the scythe with which I took the god. The cold had contracted the bands on the shaft until I could put a fingernail under them. To the west there was a gold spiral which had lived a trillion years without meeting any resistance on that side of the sky. It kept trying to turn the usual way. In the intervals I could hear metal drawn almost to the point of breaking in the spires of Lethra. The topmost glass dome hit the sky and sent down a shower of red sparks. I watched a few of them sail through the long folds of my coat.

I could see my orange eyes in a pane at the first step and for a moment had the impression Neret had come back to watch me climb it. Higher up, when the spiral lit the whole stair, I saw the woman I had come for. She looked down and I set the scythe upright. Most people like to have a good look from where they stand.

## Opening 2

By the time Death came in sight of Lethra, the galaxies had ceased to keep their distance. One that had turned for most of its ages in the farthest tract of the north now dragged a gold arm between the spires. Blue light came on behind it in such quantity that Death could see the little black grasses at its feet bending before each shudder of the ridge. It planted the scythe and waited, the hem of its cloak dragging in the dust whenever the road moved beneath it. A piece of sky went spinning through the grass without setting anything alight.

Seven steps up the old stair was the window Death had been looking for. It was as red as it had been on the night Neret died. She had put the sill in herself, low and deep enough to seat a visitor with a long back and very hard bones. “If you’d give the stair a rest for half a minute,” she’d said, “you’d see how far out I can see from here.” Her pulse had faded under Death’s two fingers as they sat. An orange thread worked its way across a bubble in the glass, below the reflection of the skull and its two small fires. She had been right about the view. From the sill you could see the lower towers all the way to the bare rock where Lethra, the last of the cities, ran out.

Coming back had been Death’s own idea. It had walked the stars for a septillion years, had set out from empty cities with their last kings, had carried a god away still drawing his kingdom in the air with his hands. It could remember every journey by the journey it began afterward. When it told Neret where it meant to sit after making the last crossing, she was already on her feet, with a quickness Death still found startling in the newly dead. She turned right around to judge the place she was giving up. Then she told it she had meant the cushion to go with the window.

It lifted the scythe. Above the stairs the curve of the blade and the rim of the sky briefly lined up. The light was straining upward, and every spire it caught gave a deep iron note through the shaft in Death’s hand.
