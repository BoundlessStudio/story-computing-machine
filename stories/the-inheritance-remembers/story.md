# The Counted Road

By the time the excavator reached the front step, Elsidra had painted two hundred meters of temporary centerline past the Talwin house, where she and Rovandis had grown up, and answered the same question about rain four times.

“Forty minutes,” she told the flagger through the open cab window. “If it starts before that, we close the lane and do it again.”

Across the cones, the excavator bucket lifted the old stone threshold. A demolition worker crouched in the cavity it left and pulled out something black and narrow.

Her mother moved faster than Elsidra had seen her move all day. Vaudessa ducked under the tape before the worker had straightened.

“Put that back.”

The worker held up an iron spike as long as his forearm. Its square head had been hammered almost flat. Hooked grooves wound down its shaft, packed with pale mortar.

“Can’t,” he said. “Step’s coming out.”

“Then don’t take the step.”

He looked toward Elsidra, because Elsidra wore reflective orange and therefore appeared to be in charge of every machine on the street.

She shut off the spray bar. “That’s my mother’s until handover. Bag it for her.”

“I don’t want it bagged.” Vaudessa did not take her eyes off the spike. “I want it back under the step. It was under that step before my mother was born.”

Elsidra looked at the bucket, where the threshold stone hung in its teeth. “Mom. There isn’t a step.”

Vaudessa held out both hands. The worker gave her the spike, and the grooves left four white hooks across her palms.

Caelumar came out carrying the second battery for his delivery bike and a paper sack of screws he had promised to save for his father. He was the last of them still using the old house as if it might ask for one more favor. He checked the upstairs windows, pushed the gate until its loose latch caught, and swung onto the bike.

“You’ve got your rear light?” Elsidra called.

He tapped the brake lever. Red flashed against the wet-looking new line.

“And your charger?” Rovandis asked from beside his parcel van.

“That’s what the battery is.”

“The charger charges the battery.”

“It’s in my bag.” Caelumar pushed off. “Both of you can stop inventorying me.”

He rode between the cones, raised one hand to Vaudessa, and turned downhill.

Vaudessa watched the empty bend. Her thumb traveled from hook to hook on the spike.

“One,” she said.

Elsidra opened the paint valves again. “One what?”

Vaudessa wrapped the spike in her cardigan. “Last out.”

The flagger called that the first drops had started. They had not, but the western cloud had gone the color of dirty steel, and Elsidra had forty minutes of work left.

She put the rig in gear.

* * *

At the roundabout, the paint display showed 8.0 liters remaining.

Elsidra knew that was wrong because she had filled the tank herself. She thumped the display housing, looked at the sight glass on the tank, which showed full, checked the pressure, and leaned out to inspect the line. The white stripe behind her ran clean for six meters, bent into a hooked black-edged mark, then ran straight again.

She stopped so hard the bead dispenser clicked against its mount.

“What did you feed it?” her crew lead asked over the radio.

“Nothing. Hold traffic.”

Elsidra reversed along the closed lane. The mark had clean edges, as if someone had meant it: one short downstroke and a curve biting back toward the house.

Her phone rang. Rovandis.

“Did Caelumar change his first stop?” he asked.

“I’m losing a line to rain, Rov. Ask dispatch.”

“Dispatch asked me. His first scan came up with no destination. So did the second. Three scans, same route, nothing on any of them, and he’s due at the print shop at nine-fifteen. He’s never late to the print shop.”

The paint display changed to 7.0. The tank gave no settling knock. Pressure stayed at one hundred eighty.

Elsidra took a photograph of the hook. When she opened the image, the road was visible, the cones were visible, and the mark sat on blank gray pavement without the roundabout behind it. She lowered the phone.

“Call him.”

Rovandis already had. The connection opened on chain noise and wind.

“Caelumar?” Rovandis said. “Pull over.”

“I am over.” Caelumar sounded irritated, then less certain. “I’m by the... the blue...”

“Shop?”

“No. The tall round thing. They keep feed in it.”

Elsidra knew the old feed silo. Caelumar had used its blue dome since he was twelve to judge the river turn.

“Which side of the river?” she asked.

Silence, except for the tick of his freewheel.

“What river?”

The line behind Elsidra’s rig trembled in its wet edges. The display read 6.0.

Her phone chimed with a second call: Vaudessa. Elsidra merged it with Rovandis’s, and her mother said nothing at all. A sequence of vibrations traveled through the call: six blunt knocks, a pause, then six again.

“Your phone’s against something,” Elsidra said.

“The spike.”

Rovandis spoke over her. “This is a routing outage. Caelumar, give me your coordinates.”

“Screen’s blank.”

“Compass heading, then.”

“I don’t carry a ship, Dad.”

“Your courier app has a compass.”

“Dad. It’s blank. The whole app’s white. I keep looking at it and it’s just white.”

Elsidra watched the fresh hook reach for the unpainted lane as rain began ticking on the windshield.

“Don’t move,” she told Caelumar.

“Aunt Elsie, I’m in a traffic lane. There’s a truck right behind me.”

“Move to safety. Then don’t choose a turn.”

He breathed once into the phone. “Okay. Okay. I’m in the bike lane. I stopped. My light’s on. I think it’s on.”

The count stayed at six.

* * *

They met under the fuel-station canopy three blocks from the work zone. Vaudessa arrived in Rovandis’s van with the wrapped spike across her knees. Elsidra drove the paint rig over herself and left her crew to secure the roundabout and take their break.

Rovandis spread his parcel scanner, two phones, and an old printed route sheet across the van’s hood.

“Network’s up,” he said. “Dispatch can see every driver but him. They can call him, and he picks up, and they still can’t place him. I had them try twice, Elsie. Twice.”

Vaudessa pressed her fingertips into the cardigan. The phone beneath it gave six vibrations.

“Stop doing that,” Rovandis said.

“I’m not.”

He pulled up a photograph. Caelumar stood beside his bike holding a birthday cake badly balanced on the parcel rack. Rovandis tapped the empty distance behind him.

“That was outside our old apartment.”

Elsidra saw Caelumar clearly: hair blown across one eye, knee muddy, smile impatient with the camera. Behind him was a wall with no door, no number, and no street.

“You know him,” Vaudessa said.

Rovandis’s look made her retreat half a step. “Of course I know him.”

“Say what he’s carrying.”

“Cake. Bike lock. The ridiculous green bottle he won’t throw out.”

“Which street was the apartment on?”

“Off the...” Rovandis stopped. “We lived there six years.” He enlarged the blank wall until the pixels broke apart.

Vaudessa unwrapped the spike. “My mother counted everyone across a sill. In and out. So did hers. Four people, four taps. If someone left alone, you waited for them to answer before you shut the door.”

“You said it was courtesy,” Elsidra said.

“It was courtesy. We didn’t know why.” Vaudessa touched the first hook. “There was another part. The spike stays down until the last one out has answered. Something like that.”

“And if it came up first?”

Vaudessa’s thumb stopped.

“Nobody remembered.”

Rovandis picked up his keys. “Then I’m not standing here for it. He’s on my route. I could drive my route asleep.”

The scanner in his hand chirped. A saved stop vanished from the route sheet. The vibration beneath Vaudessa’s palm struck five times.

Rovandis stared at the screen, then at the van.

“Which stop?” Elsidra asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You were looking at it.”

“I know.” He pressed the key fob hard enough to sound the van’s horn. “Get in.”

They tried Rovandis’s route first because he had driven it five days a week for eleven years.

At the first turn, the municipal sign showed bolt holes and a clean green rectangle. Rovandis turned anyway. Two minutes later they passed the same fuel station, though neither Elsidra nor Rovandis remembered making a circle. Vaudessa’s phone struck four times.

Rovandis braked beneath the canopy.

“Again,” he said.

“No.” Elsidra took his printed sheet. The line after DEPOT read three blank spaces and an arrow pointing nowhere. “Every old cue costs one.”

“You don’t know that.”

“We lost the silo. Six. Lost your stop. Five. Lost this turn. Four.”

“Then I use a road I don’t drive.”

He chose one. The van’s map drew a blue line through three buildings and returned them to the same intersection.

Vaudessa’s fingers jerked. Three knocks sounded against the van door.

Rovandis reached for the gearshift again.

Elsidra put her hand over it. “You don’t get another guess.”

His hand stayed on the gearshift under hers. “He’s stopped in traffic, Elsie. He’s stopped in traffic with no route.”

“And every route we already know is feeding that thing.”

Vaudessa tilted the spike. One groove had darkened where her pulse pressed it. “It caught on that turn. And at the sign before.”

Elsidra called Caelumar. Wind battered his microphone now.

“Cael, it’s me. What’s your battery?”

“The bike’s... forty-something. Forty-three. Phone’s nineteen. I keep checking it and it’s still nineteen.”

“What’s the road like?”

“It’s a road. It’s old, there’s a patched bit along the edge. The trucks are really close. I haven’t turned. You said don’t, so I haven’t.”

“Good. That’s right, Cael. Can you see any fresh road paint?”

“No.”

“Anything that wasn’t there this morning?”

Caelumar took time to look. “Temporary sign. Orange back facing me. New trench plates. Someone dumped a blue chair.”

His reporting steadied as the objects became smaller and newer. Elsidra climbed out of the van. Her rig stood where she had left it, at the mouth of the service lane behind the fuel station. Caelumar had never used that lane. Nobody had painted anything on it.

“I’m going to give you a mark,” she said. “Fresh white dash, about a hand wide. It may be nowhere near you. If you see it, flash the bike lamp twice.”

“Where?” Rovandis said. “We can’t tell him where. We don’t know where he is.”

“I don’t know either.” Elsidra was already walking to the rig. “Everything we’ve lost was there this morning. He can still see a chair somebody dumped today. So I paint something new, and we wait for his light.”

She extended the hand sprayer and drew one new dash across the service lane entrance.

For six seconds nothing happened. Then Caelumar said, “White dash,” and in the distance beyond the garages a point of light blinked twice.

Vaudessa counted the vibrations against her palm. Three. She waited and counted again. Still three.

Elsidra started the rig. Her mother was already climbing into the passenger seat with the spike held against her chest. “Rovandis, get your van behind me.”

Rovandis looked down the narrow service lane, then ran for the van.

* * *

Elsidra painted only what did not exist before she reached it.

She added Rovandis to the call and set her phone to speaker on the dash. At each junction she laid a single dash on the safe branch and waited, and Caelumar answered with two white flashes when he saw it. Rovandis followed in the van, using one short horn blast to hold and two to advance. Caelumar’s end of the call had gone mostly to wind, and he was saving the phone, so he answered each instruction with his brake light: steady red for holding, two taps for moving.

They built the route a piece at a time, and no one named a street.

Dispatch called Rovandis twice. The first time he said there was a family emergency. The second time he asked them to keep trucks off any closure with fresh hand marks and hung up before they could demand a permit number.

At the third junction, Vaudessa slapped the dashboard.

“Not left.”

Elsidra held the rig straight. “Why?”

“A hook’s pulling.”

Rovandis’s voice came through the speaker. “Pulling how, Mom? Are you all right?”

“I don’t know. My hand keeps going straight.”

Elsidra painted straight. Far ahead, the bike light answered, and the count stayed at three.

Rain silvered the lane and smeared the edges of every old marking. Elsidra kept the pressure low and the dashes short. At a fork beside a shuttered garden center, Caelumar’s light appeared on the wrong branch, moving away.

Rovandis laid on the horn.

Elsidra leaned toward the phone on the dash. “One. Just one, Rov.”

He released it, waited, and gave one short blast. Caelumar’s brake light burned red.

Vaudessa turned the spike between both palms, flinching as the hooks caught. “Back him up. I’ll say when.”

Rovandis gave three clipped horn taps, the new signal they had chosen for reverse. The brake light vanished, returned, vanished again as Caelumar walked the bike backward. On the sixth pulse, Vaudessa opened her cramped hand.

“There.”

Elsidra painted a dash across the other branch. Caelumar saw it, turned, and answered.

The dash persuaded Rovandis, though it did nothing for his patience.

When Caelumar’s phone battery fell to eight percent, Rovandis pulled around the rig and accelerated toward the next junction.

The road sign there said NINE-LARCH. Elsidra recognized the name, then did not. The letters seemed to drain while she looked at them, leaving a wet green board.

Vaudessa’s phone struck twice.

Rovandis braked sideways across the lane. “I can reach him faster.”

“You just spent one.” Elsidra stopped the rig nose-to-nose with his van. “Back behind the paint.”

“His light is dying.”

“Wait for the next flash.”

“I can’t see him.”

Elsidra pointed through the rain. A faint red pulse appeared beyond the bend, reflecting off the wet trunks rather than shining directly at them. Caelumar had stopped. He was waiting for the next thing they made.

Rovandis reversed.

This time, when Elsidra painted, he did not move until the red light tapped twice.

The road narrowed between a ditch and a line of dark larches. Trucks moved on the far side of a temporary divider, spraying dirty fans over the cones. The sight glass on Elsidra’s tank still showed full. The display held at 2.0.

Then the old centerline was gone. Her headlights found only black asphalt where reflective glass had shone a breath earlier.

The spike twisted in Vaudessa’s hands. Her phone struck once.

Ahead, Caelumar’s white lamp wavered near the ditch.

Rovandis’s voice came from the dash. “What signal?”

Elsidra looked at the road. The shoulder ended at the concrete footing of an old billboard, its board long gone. The traffic lane beyond the divider was no place for a bike. Her sprayer could reach the near edge, but not the far verge where Caelumar waited.

“Hold,” she said.

One short blast. Caelumar’s brake light came on and stayed on.

Vaudessa slid her thumb over the grooves. “It’s tight straight ahead.”

“Straight ahead in the lane or along the ditch?” Rovandis asked, but quietly now.

“Is there a flashlight in here?”

Elsidra took the rig’s flashlight from the door pocket and handed it across.

Vaudessa pressed the light against the spike’s head and rotated the shaft. The hooked grooves threw thin crescents across the cab. Three pointed toward the traffic lane. One lay along the ditch, ending at the concrete edge of the footing.

Elsidra took the flashlight back and climbed down with the hand sprayer. Rain rattled on her hard hat. She walked to the last cone, clipped her safety line to the rig, and stretched as far as the hose allowed. The first paint struck water and feathered away. She lowered the nozzle until it almost touched the road and drew a thick white bar from the lane edge to the footing.

“Advance,” she told Rovandis.

Two horn blasts.

No answer.

“Again?” he asked.

“Wait.”

The display held at 1.0, and they had no useful word for where Caelumar stood. Elsidra could see his lamp only as a weak stain through the trees. She crouched and put one glove on the fresh bar. The paint was cold and gritty with beads. New all the way across.

She blinked the flashlight twice along the white bar.

Caelumar’s lamp answered once, failed, then answered again.

Rovandis gave the two horns.

Red brake light: two taps. Moving.

Vaudessa counted the grooves under her thumb, not aloud. Elsidra kept the flashlight on the paint. Rovandis leaned on the open van door, listening between the passing trucks for the small electric whine of the bike.

It came from the ditch side, stopped, then came again. A front wheel edged into the beam at the footing. Caelumar walked behind it, soaked to the skin, one hand over the dead phone clipped to his handlebar. He did not look at the van. He looked down at the white bar.

Rovandis inhaled to call him.

Elsidra raised one finger.

Two horn blasts.

Caelumar squeezed the brake twice, released it, and pushed the bike across the fresh paint.

The display on Elsidra’s rig held at 1.0.

Rovandis reached him first. He checked Caelumar’s face, shoulders, hands, then the bike as if dispatch required a damage code.

“Anything hurt?”

“Left ankle. Not bad.” Caelumar blinked rain from his eyes. “My charger is in my bag.”

Rovandis caught the back of his jacket and kept hold of it.

Vaudessa stepped down from the rig. The spike shook between her palms hard enough to make its hooked light jump across the road.

“Elsidra.”

Elsidra took the short sledge from the rig’s barrier box. She set the spike across the edge of the billboard footing.

Vaudessa removed her hands. The metal went still. Elsidra struck once, and the spike rang and jumped. Rovandis put his boot on the square head, away from the grooves.

“Again,” he said.

The second strike bent it. The third split the shaft through a pale seam beneath the rust. Vaudessa’s phone gave one loose mechanical buzz in her pocket, the kind it made for a text.

Behind them, the green road sign carried its white letters again.

No one read them aloud.

* * *

Elsidra’s crew lead arrived with the closure truck and an opinion about every unauthorized dash on the route.

“Rain’s ruined half of them,” he said.

“Good,” Elsidra told him. “Cone that footing. We’ll strip the rest in daylight.”

Rovandis wrapped Caelumar’s ankle. Vaudessa collected both halves of the spike in a plastic paint tray without touching the grooves. Caelumar changed his bike battery under the open rear door of the van, then tested the headlamp.

“One flash,” Elsidra said. “Not two. Two means advance.”

He gave her one white blink.

She answered with the rig horn and went to secure the wet line before traffic opened.
