# The Greatest Conquest — provisional direction

The promised pleasure is the reversal from unwanted, shabby inheritance to alarming authority: people who could ruin kingdoms matter-of-factly expect an estranged orc daughter to be their boss. Explicit requirements are the dead notorious chieftain, the daughter, the tavern inside human territory, thirty secret years of neutral ground, and its dangerous regulars' allegiance to her. Earthy comedy with real menace and an emotionally complicated inheritance is my interpretation. The room can stay close while power outside it becomes spectacular. The central living relationship is between the unwilling heir and her incompatible followers; her changing understanding of her dead father runs through it. The payoff should be her first consequential command, followed by a choice to keep this inheritance on terms her father never offered her. Neither a harmless clientele nor forgiveness is required. The supplied original guides her adult appearance and the candlelit timber tavern; it does not impose coziness or a plot. No audience was specified; aim initially for General intensity, without shrinking the action.

Close third through **Harka**, roughly 3,500–4,500 words. Title: **The Greatest Conquest**. Her father is **Borzug**. Proposed principal regulars: **Igrith**, a dragon who takes an elderly human guise indoors; **Veyr**, an assassin with unnervingly literal professional courtesy; and **Dame Cadrel**, a living necromancer whose armies have made her an enemy of the Crown. Their crimes and enmities are real, even if royal accounts exaggerate them. These names have no exact reuse in `stories/NAMES.md`, universe authority, or current `story.md` files. This is an independent magic-active kingdom; no crossover is intended. Cadrel supplies any magic used to move dead bodies; the dead are not independent magic users.

- **The inheritance, and the room.** Harka arrives intending to sell the tavern and leave. She has spent years as a hired scout after refusing the role Borzug chose for her; the estrangement had a specific injury and was not merely a comic misunderstanding. The building is genuinely shabby. The apparently unremarkable mourners are not. A dangerous recognition, then an assassination quarrel interrupted by her instinctive order to sit down, exposes the clientele. Everyone obeys. They ask what she wants done about the royal force approaching to exploit Borzug's death.
- **What she has inherited.** Discoveries arrive through urgent disagreements, not a tour of a benevolent institution: the regulars have brought bitter enemies here for thirty years; Borzug made and enforced the rule that quarrels could enter but could not be finished under this roof. Each accepted his right to name a successor because all needed a place the others could not take from them. Their allegiance is voluntary and already offered to Harka, not a spell forcing obedience or a test she has to pass before anyone considers her boss. He really was a ruthless chieftain. This refuge does not acquit him as a father. Harka's command experience matters once the astonishing force at her disposal becomes clear.
- **The offer.** A royal commander surrounds the tavern and offers Harka money and safe passage in exchange for the patrons. The Crown has tolerated the place because Borzug could retaliate; the commander thinks the heir cannot hold his enemies together. The patrons readily offer destruction. Harka could sell the inheritance she came to sell, abandon people who have placed themselves in her hands, or become her father's kind of conqueror. The real danger is not merely damaged premises: the patrons' retaliation could start the war this room has helped postpone.
- **Her first campaign.** Harka rejects both surrender and indiscriminate retaliation. She gives specific interdependent orders: the dragon takes her full size and disables the siege weapons; Cadrel's living-directed dead pin the force without slaughter; Veyr brings its commander alive across the contested ground. The assassin needs the other two to make the abduction possible. Harka goes into the exposed yard herself to hold the timing and take responsibility rather than safely outsourcing all danger. The regulars' powers decisively change what is possible, and their willingness to obey her creates the victory. Keep action intelligible and non-graphic, with the incongruity of an innkeeper commanding calamities.
- **Neutral ground cuts both ways.** Once the commander is inside, Harka forbids the patrons to hurt him. Some want revenge; obeying this order costs them something. She makes him negotiate the withdrawal and safe passage of the guests from a seat among people he meant to destroy. Her authority protects even this enemy; the tavern cannot survive as merely a faction's headquarters. She has made a choice the patrons can recognize and must live with, without making all of them good.
- **The seat she chooses.** After the force withdraws, a practical question from one of the monsters makes plain that they expect her to stay. She chooses to hear tomorrow's dangerous business rather than sell. Leave room for one concrete, imperfect recollection of Borzug, elicited by what she has just done, which alters her grief without supplying absolution. End in her first evening as their actual boss: the terrible guests wait on her decision, and she takes the seat freely. The closing beat should have warmth and a remaining edge, not a renovation plan or a declaration that everybody is family now.

Strongest possibility: the same order that unleashes spectacular power must then restrain it on behalf of the defeated enemy, making the neutral-ground revelation drive both climax and ending. Largest risk: a stock royal attack could crowd out the odd, dangerous intimacy of these people expecting her to govern them. Keep their competing desires present through the campaign, and keep the father's failure specific enough that his secret work cannot erase it.
