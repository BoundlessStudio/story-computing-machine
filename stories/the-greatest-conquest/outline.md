# The Greatest Conquest — provisional direction

The promised pleasure is the reversal from unwanted, shabby inheritance to alarming authority: people who could ruin kingdoms matter-of-factly expect an estranged orc daughter to be their boss. Explicit requirements are the dead notorious chieftain, the daughter, the tavern inside human territory, thirty secret years of neutral ground, and its dangerous regulars' allegiance to her. Earthy comedy with real menace and an emotionally complicated inheritance is my interpretation. The room can stay close while power outside it becomes spectacular. The central living relationship is between the unwilling heir and her incompatible followers; her changing understanding of her dead father runs through it. The payoff should be her first consequential command, followed by a choice to keep this inheritance on terms her father never offered her. Neither a harmless clientele nor forgiveness is required. The supplied original guides her adult appearance and the candlelit timber tavern; it does not impose coziness or a plot. No audience was specified; aim initially for General intensity, without shrinking the action.

Close third through **Harka**, roughly 3,500–4,500 words. Title: **The Greatest Conquest**. Her father is **Borzug**. Proposed principal regulars: **Igrith**, a dragon who takes an elderly human guise indoors; **Veyr**, an assassin with unnervingly literal professional courtesy; and **Dame Cadrel**, a living necromancer whose armies have made her an enemy of the Crown. Their crimes and enmities are real, even if royal accounts exaggerate them. These names have no exact reuse in `stories/NAMES.md`, universe authority, or current `story.md` files. This is an independent magic-active kingdom; no crossover is intended. Cadrel supplies any magic used to move dead bodies; the dead are not independent magic users.

- **The inheritance, and the room.** Harka arrives intending to sell the tavern and leave. Early, establish the specific estrangement: Borzug pledged her in a political marriage to secure a mountain pass; when she refused he told the envoys she would come round. She left that night and became a hired scout. The building is genuinely shabby. Brief roadside or knightly prejudice can sharpen the hostility she expected. The apparently unremarkable mourners are not. A dangerous recognition, then an assassination quarrel interrupted by her instinctive order to sit down, exposes the clientele. Everyone obeys. They ask what she wants done about the royal force approaching to exploit Borzug's death.
- **What she has inherited.** Discoveries arrive through urgent disagreements, not a tour of a benevolent institution: the regulars have brought bitter enemies here for thirty years; Borzug enforced the rule that quarrels could enter but could not be finished under this roof. Each accepted his right to name a successor because all needed a place the others could not take from them. He named Harka before them last winter. Their allegiance is voluntary and already offered, not a spell or a test of her worth. They will accept her refusal, though the refuge would then dissolve. His choice recognizes her ability while again deciding her life without consulting her. The refuge does not acquit him as a father, and she need not invent a morality he lacked to claim it on her own terms.
- **The offer.** A royal commander surrounds the tavern and offers Harka money and safe passage in exchange for the patrons. The Crown tolerated the place because Borzug could retaliate; the commander thinks the heir cannot hold his enemies together. The patrons offer destruction, with incompatible private aims. Harka could sell the inheritance she came to sell and buy her freedom by disposing of other people's futures, the very injury she left her father over. The real danger is not merely damaged premises: retaliation could start the war this room has helped postpone.
- **Her first campaign.** Her scout's eye catches concealed siege pieces and a reserve in the orchard; the visible force is bait. She coordinates limited interdependent orders from a dangerous yard position where she can see both lines and signal their timing. The dragon takes her full size and disables the siege weapons; Cadrel's living-directed dead pin the force without slaughter; Veyr needs those openings to bring its commander alive across the contested ground. Lesser dangerous guests hold exits and protect one another. Cadrel wants this particular officer dead; Igrith wants to burn the royal standard; Veyr dislikes giving back a captive. Their powers decisively change events, but these remain people making choices. Keep the action intelligible and non-graphic, with the incongruity of an innkeeper commanding calamities.
- **Neutral ground cuts both ways.** At the threshold Harka makes Veyr release the commander and forbids all threats inside. He names the coercion. She acknowledges his capture, guarantees his safe departure, and steps out of his way before asking him to negotiate. Protection constrains her own treatment too: she has surrendered a useful hostage, to the patrons' anger. He chooses terms because his intact troops are pinned and another assault would face the combined force. Publicly witnessed safe passage and the continuing credible deterrent sustain withdrawal; this is a settlement, not permanent peace. Her authority protects even this enemy, without making all the guests good.
- **The seat she chooses.** After withdrawal she elects to hear tomorrow's dangerous business rather than sell. Her appetite and ability for command, not a posthumous obligation, make the choice hers. Let a specific imperfect recollection of Borzug, elicited by what she has done, alter her grief without supplying absolution. End in her first evening as their actual boss: the terrible guests wait on her decision, including one she has just thwarted, and she takes the seat freely. Warmth with a remaining edge, not a renovation plan or a declaration that everybody is family.

Strongest possibility: the same order that unleashes spectacular power must then restrain it on behalf of the defeated enemy, making the neutral-ground revelation drive both climax and ending. Largest risk: a stock royal attack could crowd out the odd, dangerous intimacy of these people expecting her to govern them. Keep their competing desires present through the campaign, and keep the father's failure specific enough that his secret work cannot erase it.
