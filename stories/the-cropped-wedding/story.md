# At the Edge of the Frame

By the time the bride asked Vera to remove the groom from every photograph, Vera had already taken seventeen pictures of him trying to free the bride's train from a folding chair.

The chair belonged to the community hall. Its metal foot had found a loop of lace and was holding on with the stubbornness of an old tenant. Oscar knelt in his good trousers while Meredith stood on one leg and told the guests gathering around them that this was the first dance. Vera had been close enough to hear the snap when the lace came free. In the last photograph of the sequence, Oscar looked up at Meredith, triumphant, with a pale loop of thread on his thumb.

Now Meredith had followed Vera into the little kitchen behind the hall. The caterers were assembling trays of stuffed mushrooms. Meredith shut the door as far as the cable running under it would allow.

"When you do the pictures," she said, "could you crop Oscar out of all of them?"

Vera had a memory card between her teeth. She took it out. "All of which pictures?"

"Every picture. The ones you send me, I mean."

Through the door came a burst of applause. Someone had found the guest book, or lost it.

"I can make you a selection of just you," Vera said. "I took some while you were getting ready. And I can take more now."

"Those are mostly me putting on a shoe."

"A very complicated shoe."

Meredith didn't smile. She rubbed the red mark left by the chair leg on the back of her calf. "Faye asked if she could have pictures of today for her room. She said she wanted pictures of me, not the wedding. I thought I could make her an album."

Faye was Meredith's daughter, twelve, in a green dress she had picked herself. Vera had photographed her that morning standing in front of Meredith with a hairpin between her lips, her hands working at the veil. In the picture, Meredith's eyes were on the mirror; Faye's were on the pin. They had been easy together until the ceremony began.

"I can do an album," Vera said.

"Without Oscar. In every picture." Meredith heard herself and winced. "I'm not asking you to erase the whole man. Just the version she has to put on a shelf."

Vera slid the card into its case. She could have said that a crop was not an eraser. It couldn't remove Oscar's hand from Meredith's shoulder or give back the section of chair hidden behind his knee. Instead she said, "Let me see what I have. I'll take a few of you and Faye after the speeches."

"She'll hate that."

"I'll ask her."

Meredith looked toward the door, where the applause had become a rhythmic chant. "You'll ask her better than I did."

Vera was not sure that was true. She had met Faye six hours ago, and Faye had already asked whether the camera's flash could set off the smoke alarm.

When Vera came out, the bride's mother was holding a pair of serving tongs over her head like a trophy. The missing guest book was under the mushroom trays. Vera photographed the tongs going down. It would be the last picture she took before the groom made his request.

Oscar found her by the coat hooks, where she was changing lenses. He held a small plate with one mushroom on it, as if bringing proof that dinner had begun.

"I need to ask you something," he said. "About the pictures. Can you take Meredith out of mine? All of them?"

Vera stopped turning the lens. He waited while she looked at him.

"Why?" she asked.

"Jules's mother has asked for some photos of us." He lifted the plate a fraction. "Jules and me. I want him to have some that aren't a conversation with her."

Jules was thirteen. At the ceremony he had stood beside Oscar in a blue jacket too warm for the room. When the rings were passed, he had held out the open box to Meredith without looking up. Vera had taken that picture too.

"I can give you photographs of you and Jules," she said. "There are a few already."

"I know you're supposed to give us the whole day. I'm asking for my set, if that's possible." He glanced toward the tables. "If there's a fee for two galleries, that's fine."

He spoke as though the extra fee was the part he could bear to negotiate. Vera remembered Meredith offering no fee at all. She had been too embarrassed to think of one.

"I'll see what's possible," Vera said.

Oscar nodded and ate the mushroom in one bite. It was much too hot. He made a face, apologized to nobody, and went back into the hall.

Vera took a photograph of the coat hooks. One held a child's white cardigan. The other eleven held wet overcoats, although it had stopped raining at noon. There was room for everyone who had come, but not much room for anyone to move.

She had a shot list in her bag. The couple had made it together, in a spreadsheet with generous margins and a column headed *People who will leave early*. There were fourteen family combinations, one picture of the cake before anyone cut it, and a request for a photograph of their hands during the vows. Nothing on the list suggested they wanted to be taken out of each other's day.

At dinner Vera worked through the list. She put Oscar with his aunt and his father in the blue hall by the toilets, where a window provided the least unkind light. She took Meredith with her mother and Faye outside beside the bicycle rack. Faye stood beside her grandmother, leaving a conspicuous person-sized gap between herself and Meredith.

"A little closer," Meredith's mother said.

"I'm close enough," Faye said.

Vera took the picture before anybody could make it worse. Then she lowered the camera.

"I've got the formal one. Do you want one that's just you two?" she asked Faye.

Faye looked at her mother. "Not if I have to smile."

"You don't."

Meredith's mother went inside. Faye stayed by the bicycle rack while Meredith came over. There was still an arm's length between them. Vera could see a smear of green ink on Faye's wrist. Meredith saw it too, and reached for a tissue in her dress pocket. Faye took the tissue and cleaned her own wrist. Vera made one picture, then another after Meredith stopped holding out the empty hand.

Inside, Oscar was telling Jules's mother on the phone where to find the parking lot. She had come to collect him early, as agreed, and was waiting at the wrong entrance. Jules listened from a few feet away, coat half on. Vera couldn't hear the woman on the phone. She heard Oscar say, "Yes, he ate," and then, "Yes, he's got his things."

Jules looked at Vera's camera. "Did you get the cake?"

"Before anybody touched it."

"Good." He zipped his coat. "He made it. The icing, I mean."

Oscar lowered the phone. "She found the door."

Vera pointed at the strip of light beneath the exit sign. "I can take one of you together on your way out, if you want."

"All right," Oscar said.

"Don't make me stand in front of that sign," Jules said. "It'll look like I'm escaping."

"I was going to suggest the coat hooks."

"Worse."

They settled beside the noticeboard, where last month's raffle winners were still pinned under plastic. Oscar put a hand behind Jules's back, then let it fall. Jules stepped in and pressed his shoulder against his father's arm. Vera took three frames. In the third, Oscar looked toward the door because a car horn sounded outside, and Jules looked straight at the camera with the exhausted patience of someone whose parents had been congratulated all afternoon.

When the car came, Oscar walked him out. Vera stayed inside. She had promised Meredith a picture after the speeches, and the speeches were starting without her.

The best man's notes were written on the back of a parking ticket. He had lost his proper speech. His new one lasted forty seconds and included a story about a refrigerator Oscar had once carried up four flights of stairs. Meredith's mother gave a speech about the years when Meredith would repair a broken thing before announcing it was broken. Neither speech turned the couple into a lesson. People laughed where they knew the people being described.

As the room settled, Faye climbed onto a chair to see over the heads in front of her. The chair shifted. Oscar caught its back before she could fall. She stepped down without looking at him, then pushed the chair closer to the table so nobody else would trip over it. Vera had raised the camera when Oscar moved. She did not take the picture. In her head she had already sorted the moment out of both private galleries.

She stood there with her finger on the shutter, irritated by the fact that the day was refusing to be as sortable as her folders.

After the speeches she found Meredith and Oscar together near the cake. Each was speaking to someone else: Meredith to a cousin in a yellow coat, Oscar to the caterer with the knife. They stood so close that when Oscar moved his elbow, Meredith moved her glass without looking. Vera could have photographed them from the waist up and cropped either out later. She took the whole frame.

The knife was handed over. The couple cut the cake badly, producing a wedge as thin as a playing card and a slab large enough for four. The guests urged them to feed each other. Oscar took the thin piece; Meredith, the slab. She held it up until he laughed and bit a corner. He got icing on his collar. He tried to return the favor and dropped his piece onto his own shoe. He looked down at the damage.

Faye, who had been watching from the door, laughed hard enough to lean against the wall. Meredith saw her and laughed too. Vera took a wide shot with the doorway in it.

"You're still here," Meredith said when Vera lowered the camera.

"You booked me until nine."

"It's nine?"

"Eight forty. I have a photograph of the clock if you need one."

Meredith reached for a napkin to wipe Oscar's collar. Oscar waved it away and got a second napkin himself. Both were smiling, but neither seemed to know where to put a hand. Vera had seen wedding couples who could not stop touching and couples who performed each touch for the lens. These two had been easy when nobody had asked them to demonstrate anything.

"I want to check something with both of you," Vera said.

They turned toward her. She had planned separate conversations, phrased gently enough that neither would know the other had made a request. She had waited too long to have them.

"You've each asked me for a gallery with the other person cropped out," she said.

"Each?" Meredith said.

Oscar looked at her. "You asked for me to be taken out of every picture?"

"Only the ones she sends me." Meredith glanced at Vera, then back at him. "You asked too?"

Vera held the camera against her chest. "I should have asked before bringing up your requests together. I'm sorry."

Oscar set down his napkin without wiping his collar. "Jules's mother asked for pictures of him and me. I asked Vera for all of mine."

"Faye asked for pictures of me," Meredith said. She folded the icing-stained napkin in quarters. "I asked for all of mine."

Neither reached for the other. Vera turned the camera so they could see the last photograph: Oscar looking down at his shoe, Meredith laughing beside him, Faye laughing in the doorway.

"I'll keep this in the full gallery," Vera said. "I can take separate pictures for your other sets. I have twenty minutes."

Oscar glanced at the cake. "I'm going to need a different shoe."

"The shoe can stay in the picture," Vera said.

Meredith smiled at the shoe, then looked again at the little screen. "Can we see what you've already taken before we decide what to send the kids?"

"Of course. I'll send proofs to the two of you first. Nothing goes to anyone else without your say."

"Good," Oscar said. He pressed the stained toe of his shoe on a napkin, accomplishing very little. "Could we get one of Faye with Meredith before she leaves?"

"We have one," Vera said. "We can take another if Faye wants."

Meredith glanced toward the doorway, where Faye was now helping a small boy balance three slices of cake on one plate. "Ask her."

Faye agreed to one photograph on the condition that she could keep the plate. The small boy, deprived of his cargo, followed them anyway. Vera set mother and daughter near the window. Oscar stood out of sight by the cake with the boy, retrieving fallen crumbs from his jacket. Faye held the plate at hip level. Meredith stood beside her, hands at her sides.

"You can eat," Vera told Faye.

"Then you'll get me chewing."

"That's possible."

Faye considered the bargain and took a forkful. Meredith laughed, not at the chewing but at the long, appraising look Faye gave the cake. Vera caught Faye with her fork held out toward her mother. Meredith bent to accept it. There was no room in that frame for Oscar, though he stood only six feet away.

Oscar got his own portrait with the remains of the cake after the guests began to leave. He asked Vera to make the icing on his collar visible. He said Jules would believe that more readily than a picture of him in a clean suit. Vera put the cake stand in front of him and took the photograph, then one with Meredith's mother carrying away the tongs in the background. She did not tell her to move.

On Monday, Vera sat at her desk and made the requested selections. The picture by the bicycle rack caught Faye with her eyes half closed; the one at the window had a serving spoon appearing to grow from Meredith's shoulder. Vera chose around those defects. The cake picture stayed in the full gallery, as she'd promised.

The gallery for Oscar and Jules had only five photographs. Vera almost apologized for the number. Then she looked at the one beside the raffle board. Five were enough to choose from. She sent the couple a proof link with three folders: the full day, Faye, and Jules. The message said only that they could select which photographs, if any, to share.

Two days later Meredith chose the window portrait for Faye's room. Oscar picked the raffle-board picture for Jules and the cake portrait for Jules's mother. He asked if Vera could leave the noticeboard legible. Jules had won the raffle the month before. His prize had been a slow cooker neither household had wanted; it was now in Oscar's kitchen.

Vera enlarged the picture to check the printing. Behind the two of them, on a blue card held by a thumbtack, Jules's name was written in thick black marker. The letters stayed sharp. Jules's coat pressed flat against Oscar's sleeve at the shoulder; below it, Oscar's hand hung an inch from the coat pocket. She ordered the print at the size Oscar had asked for.
