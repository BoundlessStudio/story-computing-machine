# The Reach of a Bell

I used to ring a bell once and catch the clapper before it swung back. No second note, no little iron hiccup at the bottom of the swing. I liked getting it exactly right. When I got good enough to do it from the saddle, there was an innkeeper in the south of Brevast who worked out which rider was responsible and rang a handbell outside my window before first light. I used to tell the recruits that story. The ones who laughed were generally the ones who wanted me to show them the trick.

Lammet's bell had gone green, though when it swung up it showed a clean stripe of bronze around the rim. I'd given it a pull for my own pleasure a dozen times, but the morning the mountain came down I was trying to get off my horse. She and I were in disagreement about whether she'd carry me uphill with a sore place under her girth. We'd come out of the gorge without meeting a soul, so I'd been talking to her as if there were a chance she might see my side of it. The wind up from the square smelled of the fleece they'd washed at the first thaw. Then we both got a look at the sheep coming down into the village, and the horse took two steps backward without either of us consulting the other.

The wool was still wet on the racks. A man saw the sheep coming, set his load of fleece down in the road and spread his arms. In my part of the country you can turn a flock like that, if they're merely going someplace. The first one took him square in the knees and the next three cleared him on the ground. I remember how slowly he got up. His blue sleeve had torn loose at the shoulder and he stood looking for a hole in it with his good hand. Above him an uprooted pine came over the skyline sideways. For a breath I could see light through the roots.

I reached into the mountain far enough to feel the ledge start to break with the weight of half the slope bearing on it. The bolts of a siege gate felt like that inside the wood an instant before they bent. In the companies they teach you to hold up a weight for as long as you've got the strength, and when you let it go it ought to come down the way it always has. Change how a thing falls under its own weight and up you go to the High Court to have the magic taken out of you. I let go of the reins and put my feet on the ground.

The clapper was a hundred yards away. As soon as I reached for it I could feel every pound of its weight, the small end of the pin where it hung and the give on either side of the pin when I pulled. I hit the bell hard, let the clapper swing back and hit it again. When I came into the square a boy was standing under the wool racks holding a piece of bread. He watched the top of the tower as he ate, and the next time he put the bread to his mouth he forgot to bite.

A woman had hauled the man in the blue coat under a trestle. She came out to meet me with a hank of wool round her boot and caught my forearm. “Go to the top houses first. The old people are up there and they've shut themselves in against the cold. They'll never hear the bell through those shutters.”

“Get everybody in the square down flat. Pull down anyone who tries to run.”

“You'll have to go up there, then. My father hears that bell twice a year, when we get him out in a chair.”

The road ran the length of Lammet on a shelf above the sheep pastures. At one end of the village it curved up past her father's house, directly under the broken ledge. At the other end I could already see stones shooting across the stretch I'd ridden in on from the gorge. One or two people tried the slope down to the pastures and came crawling back when the stones went over the edge at their feet. All I could see of the top houses now was a series of chimneys with a solid mass of earth and timber gathering behind them.

I caught the woman's hand against my arm. “I can hold the slide from here. If I start up to your father, the whole thing will come down before I reach his door. Help me get people off the road.”

She let go of me to catch a boy by the collar of his shirt as he ran past her. “Under the racks with you. Leave the wool be.” She put him down by the man under the trestle and got the next person by an arm. I planted my boots in the wet wool and bore against the front of the slide. The belt under my ribs had a buckle with a cracked tongue. I leaned into it so hard I heard the tongue give. There was so much weight in my hands that when my boot began to slip I thought it was the ground. The first stones stopped level with the chimney tops. Behind them the slide kept coming, and everything I held began to push up at the middle.

I could still force the bottom back and buy Lammet a few seconds. A few seconds would get nobody off the shelf. Off to one side I could see the near lip of the gorge. I'd seen nobody on the track down at the bottom when I rode in, and now I tried to count the minutes it had taken me to get from there to the square, as if I could tell from that how far along the track somebody behind me would have come. While I counted, the pines at the back of the slide started tipping toward the roofs.

The first time they'd put a stone in my hand to teach me the weight of it, I'd wanted to know how it would feel if I changed the way it fell. I swept my braid off my face, took my strength off the front of the slide and turned the pull of the loose rock toward the gorge.

The stones that had been uppermost went first. They sped crosswise along the slope and swept over the lip of the gorge. Then the ledge gave all at once, and the whole moving face of the mountain went after those first stones. Pines went wheeling up with the earth that still held their roots. A strip of turf as long as the village went out flat over the ravine, and hung there long enough that I saw its underside shining with the thaw. When the last rock had cleared the lip, I let the ordinary pull take it. We heard it land far below us. I listened till all I could hear was the shutters knocking in their hinges. A thin line of slate came sliding off the roof of the weaving shed. I could smell sap and cold earth.

In the quiet that came over the square, a man cried out from one of the top houses. The woman with the wool round her boot threw back her head to answer him. More people began to call, and as she recognized the voices she started to run. Even then she needed both hands to get the wool loose.

I stayed where I could see the top houses until the woman came down the hill with her father's chair between her forearms and her father behind her, using the wall to walk. By afternoon every house had sent somebody down to the square to give an account of the people in it.

Everybody in Lammet was alive.

In the gorge the stone had settled in a ridge tall enough to catch the sun. In the square, where the tower cut off the light, you could still see wet wool on the racks.

* * *

It took a week for the High Court to convict me. The third day they put a man in the witness chair who had measured the new ridge in the gorge. He had done it by walking from one end to the other along the crest. The judge kept asking if the slide could have uncovered a spine of rock that had been there all along. In the end the man had to tell him three times where he'd found the roots of the trees.

They kept me in a room off the pass road with soldiers my own age to watch the door. I lay awake the night before they took me to the arcade. There was a great deal of running I could have done from that room, if I'd started by putting both soldiers down. One of them used to bring his deck of cards after the watch changed and let me have my pick of the bent ones. They walked me up the road in my red cloak. I'd scrubbed the dirt off the star the night before. The cloth around it was still wet.

The High Court has three arches opening on the valley. At the end of a hearing the people who run livestock through the pass put their feet up on the rail and reckon what the judge has cost them a head. From the middle arch it's a clear mile down to Lammet. The ground had shaken hard enough to open a crack all the way across the low parapet next to it. Higher up, a bar of iron had been taking the wobble out of that arch since before I was born. I could feel it working as soon as I came out of the stair. There was a little tug on one end when the wind hit the stone, and a longer pull through the middle as it settled.

Several of my company had come and found a place to stand against the rail. They had the wind off the valley on the backs of their coats. A boy I'd taught since winter kept his collar up with one hand.

The judge was waiting with Rinne Cade, the royal executioner. When the wind caught the dark blue banners behind them you could see the gold stars whole and hear the brass rings tick against the stone. A clerk had put a clean little stool out there and polished the court table. The wax smelled sweet as a little crowd came up behind me with slate dust on its boots.

The judge was a gray man whose beard went down between his cloak fastenings. He read the court's warrant. The High Court had found Ivet Serr guilty of setting aside the natural order and sentenced her to be stripped of her magic. He folded up the warrant and kept it on the table by his elbow.

Rinne wore her star on a white cloak. She had the hard crossed belts they gave you when you went into the companies. There was a fold in hers where mine had one. She tightened the upper belt without looking down and I put my hand to the same place on my own. Her dark curls were pinned back at the temples, with the rest loose down her back. When the wind put one in her eyes she pushed it away and I caught the gold stitching on her cuff. There were two deep lines between her brows, even before she had looked up at me.

Rinne took a second paper from a pocket under her cloak and brought it over for me to see. I knew the seal the king used for his companies. The order had my name and the number of my company and a space for the clerk to put in a date. Her voice carried easily enough for the judge to hear even when a banner hit the stone. “Beg the king's mercy here where your men can hear. Swear you'll leave the natural order as you find it the next time you stand where you stood at Lammet. You'll go back to the company with all your magic. You have until I take the last of it.”

She handed it to the clerk who was setting his ink out. I had known the king might spare me. I'd allowed myself to picture being told so, in front of every one of those people. They would have seen me take up my place again, and I would have been grateful enough to go out and post the night watch myself for the rest of the spring. I had spent the better part of the week thinking of what day I might be back with the company and much less of it thinking how I might get there.

“The boy at the rail is one of mine,” I said. “What's he supposed to do when it's his turn and he's got a village in the way of a mountain?”

“He can put whatever strength he can summon between the village and the mountain. I want him to hear from you where it has to end.”

“Walk over here.” Rinne came the two steps with me to the open arch. We could see the break in the mountain and the upper roofs beneath it. “You see where the road goes behind the first house? The ledge came loose above it. If we'd had a minute we could have got two or three people past there. Your oath would have left Lammet under the fall.”

Rinne looked where I was pointing. From up here you could see more of the road than you ever could at the bell. It went from one end of the village to the other, with the short turn up to the far houses and the strip I had ridden by the gorge. The distance had made all the dangerous points look like places a person might pass in a few good strides.

“I served in the king's companies before I came here,” she said. “At Heddon I was sent up to raise a flag once our men were clear of the siege road. The commander meant to make the road give under the first enemy tower to come that way. I saw our last wagon come round the turn. It had men clinging all the way down both sides and I took it for granted that they'd picked up everyone. So I raised the flag. The commander had it in plain sight. He changed the ground so it would bear no weight. I heard from the wagoner afterwards that three men from my own file had gone back for our corporal, who'd been sick all morning and had sat down under the bank beside the road. None of the four came back up. I stood in this arcade to give evidence. The men who'd got out on the wagon crowded at the rail the day the court took the commander's magic.”

She watched the place where the road ran out of sight again behind the far houses. I found myself trying to see if it was the kind of bank you could lean on with your coat doubled up under your head, and how long he might have been sitting there when he heard them come back for him.

“I came down the gorge on horseback that morning without passing a soul,” I said. “I thought about another horse coming in behind me. I couldn't see the track once I got to the square, and I couldn't hold the slide long enough to send for somebody who could.”

“I was certain when I raised the flag,” she said. “My corporal had every reason to believe the ground would hold him up. A man riding into that gorge would have had every reason to believe the stones would come down the ordinary way. What are you asking the boy to learn from you about that man?”

“I'd tell the boy who I saw and who I had to guess about,” I said. “He got off a dry wagon once to lead a stranger's mare over a ford and came back aboard with both boots full of water. He'd think of the rider. I'd tell him I held on as long as I could with those chimneys in front of me, and I turned the slide knowing I might bury somebody in the gorge. Put yourself at the bell with the stones over the roofs. Tell me what you'd do.”

Rinne drew the cuff of her white sleeve back off her wrist. “I would have gone on bearing against the slide for as long as I could hold it. It would have buried those houses. I am asking you to take an oath I would have to keep myself.”

“There was an old man up there who couldn't hear that bell,” I said. “I watched his daughter bring his chair down after the slide. He came down behind her with his hand on the wall. It took him the better part of an hour and I stood in the square until he got to me.”

Rinne followed the road down with her eyes to where it widened around the tower. She ran her thumb along the fold she'd made in her cuff and took her hand off the rail.

The soldier who'd let me pick the bent cards was sitting at the end of the bench, folding his cap between his hands. When I went back to the place they'd left open under the stars he set the cap down and stood up.

“My men have heard me,” I told her. “I have no oath to give you.”

She went to the table and took the stool from where I might have sat. She set it well out of the space between us. When she came to stand before me I could see it near the rail, just behind the loose corner of her cloak. I heard the clerk lay his pen down. She stood far enough away that I could see the whole spread of her hand as she raised it.

“Put one foot farther back,” she said. “You'll have more chance of staying up.”

I wanted to tell her I knew how to stay up. She pulled before I'd had time to say it. My cloak lifted at the front and my breath came out with it. Her hand was a full pace from me, so for the first few beats I couldn't credit how quickly she'd found a hold. I felt the first strand draw through the place where I would have laid a hand to count my heart. It came out gold against the wool. Under it another strand was drawing tight, and as it came free I could see it turn in the air toward the hollow of her palm.

I tried to put what strength I had left against her wrist. I caught a little weight just past it, and when I shoved, the stool came out from behind her cloak and struck the rail on its side. The sound came back off all three arches. Rinne had to put a foot back and bend her knees, and I could see the cuff jerk back down over her wrist. Her fingers stayed open as she drew her hand back toward her chest.

I reached for the weight I'd felt all morning in the iron above us and got a flicker of the short tug at the end where it entered the stone, and when I waited for the long pull through the middle I could almost have had it again if something at my breast hadn't pulled loose.

I was still looking at the same iron.

Rinne had stopped moving her hand. I looked back down from the iron. Her brows were still drawn together and her eyes were still on my face. “Ask now, Ivet,” she said. “Ask for mercy and let the clerk hear you. You'll go back with your men.”

I had a good idea what the company would say when we got out on the pass road. The boy would let go of his collar long enough to shake my hand and ask if I could still ring a bell through a stone wall. I might reach ahead of us to the old wooden bridge and feel the cracked plank give a little under the first man across. I could make them go over one at a time the way I'd done the winter before, even with the men behind laughing to keep me from seeing how afraid they were. There would always be some reason to lay my hand on a weight a long way off. Rinne's fingers were still spread. I could get the feeling back for the price of a few breaths over the clerk's paper, and put off thinking about Lammet until the next time there was a Lammet.

My braid had caught against the star on my shoulder. I could see a few red hairs lifted along the gold. I raised my chin to get it loose and the force took my head back farther than I meant to let it go. The skin over my heart felt as if it must come away next. I closed my eyes. I had to open my mouth to make a passage for the little air I could get in, and while I stood there with my mouth open I thought how easily I could turn that into the beginning of a word. It took me two tries to let the full breath out.

“If I had that ground to stand on again,” I told Rinne, “I'd send the mountain into the gorge. I'd save them again.”

Her hand moved another inch. Light came up under my eyelids, and a sound got out of me that made me expect the hand to stop. Rinne kept drawing. I could hear the rings of the banners ticking against the stone through all the time she took to find the last of it. When I opened my eyes the threads ran straight from my chest to her bare hand, trembling there where anyone from the back of the arcade could see them. Rinne drew her hand in to her own breast and the far end of the last thread came out of me. She held it in the air until I saw the light go out.

Rinne closed her hand and lowered it. The lines between her brows were still there. I tried to go to the rail and got two steps before my legs gave. The boy came away from his place and caught my arm. He was still hanging on to his collar with his other hand. By the time I got my balance the loose side of it had folded over against his neck. He gave up on it and took hold of me with both hands.

We went over to the opening. More of the valley had come into the sun. You could see the top of every house below the break in the mountain. Beside the village the new ridge cast a shadow in the bottom of the gorge. I could see the road where I'd stood to ring the bell.

I put my hands on the iron rail. Down in Lammet they were ringing noon. I saw the bell turn out of the shadow in its tower and thought to catch the clapper. I reached and felt the cold of the rail straight through my palms. The bell turned again and caught the light a second time before I heard the first stroke.
