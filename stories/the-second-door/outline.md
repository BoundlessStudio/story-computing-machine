# The Second Door

Provisional direction; the finished prose may find a better route.

Della is an amateur stage magician who has finally secured a paid evening at the shabby Bellwether variety theatre. She wants to revive the cabinet act she built with Sahar, her former performing partner. Sahar left after too many evenings of being announced as Della's assistant; she now has her own skilful act and has agreed to discuss one shared booking. Della has lived with dissociative identity disorder for years. Amos and June share that life, sometimes with overlapping awareness and sometimes with missing time. Both have existing relationships with Sahar. Amos misses a particular garden and its keeper; June loves noisy radio serials, has been teaching herself to sing, and has applied for a separate singing audition at this theatre without advertising any power.

A recent unexplained light over the city has given many residents powers. Della can hold a small object motionless in the air. June can make her voice or another nearby sound audible on the other side of a solid wall. Amos can make a door open onto another doorway he knows personally, but must keep a hand on the source handle to sustain it. Letting go, or ceasing to be the identity sustaining it, restores the source door's normal destination. People and things can cross; a completed crossing leaves them at the other place when the passage ends. Amos cannot take their whole body through while keeping that hand at the source. We will establish this through a small, intelligible experiment rather than a lecture, and will not place anyone halfway through a closing portal.

Della sees a chance to make the old cabinet trick astonishing and to persuade Sahar that this time they will both matter. The impresario offers a much better fee for a real disappearance and return, then advertises the booking around three powers in one body. Della accepts too quickly. Sahar objects partly because the promised return requires Amos to remain present, which Della cannot promise, and partly because Della has again decided what Sahar's job will be. Della discovers June's audition application and her explicit refusal to supply a sound effect for the trio billing. A funny, consequential private demonstration will establish June's sound power without making her a summoned cue. Amos wants contact with the distant garden, but the hand-on-handle rule means he can only hold its door open and look: he cannot visit it himself. That impossibility is faced, not patched. No rehearsal schedule makes switching an on-demand technique.

Rehearsal establishes the portal with a deliberate release test, then exposes the ordinary discontinuity on which the advertised trick cannot rely. Sahar crosses into a theatre service room; when the passage changes, she remains there and takes the ordinary route back. The return is long because fixed scenery obstructs the short backstage corridor. This is broken timing, not a rescue crisis, and Amos's wanting the garden does not cause a convenient switch. Sahar spots that repositioning the cabinet beside that corridor and restoring its concealed side exit would recover their original ordinary illusion. Her craft makes a viable performance regardless of who is present.

Della initiates the change while the advertised performance is still available, before an audience can validate it. She renegotiates against the impresario's preference, removes the three-miracle promise, and gives up the enlarged fee. The cabinet becomes the theatre's property in one transaction that repays the spent repair advance; this is one concrete cost, not stacked punishment. Sahar will not return to being an assistant or promise that one good night repairs the friendship. June's independent audition remains hers.

The rebuilt trick stands as an ordinary illusion without a portal, so no cue requires Amos to appear. In the final scene Amos is already present; he chooses what to offer instead of fulfilling Della's itinerary. He opens onto his garden, creating a real, unscripted domestic exchange. Something small and material can come through from its keeper. Nobody crosses into the distant garden. Sahar can disappear and return locally by the concealed side exit, whether or not the portal continues. Amos's particular choice makes the ordinary act astonishing and gives him contact he wanted; the backstage route supplies continuity. The ending may leave Sahar agreeing to one future rehearsal on her own terms. The final image should make the second door and the relinquished cabinet felt, without claiming a cure, unanimous agreement, or applause that settles the relationship.
