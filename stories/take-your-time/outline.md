# Take Your Time

Provisional direction, 2026-10-11.

Four teenage heroes, together for only a few rescues, meet in the back room of a closed roller rink to decide whether to exchange their ordinary identities. Sixteen-year-old Romy Calder, known to the others as Afterimage, has brought supermarket cupcakes and quietly imagines the team becoming the people she eats lunch with. She can cast one brief light decoy; the confident girl her teammates know is an easier self to inhabit than the girl at school. A recent rescue left Cricket dazed, with nobody knowing whom to call, so her argument for disclosure has practical weight as well as private hunger.

Sunspot (Zuri Keene, whose palms produce heat) is willing to reveal, but wants usable emergency help rather than a grand proof of loyalty. Anchor, who can fix themself in place against great force and wears a motorcycle helmet, wants to stay masked. Cricket, a spectacular jumper, favors revealing. Give their conversation specific teenage mistakes, diversions, and interruptions: inconvenient food, costume discomfort, a name accidentally spoken too early. Romy initially hears Anchor's refusal as a refusal of the friendship she wants, and overreaches. Anchor need not disclose a dramatic history in order to make the refusal legitimate.

When Cricket unmasks, Romy recognizes Len Dacre from school. She forwarded a rehearsal clip of his voice cracking to a class chat; when he asked her to delete it, she said, "It's already everywhere." The easy masked friendship acquires a real past. Do not make Len a surprise moral lesson or demand that he forgive her because she is lonely. He can be funny, proud of his powers, and angry at losing the version of their friendship in which she was kind to him. His choice about what to disclose to the others, and whether to stay, matters as much as her apology.

Possible movement: the meeting fails to deliver the unanimous belonging Romy planned; she owns the specific act without turning it into a speech, accepts that Len may withdraw, and stops demanding Anchor's face. They make a mixed decision: some share ordinary identities, Anchor does not, and practical contact details do not require identical disclosure from everyone. The ending should rest on changed contact between people, perhaps Romy giving Len time rather than chasing reassurance, with Anchor still present. Avoid a polished privacy policy, a villain arriving to settle the argument, or a tidy tableau in which removing masks repairs everything. Stay mostly in this one evening and use a civilian coda only if it gives an effect the rink cannot.
