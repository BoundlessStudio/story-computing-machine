# Take Your Time

Provisional direction, 2026-10-11.

Four teenage heroes, together for only a few rescues, meet in the back room of a closed roller rink to decide whether to exchange their ordinary identities. Sixteen-year-old Romy Calder, known to the others as Afterimage, has brought supermarket cupcakes and quietly imagines the team becoming the people she eats lunch with. She can cast one brief light decoy; the confident girl her teammates know is an easier self to inhabit than the girl at school. A recent rescue left Cricket dazed, with nobody knowing whom to call, so her argument for disclosure has practical weight as well as private hunger.

Sunspot (Zuri Keene, whose palms produce heat) was the one left sitting with dazed Cricket, telling him he was fine while not knowing how to find his people. She wants relief from that frightening responsibility and initially presses for names and photographs as hard as Romy does; she does not arrive carrying the solution. Anchor, who can fix themself in place against great force and wears a motorcycle helmet, wants to stay masked. Cricket, a spectacular jumper, favors revealing for his own practical need and because he wants to be known by these new friends. Give their conversation specific teenage mistakes, diversions, and interruptions: inconvenient food, costume discomfort, a name accidentally spoken too early. Establish their inexpensive voice filters lightly. Romy initially hears Anchor's refusal as a refusal of the friendship she wants, and overreaches. Anchor need not disclose a dramatic history in order to make the refusal legitimate, but their refusal imposes a real condition on the proposed safety plan.

Romy's pressure helps move Cricket to unmask first, although he has reasons beyond pleasing her. She recognizes Len Dacre from school. She forwarded a rehearsal clip of his voice cracking to a class chat; when he asked her to delete it, she said, "It's already everywhere." Still masked, she can preserve the easy friendship by keeping herself hidden. Instead she chooses to give her name and face, knowing what this will cost. Anchor's privacy and her temptation to evade accountability are not morally interchangeable. The friendship acquires a real past. Do not make Len a surprise moral lesson or demand that he forgive her because she is lonely. He can be funny, proud of his powers, and angry at losing the version of their friendship in which she was kind to him. He controls whether Sunspot and Anchor hear the clip story; Romy should not turn disclosure of his humiliation into her public redemption. Len still wants this team for himself. His terms for staying can leave their school relationship unresolved.

Possible movement: the meeting fails to deliver the unanimous belonging Romy planned; she owns the specific act without turning it into a speech, accepts uncertainty with Len, and stops demanding Anchor's face. Zuri has to ask for the reachable help she actually needs rather than treat a photograph as a complete emergency plan. The fallout changes the practical proposal. They make a mixed decision: some share ordinary identities, Anchor does not, and practical contact details do not require identical disclosure from everyone. The ending should rest on changed contact between people, perhaps Romy giving Len time rather than chasing reassurance and Anchor offering some small physical help without removing their helmet. Do not promise eventual forgiveness or an inevitable lunch friendship. Avoid a polished privacy policy, a villain arriving to settle the argument, or a tidy tableau in which removing masks repairs everything. Stay mostly in this one evening and use a civilian coda only if it gives an effect the rink cannot.
