# Opus review: The Door Knows Home

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the apprentice–executor exchanges run on claim-and-correction, the brother names his "surprise" destination before the test, the apprentice asks for the youngest's destination after banning exactly that, and the decisive scene settles by lecture and chattels schedule.
- Prose: PASS — tactile, well-paced mechanical prose; only an early rule-dump paragraph, two POV glosses and a shared "they" need polish.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the marking-blue evidence includes a turn made before the blue was applied, and the no-naming test protocol is enforced inconsistently; the prompt's promise, length (about 3,390 words) and rating are met.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree — it missed the correction spine, two Ground slips around the tests, and the out-of-order evidence.

This story's dialogue is better than the collection's worst. The siblings are told apart by what they attend to (property, loads, household, materials), and the middle sister's phone thread gives the story its most human lines. The failure sits mainly in the apprentice–executor relationship. Almost every exchange between them is a claim followed by a precise correction ("It drifted." / "A drift would leave the pin proud…"), and the executor is repeatedly pulled into the apprentice's lab register just so she can be corrected. Around that spine, the test sequence has three slips a careful reader will catch: the brother's pre-announced cabin, the apprentice's glassine questions, and the blue-marked trail of a turn made before the bluing. At the decisive moment the apprentice rules and the executor dictates a schedule, while the one loaded fact goes untouched: her arc runs beside her mother's groove, into a house that completes tomorrow. The edit should keep the procedural texture and the executor's armour, cut the apprentice's unneeded rebuttals, fix the three test slips, and give the decisive scene one beat between siblings that is not about procedure.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt requirements: an apprentice locksmith repairs a family's traveling door that opens where its living user sincerely recognizes home. Four relatives open it to four different destinations, and the door "insists" on each through the same measured evidence (flush witness pin, eleven-millimetre throw, square contact band). The story must resolve that promise. Keep it within 2,500–4,000 words; it is currently about 3,390, so edits should roughly hold length. It must stay broadly accessible. The active craft profile is prospective-2026-08-21, from the rewrite section. The rewrite request asked specifically for a dialogue rewrite.
- POV and naming: close third through the apprentice, who is unnamed and "they". The siblings are named only by role (executor/eldest sister, brother, middle sister, youngest, who is also "they"). Do not add names or change POV.
- Universe: LOCKED "Living agency and magic" (only life uses magic): the jig cannot route (L75), and the brass keeps wear, not the dead user (L231). Do not let edits imply the door holds the mother. LOCKED "Personhood and moral consequence": no sibling's home may be reduced to a malfunction. CANON "Portals and crossing systems": keep this door's rules local.
- Internal facts to keep consistent: collection at five (the van arrives at 4:51); the house completes tomorrow; four equal shares; the lamp is still disputed; one bare hand; no second contact; hold-open brace until return; closing resets the route; no remote return; the test weight goes across and back; the blue folder, socket, labels, freezer pack and glassine errands; the deep groove is the mother's wear.
- What works and must not be flattened: the mechanical texture and test-log rhythm; the middle sister's split attention and the offstage "Are you coming home now?"; the executor's steering lines; sibling pettiness ("Useful.", the lamp); the youngest's wordless crossing; the understated transport-pin ending. Do not add a speech about plural homes; the outline's own anti-default forbids it.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Apprentice–executor exchanges default to correction ping-pong

- Where: L53–L55 (brace), L121–L123 (after the barge), L203–L205 (four openings); also L161–L173 (see D2) and L239–L241 (see D6)
- Text:
> “Pin’s bent.”
> “Not bent. Installed backward.”
> “It drifted,” the executor said.
> “A drift would leave the pin proud or shorten the throw.”
> “Four different openings,” the executor said.
> “Four different destinations,” the apprentice said. “The mechanism has given me four identical valid sets.”
- Problem: Identical Voices / correction ping-pong. The apprentice answers nearly every executor line by fixing her word rather than her worry. At L123 she hears jargon she can't parse ("pin proud"). At L205, the premise's hinge where the repair "seems to fail", the reply opens with a pointless word correction (openings to destinations) before it reaches the evidence. The apprentice is equally composed throughout, even though L25 sets their own bench number at risk in front of an appraiser (uniform fluency under pressure).
- Fix: Keep L55 and L97 (natural trade exchanges). Rework L123 and L205 so the apprentice answers what she fears, the repair failing, with the gauge and their own stake, not with a better word. Let at least one executor claim go unanswered or be met by action (measuring, showing the pin). Illustrative only (L205): "Every one of those seated square. If I thought one was bad, I wouldn't put my number on it."

### D2 · Major · Prompting the youngest plays as setup and rebuke

- Where: L159–L173, executor steering the youngest
- Text:
> “We’re trying to reproduce the first result.”
> “Then reproduce the conditions. One user, no named target, intention to cross.”
> “I was only steadying them,” the executor said.
> “You were changing the input.”
- Problem: Punchline Patch and Tennis Match. The executor's "reproduce the first result" is lab language she never uses elsewhere. It exists only to set up the apprentice's neat inversion. "You were changing the input" then gives the apprentice the last word on a moment that belongs to the siblings. The executor's pressure on the youngest ("You remember the back step") and her small evasion are the real content, and the rebukes flatten it into procedure.
- Fix: Keep "Think of the pantry… You remember the back step.", "No prompting," the fingers on the knuckles, the chatter, and "I was only steadying them." Replace the executor's L163 with a line in her own register, one that shows she believes the first result was the true one. Cut L173 so her excuse hangs and the youngest's rubbing and glassine line answer it. Illustrative only: "It went to the house for me. It can go there for them."

### D3 · Major · The brother names his cabin before the "surprise" opening

- Where: L99–L113, brother's test
- Text:
> “I need the long socket from my cabin. Sixteen millimetre. We can get two jobs done.”
> “No destination name,” the apprentice reminded him.
> “That is not the house,” the executor said.
- Problem: Ground/knowledge and uptake. The family has just said "We all know where it goes" (L29), yet the brother predicts his cabin and "two jobs", and his stance is never marked (prediction, dare or hope?). The executor, who polices every detail, lets "my cabin" pass and objects only after the door opens. The apprentice objects only to the naming. The reveal is spent before the door opens, and "The brother stared once" can't tell the reader whether he is surprised.
- Fix: Cut "from my cabin" and "We can get two jobs done" so the errand is only "the long socket", and the barge is a reveal to the family, the reader and perhaps him. Then cut or move the "No destination name" reminder (see C2). His later "Did anyone move a red case when we cleared Mum's shed?" already shows he half-expected the socket at Mum's. If the editor prefers a brother who knows, mark it as a challenge and have the executor object at once, before the turn.

### D4 · Major · The apprentice fishes for the youngest's destination after banning it

- Where: L175–L191, glassine exchange before the youngest's turn
- Text:
> “Where is the glassine?” the apprentice asked.
> “The studio?”
> “Print shop. Loft above.” The youngest glanced at the three siblings, then at the lever. “Rented. Access runs to month-end.”
- Problem: Ground/knowledge and Explanatory chorus. The apprentice has just stopped the executor's prompting (L161) and told the brother "No destination name". Now they ask the youngest where they're going, twice, and guess "studio" aloud. Nothing marks it as deliberate kindness, so it reads as the author steering. It also reveals the loft before L197, the only home the reader learns about in advance. "Rented. Access runs to month-end." is the youngest's one personal disclosure, but it comes in the executor's contract register, and its point (are they losing the loft, or defending it as home?) can't be recovered. The executor opens her mouth and is cut off, so nobody takes it up.
- Fix: Cut the apprentice's two questions. Let the youngest's need for glassine become their errand, as the folder and socket were for the others, without naming a place, and keep "One instruction". If the loft's precarity matters, show it on the other side of the door (packed racks, a notice) or let a sibling react after the crossing. Illustrative only (after "Not for ink."): "I've got some."

### D5 · Major · The brother closes the forced turn with an aphorism

- Where: L213–L225, the executor forces the pantry through the brother
- Text:
> “Then open there.”
> The brother let go. “Can’t route a load by reciting the quay either.”
> The apprentice did not answer.
- Problem: Philosopher and last-word line. His sister has just held his sleeve and recited the pantry at him, and he has failed in front of everyone. He answers with a trade maxim that states the story's lesson (a home can't be reached by recitation). "either" also hangs off a negative nobody spoke. The exchange closes on theme instead of on what it cost him.
- Fix: Keep "Say it. The pantry.", "I know the room." and the failed turn. Replace L223 with a reaction to being handled: irritation, a plain report of what he felt, or a demand. Illustrative only: "Let go of my sleeve."

### D6 · Major · The decisive exchange settles by lecture and ruling while its loaded fact goes untouched

- Where: L229–L243, magnifier to the executor's schedule
- Text:
> The executor’s arc ran along one shoulder of the deep old groove.
> “If that deep track were a fixed address,” the apprentice said, choosing each conditional as carefully as a shim,
> I can certify four usable sets and the blocked conditions we produced. I can’t rank the sets or assign property from them.”
> “And it is removable personal property.”
> “That’s for your inventory.”
> “The sale of the house continues. The door comes off the fixtures schedule and goes on the estate chattels list, working condition subject to certificate.
- Problem: Negotiation/procedure routine and Tennis Match at the decisive beat. The apprentice lays out a conditional proof ("They don't." / "It isn't.") and a ruling. The executor asks two procedural questions. The apprentice caps them ("That's for your inventory."), and she dictates a chattels schedule. The executor's arc beside her mother's groove, and her pantry route into a house that completes tomorrow, is the story's most loaded fact, and no sibling registers it. The relationship moves only through paperwork. The executor's procedural armour is right for her (the Voice capsule has her repeat schedules), but the scene gives it nothing to armour against.
- Fix: Cut the apprentice's speech to what the executor can see under the magnifier: all four arcs end square, and the deep groove is one user's wear. Keep the limit on ranking only if she has just asked which route is right. Cut "That's for your inventory." Before her schedule, add one short beat in which a sibling notices where her arc sits, and let her answer by picking up the form rather than by explaining. Keep her schedule and the middle sister's "It was always four shares" as the reply. Illustrative only (middle sister, at the magnifier): "Yours runs right up against hers."

### D7 · Minor · A boat instruction with no addressee

- Where: L109, the brother at the open barge cabin
- Text:
> “Towpath side. Keep your weight there. The dock blocks are sound, but she’s light with the tanks empty.”
- Problem: Ground/referent. Only he crosses, and everyone else stands on a level workshop floor, so "Keep your weight there" has no listener and "there" no place anyone can use. The second half works as reassurance to an apprentice who has just required a safe receiving threshold.
- Fix: Cut "Towpath side. Keep your weight there." and keep the reassurance, with a beat or glance showing it is aimed at the apprentice.

### D8 · Minor · A setup line that exists for the "That's why." capper

- Where: L149–L153, the middle sister in her kitchen
- Text:
> “We need them,” the executor called.
> “That’s why.”
- Problem: Punchline Patch. The executor's call exists to be capped, and it adds to a story full of last-word replies. The labels already pay off when she returns with them over her wrist (L155).
- Fix: Cut both lines and let the returned roll of labels do the work. Or keep the executor's call and let the sister ignore it while she talks to the person at home.

### D9 · Minor · A recap of shares for the reader

- Where: L245, middle sister labeling boxes
- Text:
> “Mine is kitchen and linens. His is tools. The flat one goes with the youngest. Yours is documents and silver. The lamp is still disputed.”
- Problem: Exposition. All four siblings know the split. The list briefs the reader for the box-moving that follows, which the narration already describes by owner.
- Fix: Keep "It was always four shares," and "The lamp is still disputed." Cut the itemized list, or turn it into the order of her labeling.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Blocking · The marking blue records a turn made before it was applied

- Where: L219–L229, forced turn, then case reopened and blued
- Text:
> This time they brushed a thin film of marking blue over the cam edge and asked each sibling for one neutral turn without opening the door.
> The failed prompted turn left a wavering trail between two positions and an edge smear where the cam had never met flat.
- Problem: Causality/sequence. The prompted turn (L219) happens before the case is reopened and blued (L225–L227), so it cannot have left a trail in the blue. This is the evidence the apprentice's conclusion (L233, "If the new arcs were slips…") rests on.
- Fix: Either add one deliberate control turn on the blued cam to L227 (for example, the brother repeats the recited attempt once, stopped at the brace), or cut the L229 sentence and let "slips… would be on an edge" rest on the apprentice's trade knowledge.

### C2 · Major · The no-naming test protocol is enforced inconsistently

- Where: L75 (rule), L83 (executor), L99–L101 (brother, see D3), L181–L187 (apprentice, see D4)
- Text:
> Don’t name a destination. Don’t tell each other what to remember.
> “I’ll go first. The survey folder is still in the pantry. We need it for the appraiser.”
> “No destination name,” the apprentice reminded him.
- Problem: The apprentice sets the rule, and the executor breaks it with her first words without comment. The brother is reproved for the same thing, and the apprentice later breaks it themselves. The protocol is the story's evidence engine. If naming could steer, the executor's pantry result is tainted; if it can't, the reminders are noise. Either way the apprentice looks careless or partial toward the client.
- Fix: At L83 either cut "in the pantry" ("The survey folder. We need it for the appraiser.") or have the apprentice stop her there with the reminder now at L101. Then apply D3 and D4 so that nobody names a destination before crossing.

### C3 · Minor · Staging slips

- Text:

> The other two siblings carried document boxes against their chests.
> The brother put his boot on its foot.
> Only when both her feet were back on the workshop boards did the apprentice release the brace.
> Through a small round window, steel props and the algae-streaked underside of a canal barge rested in dry dock.
> set the weight beside the clear wall

- Problem: At L11/L13 four siblings push the door, but two are carrying boxes, and the youngest's box is a flat print box (L35), not a document box. At L93 the brother holds the brace, yet the apprentice releases it. At L107 a porthole in the barge's own cabin shows that barge's underside. "Clear wall" at L149 is unclear. At L255 she "started labeling" boxes she was already labeling at L245, and the lamp argument there is the sister's with the executor (L247–L249), not the brother's.
- Fix: "Two people pushing it"; "a document box and a flat box". Have the brother lift his boot, or the apprentice hold the brace. Make the porthole show the yard or a neighbouring hull. Write "the clear floor" or "beside the table". Write "kept labeling", with the sister and the executor arguing.

### C4 · Minor · Canal infrastructure used as incidental texture

- Where: L107, L109, L223 (canal barge, towpath, quay); borderline L63, L127 (pump spares, dry dock)
- Text:
> the algae-streaked underside of a canal barge rested in dry dock
> “Pump spares. They were in the scullery because the shed roof leaked.”
- Problem: Policy (style guide "No default water infrastructure"): canals are listed explicitly, and the prompt does not ask for them. The towpath and the quay metaphor (already cut by D5) come with the canal.
- Fix: Make the brother's boat a river or harbour boat on a yard slipway or hardstanding. Drop "canal" and "towpath". Keep the tilt, the empty tanks and the hull plates. "Pump spares" can become other boat spares.
