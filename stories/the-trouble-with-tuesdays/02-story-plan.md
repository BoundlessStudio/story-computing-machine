## Premise and promise

**Premise:** Seven Tuesdays after his sister's death, habitual second-chance user Eli Fenwick is audited by a woman who calls herself the Goddess of Time and learns that every failed rescue he overwrote remained a lived death for Mae, forcing him to choose between replaying one last conversation and finally allowing loss to be irreversible.

**Central dramatic question:** When Eli can always make a painful moment happen again, can he accept one imperfect goodbye and let time move forward?

**Story promise:** A playful domestic audit of tiny temporal cheats will tighten into an intimate grief story about consent, consequence, and the difference between revising an outcome and caring for a person. Eli's midnight refusal completes his emotional movement; the short Rose coda then pays off his incomplete practical choice to keep the watch, closing the story on an earned, ominously comic cycle.

## Story controls

- **POV:** Close third person, past tense, tightly limited to Eli through the main story. After a clear section break and a large time jump, one brief close-third coda follows adult Rose. No omniscient statements about divine taxonomy, universal time, or the afterlife.
- **Length:** 3,650-word working budget; acceptable final range 3,000–4,000 words.
- **Tone:** Dry, playful domestic comedy that turns without sentimentality toward grief and moral consequence, then ends on a compact cyclical sting. Jokes should expose Eli's defenses, not mock Mae's deaths or grief.
- **Genre/scale:** Intimate modern-like speculative fiction. The watch and visitor are private anomalies; no public magic, lore tour, institutional exposition, or crossover.
- **Content boundary:** Teen. Mae's deaths are conveyed through selected sensory fragments and emotional aftermath, never gore. Eli's later heart attack is reported without bodily detail.
- **Setting restraint:** Use a generic large-city station, shops, and flat. `Hinge` may remain only as Eli's private label for one date; do not use real geography or brands to establish literal present-day Earth.
- **Ending discipline:** The ending may open a new consequence, but it must not undo Eli's earned choice. Rose's rewind starts a later cycle; it is not retroactive proof that Eli's acceptance was pointless.

## Character engine

### Eli Fenwick

- **Role:** Main viewpoint; Mae's younger brother, Rose's uncle, and current bloodline holder of the copper watch.
- **Concrete desire:** At first, to defend his use of the watch and preserve the possibility that a sufficiently clever correction could still make Mae's death solvable. During the tea encounter, this narrows to obtaining a better goodbye and relief from guilt.
- **Pressure:** The visitor's forty-six-use audit makes the cost immediate, while the watch remains available in the next room. Because the visitor cannot seize it or command a surrender, delay leaves the decisive act entirely Eli's.
- **External stakes:** Further use would overwrite more lived experience, and his unaddressed ownership leaves the same temptation available to his family.
- **Internal stakes:** If every loss is only a draft, Eli never has to admit that love does not grant control; if he accepts finality, he must grieve without the anesthetic of another attempt.
- **Blind spot:** He treats life as a lock whose right sequence will force a humane result. He confuses the ability to change events with the authority to decide which of another person's experiences count.
- **Movement:** Glib optimizer -> furious would-be rescuer -> listener confronted by Mae's independent memory -> grieving brother who chooses not to press the stem. His growth is real but incomplete: he stops using the watch yet cannot destroy, disclose, or safely dispose of it.

### Mae

- **Role:** Eli's older sister, Rose's mother, and the moral center of the story.
- **Desire and agency:** She voluntarily accepts a three-minute mediated conversation so she can make Eli hear what repeated rescue attempts did to her. She wants him to stop rehearsing her loss, not to absolve him on demand.
- **Personhood rule:** This is Mae's continuing self, not an echo, prediction, recording, generated copy, or divine ventriloquism. She remembers thirty-one genuinely lived deaths, chooses her words, withholds immediate forgiveness, and ends the contact herself.
- **Emotional function:** Her humor re-establishes sibling intimacy; her anger breaks Eli's abstraction; her request makes his final refusal a response to a person rather than obedience to a cosmic rule.

### The woman who claims the title Goddess of Time / Time

- **Role:** Living person-like chronal visitor, auditor, and active mediator of the tea contact.
- **Desire:** To make the holder comprehend consequences she cannot coercively prevent under this watch's narrow ownership contract.
- **Limits:** She may inspect the watch's use record, perform her own small private temporal effects, explain consequences, and open a consented interface. She cannot seize the watch, compel its transfer, order Eli not to use it, or choose for him.
- **Taxonomic handling:** `the Goddess of Time` is her claimed title and Eli's practical label, not an objective narrator ruling that she satisfies the universe's definition of a god. The prose supplies no universal divine bureaucracy. Eli may call her `Time`; never use capitalized standalone `Goddess`.
- **Tone function:** Her biscuit tin, impatience, and exact accounting keep the delivery human-scaled. Her limits make Eli's choice unavoidable.

### Rose

- **Role:** Mae's daughter and Eli's niece; a child or young teen during the main events and an adult in the coda.
- **Story function:** A birthday reminder gives Mae's life beyond Eli's grief. Years later Rose inherits both the family loss and Eli's failure to warn anyone about the watch.
- **Coda desire:** In fresh grief after Eli's sudden death, she wants him back strongly enough to make an unplanned, sustained activation.

### Supporting references

- **Mara:** Eli's one-off Hinge date, used only in the compressed petty-rewind montage. She is not a crossover and has no role in the main conflict.
- **Aunt Linda:** The absent owner of a novelty mug; the visitor uses both `Aunt Linda` and `Linda` in one dry joke. She is not present.
- **Eli and Mae's grandfather:** Unnamed former watch holder. His card-game history and napkin contract are family-scale provenance, not proof of a wider order.

## Causal arc

1. **Beginning state:** Eli has learned to press the watch's stem whenever embarrassment, inconvenience, or failure makes reality feel untidy. Fifteen petty activations train him to experience the world as editable.
2. **Disaster and false solution:** On April 22, a Tuesday, Eli sees a van about to strike Mae and panics into the first rewind before impact. He activates the watch thirty more times after thirty different failed rescues. The final, unrewound sequence becomes Mae's thirty-first death. He stops only when he can no longer make his thumb move, not because he accepts her death.
3. **Inciting disruption:** Seven Tuesdays later, a woman who calls herself the Goddess of Time arrives at Eli's flat five minutes ahead of his clock and opens a forty-six-use audit over tea and shortbread.
4. **Escalating turn:** Her funny inventory establishes exact rules and then reaches April 22. Eli argues that erased attempts do not count; she reveals that overwritten public history does not erase lived experience from a continuing person.
5. **Responsibility turn:** Eli demands that she take the watch. The local witnessed ownership contract prevents her from seizing it, requesting it, or directing his choice. Removing the external solution forces Eli to own the next action.
6. **Midpoint revelation:** With Mae's consent, Time opens a three-minute tea interface. Mae independently confirms that she remembers all thirty-one deaths and refuses Eli's claim that discarded sequences were unreal.
7. **Crisis:** Mae ends contact after asking him to stop practicing losing her. At midnight Eli holds the warm watch and realizes he could rewind to ask better questions, apologize earlier, or extend the goodbye—repeating the exact moral error Mae named.
8. **Climax:** Mae's remembered flinch, not a divine threat, causes Eli to release the stem without pressing it. He places the watch beneath his grandfather's ordinary effects and closes the box. His choice makes the following years possible.
9. **Aftermath:** Time passes in unedited, imperfect increments. Eli grieves, ages, moves, and never activates the watch again, though he also never warns Rose or resolves the object's inheritance.
10. **Resonant coda:** Nine days after Eli's quick death, adult Rose clears his flat on a Tuesday, finds the watch, and grips the stem in panic. Her living agency triggers a nine-day overwrite to before his death. Elsewhere, Time reaches for the biscuit tin: the same temptation has returned through Eli's incomplete stewardship, not because Tuesday itself is magical.

## Time and activation ledger

- **Model:** One material history is overwritten locally; the story creates no enduring branches or duplicate worlds. An activation does not retroactively make the overwritten interval morally unreal.
- **Agency:** The nonliving watch is a conduit and record. It does nothing without a living bloodline holder deliberately depressing the stem. Its ticking, warmth, and hand movement are passive properties or responses, never choices. Time's arrival effects and tea interface are powered and sustained by her own living agency.
- **Memory:** The initiating holder retains conscious memory through an overwrite. When a person's life-to-death boundary occurs inside a watch interval, that lived experience remains with the same continuing person even after public events are overwritten; while embodied again, the memory need not be consciously accessible. Mae's final post-death self therefore remembers all thirty-one deaths. Keep this rule local to this watch and do not explain universal afterlife mechanics.
- **Range:** A tap produces seconds; a deliberate hold produces minutes; panic and sustained pressure can produce a much larger, poorly controlled interval. The holder supplies the act and impulse—the watch does not choose a destination.
- **Petty-use arithmetic:** Fifteen activations precede April 22. The prose may show the accidental toast (1), cinema (1), mug (1), dog mess (1), pub quiz (1), four rewinds that create five versions of the twelve-minute Mara date (4), unsent text (1), soup (1), and summarize four other tiny corrections (4): `1+1+1+1+1+4+1+1+4 = 15`.
- **Cinema correction:** Exactly nine seconds in both scene and audit. Never use eleven minutes for this event.
- **April 22 arithmetic:** Eli activates once just before the van can hit Mae, then activates thirty more times after thirty failed interventions; the final unrewound intervention ends in death thirty-one. Thus `31 activations = 31 lived deaths`, without inventing a thirty-second death.
- **Audit total:** `15 petty activations + 31 April 22 activations = 46 uses` when Time visits. Eli has not used the watch in the seven intervening weeks.
- **Visit chronology:** April 22 is a Tuesday. The audit visit occurs seven Tuesdays later, not on April 22. The old reminder to contact Mae about Rose's birthday appears after the visit and was set before Mae died.
- **Coda chronology:** Eli dies quickly on a Sunday many years later. Rose opens the box on the Tuesday nine days after that Sunday. Her sustained panic activation overwrites exactly nine days and lands before his fatal heart attack. Tuesday marks discovery and recurrence, not the rewind's destination or a universal rule.

## Proposed inventions

All items below are story-local proposals for the later canon delta; none is established until promotion.

1. **The Fenwick bloodline watch:** A scratched copper watch can overwrite a bounded interval of one material history when a living bound holder depresses its stem. Tap, hold, and panic affect range. Its maker and ultimate origin remain unknown.
2. **A narrow witnessed ownership contract:** An unnamed prior holder bound the watch to his bloodline through a signed napkin witnessed under conditions the story does not generalize. The contract prevents Time from coercing transfer or use but does not remove the holder's freedom.
3. **Local overwrite memory:** The activating holder remembers overwritten intervals. A person whose life-to-death boundary occurs within an interval retains those lived experiences in the same continuing identity, even though public material history is overwritten; conscious access while re-embodied remains undefined.
4. **A claimed Time-title bearer:** A living person-like chronal being calls herself the Goddess of Time, can inspect this watch's ledger, and is locally constrained in how she may address its owner. Her objective divine taxonomy, worship, origin, and authority beyond this case remain unresolved.
5. **A consented tea interface:** Time can temporarily mediate a two-way exchange with a deceased continuing person who agrees to participate. The medium is a window, not the source or copy of the person, and it establishes no universal afterlife procedure.
6. **Fenwick family facts:** Mae is Eli's older sister, Rose is Mae's daughter and Eli's niece, and an unnamed grandfather previously held the watch after a strange card game. These relationships explain the watch's ordinary inheritance path.
7. **Tuesday as family/story motif:** The initial inheritance, Mae's fatal day, Time's later visit, and Rose's discovery happen on Tuesdays, but the watch has no weekday rule.

## Failure modes to watch

- Do not write `eleven minutes` for the cinema. It is nine seconds in both lived scene and audit.
- Do not lose the exact arithmetic: fifteen petty activations, thirty-one April 22 activations, forty-six total. Five date versions require four activations.
- Do not create a thirty-second Mae death. The first April activation occurs before the van hits; thirty later activations follow deaths one through thirty; the final unrewound sequence is death thirty-one.
- Do not make the audit visit occur on April 22. It is seven Tuesdays later, after Eli has stopped activating but before he has accepted finality.
- Do not call the visitor objectively `a god`, establish ongoing worship merely to justify the title, or introduce a divine ministry, hierarchy, enforcement corps, or universal contract law. Keep the full title claimed and local.
- Do not use capitalized standalone `Goddess`; it creates an avoidable alias collision with Queen Aurelia. Use the woman, the visitor, the full title, or `Time`.
- Do not let the watch rewind by itself, call to Rose, choose nine days, become hungry, or exert independent will. Ticking and warmth may feel ominous through character perception but remain passive.
- Do not make grief, tears, blood, or pain fuel in themselves. Living intentional activation is required; panic affects this watch's range only.
- Do not imply persistent branches, spare Maes, disposable timelines, arbitrary age changes, or selective memory exceptions. Follow the local overwrite ledger consistently.
- Do not present tea-Mae as a memory, copy, recording, projection controlled by Time, reward, or compulsory witness. Seed consent, preserve two-way agency, and let Mae end the contact.
- Do not make Mae forgive Eli on cue or reduce thirty-one deaths to a lesson for him. Her pain, humor, anger, motherhood, and limits must remain her own.
- Do not over-explain the afterlife, watch maker, contract witness, title bearer, or card-game opponent. The unknowns protect canon and keep the story intimate.
- Do not connect this watch to the Glass Gate, other copper artifacts, Route 0, the station, Solstice, Queen Aurelia, or any repeated number/motif elsewhere in the repository.
- Do not imply `Eli Fenwick` is related to Cal Mercer or retain `Eli Mercer` in any prose artifact after registry reconciliation.
- Do not treat the two Maras as shared identity. Keep `Mara from Hinge` only after the coordinator completes the promised other-story rename and scoped validation.
