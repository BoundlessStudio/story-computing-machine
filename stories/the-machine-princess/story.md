I allowed one photographer at the Halvek Exchange. She wanted me beside a family about to go home, to show that the Machine Princess was letting people go of their own free will. I told her to get the picture from the iron deck, with the family ship on the other side of the government ships. From there she could put the League's escort and the cordon in the same frame. The difficulty with standing beside a family is that anybody can stand beside a family. I had undertaken to get these people out of the valley.

The photographer had a permit for the stone quay. I went out to the iron deck with her so she could have the use of mine. Just past the strip of carpet where the stone met the iron, a porter asked if the tree in its box was going to stay behind. It weighed nearly as much as the man who wanted to take it. It was a dead tree as far as I could see, but the man heard the porter and held out the permit for his wife, who had died while the city was under my occupation. He appeared to think we might let the tree go in her place. I asked him whether she had grown it herself. He held it up to show me a shred of green at the end of one of the little arms. I had them carry it on with his family.

My engines hung above the deck with their wings tucked under them. I lifted one finger and the nearest rose so the photographer could see out under its belly. By then the League's fliers had taken their places around the family ship. The League had come from all over the world to fight me. I could tell the ones who'd come straight out of summer by the white of their sleeves. When they raised their hands, the shield came in clear blue sheets, with a seam where each flier's light met the next. The government vessels at the valley mouth opened their line for the shield and everything it contained. The ship went through. It was the sixth day of the ceasefire and the fourth civilian ship to get through.

The photographer turned to thank me. The departure bell sounded under the red clock at the top of the stairs. Its second note had been cracked since before the war and carried twice as far as the first. By the time she looked out again she had missed her picture. I told her to stay where she was, since I would be bringing more of my people out that evening.

Up at the entrance to the waiting room a courier in government gray called Commander Rell away from the doors and put a letter in her hand. At the time it struck me that she was making him stand outside with her to give the negotiators some quiet. She read all the way to the end of the page before she took one step sideways, out of the wind.

At the top of the steps the cold came off the stone all the way to my shoulders. The deputy commander was holding one of the waiting room doors open with his heel. I knew him as Merrin, because on the first day of the ceasefire he'd gone the whole length of the Exchange introducing himself to the families. He had started giving the porters names for the journeys they made up and down the steps. Carrying a child down asleep was going by moonlight, and if you took more than four bags at once you had chosen the royal road. I had heard two porters say these things to each other by the second day, without once looking to see where he was.

“I booked us six o'clock at the barber,” he called to Rell. “I told him exactly what happened to the back of my head last time and he says he'll let you watch him this time. You can back out now if you like, but I'll have to show both sides of my face to the diplomats and ask them to settle it.”

Through the door I could see the negotiators at their table. Ours had spread a length of paper across it so there would be room for the shape of the valley and all the routes out. One of the government clerks kept standing up to see where she had been asked to draw a line.

Rell showed Merrin the letter. He took his heel away from the door before he started reading. In the several seconds it took him to read, the door swung to on the strip of weather that blew through with us, and I could still smell wet coats from inside.

“Varda, are you going to show this to the fliers?” he said. “Gather them here, at least. If you tell them how many you've been told to expect back, they'll have a chance to decide what that's worth to them.”

Rell put the letter in her breast pocket. “Bring them within call. If someone wants to sit this out, tell them they can come and say so to me. I need your flight near the steps. If it comes to fighting, get the families back on the stone.”

“You'll have to give me the width of the steps.”

She looked at me. “We can speak down on the quay,” she said.

She led me along the stone quay, away from the foot of the steps. The joint with the iron deck ran beside us. In winter it was wide enough to lose the heel of a shoe in. Air sharp enough to stir the plates of my skirt came up from either edge of the carpet. Rell stopped just beyond the carpet and held out the government's letter. I turned my bronze palm up and she let the folded paper drop the last inch.

“Look at what's underneath the order,” she said. “They want me to take you while they've still got your fleet in this valley.”

The order was to neutralize the Machine Princess immediately, before agreement could be reached on the departure of her forces. I took an interest in the figures under it. They expected to lose more than half the fliers they had brought to Halvek. The estimate broke the number down by flight. Rell was looking out toward the government vessels while I read that part. I was able to read right down to the last little number in the margin without being interrupted.

“I'll take you in alive,” she said. “Land your gunships and leave them here. Anybody in the machine quarter who wants to go can come out on a passenger ship under our shield, as we've been taking them out all afternoon. That includes anybody who worked for you. I'd put that in front of the people at the table with my name on it.”

With my first finger I pressed the old crease back into the letter, keeping the rest of my hand quite still. A ship swung to face the quay. It was the one carrying my flag, the longest ship in my fleet, with enough room inside for its guns to sit back from the windows. I had sent it on ahead of me into Halvek years before. There were people in town old enough to remember what that afternoon had been like. Rell watched it turn and looked straight back at my hand.

“We sent two passenger ships down this valley last winter,” I said. “The government had given us leave to take out people who couldn't get through the siege lines on foot. I put the sick aboard first. We had brass blankets sent up from the machine quarter because it was so cold that morning and the youngest ones were getting stiff waiting to go. At the mouth of the valley a government officer came aboard to count the passengers. The crews were made to bring both ships down at a different landing from the one we'd agreed. By the time I got word, the people had been taken inland and the officer could no longer remember promising to let them through. I sent for the officer three times. Then I took the Halvek ferry crews. I sent their names inland and said that on the day I got my people back, he could have his. On the day they came back I went down the list and opened the doors. I would take those crews again.”

“The man with the lamp down there was captain of the number four. Will you tell him you'd do it again?”

She was looking at the foot of the steps. A man in a checked coat had lighted a boarding lamp early and was holding it down close to the deck to pick out the tarred line to the next passenger ship. People had piled out of the stairway in the wrong order. He was sending two men with a long crate to the back so an old woman could have a seat. I remembered him standing in a room where there was nowhere to sit, asking whether he could go home first and bring his shoes back, because the shoes he wore to fly in were too thin to walk home in through the snow.

“He asked me himself when I gave him back his ship,” I said. “I told him I would.”

Rell waited for me to say more. The first note of the departure bell came from behind her, and then the second, which had a pull in it like a door refusing to shut. A deckhand sang out the name of a little town to the east. The man with the lamp held it up. His coat had been mended at the cuff with thread several shades too pale, and I could see the light through the part of the seam that was beginning to come apart again.

Rell said she'd been here every morning since the ceasefire began. Six days running she'd seen him come across the carpet carrying that lamp, ready to take his ship out as soon as he had leave to fly. She expected to come here tomorrow and see him do it again. She put her hand out for the letter and I gave it back to her.

“They are afraid of what you'll do with those ships once they can't reach them,” she said. “So am I. I've stood in a street under those guns. All we could do was get people out of the houses before the guns turned. I can reach them here, and if you leave your ships in the air, I'm going to try to take them. You can still put them down. The two of us can stand here until the last person on that deck is through the cordon.”

“The people on the deck have had six days in which it was possible to get out. They have been leaving a few at a time by the grace of your government, and at the rate it serves, come spring there will still be people waiting for the privilege of being counted. I can get them out tonight in the ships that brought me here. They'll leave with their guns. I would like you to tell the people who sent you what I propose to do. Give them a chance to answer it in the hearing of their own delegation and mine.”

I brought my flagship down to the outer landing. The people waiting there drew together as its shadow went over them. On the deck the man with the lamp raised it higher, so the people behind the shadow would keep moving.

The door of the waiting room opened. First out was the government clerk from the table, who had brought the long paper with her. She still had the pen she meant to draw a line with and set it across the roll to keep it from going down the stairs. Several fliers were standing at the high windows. Merrin had brought them that far and gone on down to the deck with the others. I could see heads turn toward Rell, over the clerk's shoulder. Rell turned so she could look back at them. She used the voice I had heard her use when the cold was making people wait under the windbreaks and she needed those standing at the rear to make room.

“The government has ordered our League to neutralize the Machine Princess. She has refused the terms I've offered. I'm ordering the attack. Take the gunships. I'll take her.”

I had my hand up at *attack*. Rell was within reach of my upper arm. She took it just below the shoulder and set both heels against the stone. I could move my fingers and bend my elbow. Where she held me, I might as well have been part of the cliff. The first flight went past us hard enough to raise the carpet. I closed three fingers and sent the engines above the Exchange down to meet them.

The great mistake people make in drawing that fight is to fill the sky with smoke right at the start. For a while there was wonderful light. The fliers went out three abreast with their shields joined in long sheets, and when the sheets turned they gave back the part of the sun that was already gone from the quay. I ran my small engines along those lengths of light until they buckled, and the fliers drove the light broadside through the engines. Parts of my fleet came down against the cliff in a downpour of shining pieces.

I had to watch the faces of the people on the deck and the gunnery windows of the engines I could still turn. I wanted my flagship between the people and the falling pieces. Merrin had kept his flight low over the deck. Above them a sheet of League light was coming around for the flagship from the valley side. There was a great spread of cold air below the outer landing and I had my flagship go down into it. I wanted it to come up where the League wouldn't have room to turn against its weight.

It came up among the fliers. Two struck the wall. I saw a League officer in white trying to lift another of them clear and then a whole part of the shield went dark. The ship's wing caught the outside rim of the deck as it rose, and the deck lifted with it. There was time, seeing this, to remember how well the flag on that ship used to come out in photographs. Then the deck fell back, and the two struts underneath it came out of the cliff. The sound reached me through the stone first. Near us the deck was still attached to the quay. At its far end the rail had dropped low enough that the landing lights were shining into the town below.

Rell shouted for Merrin and he came up from the tilt of the landing with the man in the checked coat in his arms. The man's lamp was gone. He hung on to Merrin until the stone was under him and then held his hands out the way someone hands you a baby, because he was still holding on in his mind. Or he meant to take hold of Merrin again. Merrin was gone before the man had quite put his feet down. He had seen a woman on her knees trying to get up the slope where the iron had buckled. Farther down a flier had caught hold of the rail. I saw Merrin make the turn, his heels lifting level with his head. The flagship's wing passed the deck again as it came round. When I had a clear view of the rail, four people were going over, and Merrin was one of them.

The edge of the decking began to come loose from the stone several feet away. I had been thinking how far I would be able to get if Rell let me go, and which of my engines could carry me out from under the League's shields. I remember too the runner of a baby's carriage turning at the edge of the split, where a man was trying to lift the carriage onto the stone. Rell took her hand off me and was at the split in three strides.

She caught the lip of the iron, took its weight between her feet, and called again for Merrin. The iron pulled at every rivet around her fist but held where she held it. From beside her on the stone I could make out a blue sleeve far below, where it had caught on a torn length of iron. I could see her try to find a way to put her free hand over the edge and still keep her feet on the quay. There was nothing down there at the distance her hand could go. She tried it twice, once with her fingers together and once with them spread, and on the second try she closed them on the air and laid the fist on the stone.

My big ship was clear of the deck again. I could have brought it round and made for the cordon with the rest of the fleet. I remember having the whole familiar order in my hand, down to where my thumb would go as the first ship turned. I saw the man in the checked coat. He was on the stone, where he could have walked straight into the waiting room. Instead he had one knee down at the lip of the deck, passing a woman up by both her wrists. She was looking him full in the face with the astonishment of finding him alive, and he had his mouth open as though the next sound he made was going to cost him dear. Of all the things I granted that afternoon, his life was the one I had done least to earn the credit for. I opened both hands and brought the ships down to the underside of the deck.

The heat of the ships carried up the smell of the lacquer burning off their wings. The deck rose a little as each ship took its share. I took my time bringing up the far end. A shoe was lying down there against the rail and I had a very strong wish to lift the iron gently enough that the shoe would stay on it. When there was no more movement, the man in the checked coat laid the woman on the quay and went straight back for another. Above him in the high windows there was no longer anyone standing, and the people who had been at the table were outside. Both delegations were looking down at the ships pressing their backs into the Exchange.

Rell kept hold of the deck until I tapped the iron with my knuckle. She took a long time lifting her hand, one finger and then the next. A crooked row of brown marks ran across her palm where she'd caught the lip. She said Merrin's name to the first flier who came down to us from the valley, asked where he had fallen, and sent the flier down again to fetch him. There were two others along the foot of the cliff who could be seen from the flier's height. She named those two as well. I slid a ship at a time out from under the deck and raised it to boarding height while the others held the iron up. A man pulled a length of carpet across the lip of the first ship. The woman who stepped across it lifted her skirts much higher than the carpet required.

Out past the town the government vessels still held the valley mouth. I had seven gunships left that could take people. It would have been possible for me to send the flagship through the line first, with the guns free to turn, and bring the loaded ships after it. The government crews had now seen what I would set my ships upon in the sight of passengers. I had to assume they could do the same. I watched one of our negotiators feel under a child's coat for the two arms, so she could lift the child over a joint in the deck.

“Are you still prepared to take me in, Commander Rell?”

“I'll still take you in,” she said. Her hand went to where her coat usually buttoned. The letter was showing white through a tear in the cloth. “Every ship that could get us out of here belongs to you.”

“Use mine. It has room left for anybody you can bring up out of the valley. I want a League shield around all seven of my ships, with their guns aboard, from here until the government vessels are behind them. The fliers can do it if you ask. And I'll put my hands in yours when the ships are through. I make the offer to your League. I have heard from the government today.”

Rell looked at the upper landing where her people had stood. There were five fliers still there, on the stone or in the air just above it. Others had gone down to the town or the bottom of the cliff to look for the fallen. Her eyes stopped at the fifth flier and she stood there for a little while longer. When she turned back toward me, she stopped first to point out the place she wanted the delegates to stand, out from the wall where everybody could hear them. They came as far as the carpet.

“Everybody at the table should hear how we got here,” she said. “I had the government's order in my pocket when I came down. I let you go on drawing your lines and I gave the order to attack while your people were still boarding.” She turned to the fliers. “I am asking for every flight that can still put up a shield. The Princess is taking the people out in her gunships and she has agreed to come into our custody. I need you to put the shield around all seven ships. Their guns are leaving with them. If the government vessels hold their line, you hold yours.”

The clerk told Rell she had better give the government ships some idea of what they would see coming up the valley. Rell said to tell them while our fliers were still coming up, so nobody at the cordon would be able to say the first they saw of us was guns. Then she stepped toward the iron. A flier was coming up with someone in his arms and she wanted to be where he could see her.

Merrin had one shoe on. I had never seen him in his socks, although I had seen him half dressed more than once, with his coat on somebody he had brought out of a building. Rell put one hand under his head as the flier laid him down inside my ship. With her other hand she pushed his hair back from his ear. “You have to tell the barber to leave this bit alone,” she said. She kept her hand under Merrin's head until the flier came back with a blanket.

There were seventeen others laid out beside him by the time the last of the people stepped off the Exchange. The hold smelled of wet wool now as the waiting room had earlier. I saw the captain in the checked coat go aboard and send the people who could stand up to the far side, to make room for those who needed to be laid flat.

From the windows of my flagship I could see the passenger deck come up a foot as the last ship pushed off from underneath it. Then the iron went down and hung by a corner from the stone. The photographer was up beside the clock. She had her camera to her eye, and might have been taking a picture of the place where I had left her, or of the line of ships coming out from under it. I watched for the crack of light that would tell me she had taken one. We rose past her before I saw it.

The fliers came up along the cliff and took their places. Two had come straight from carrying the dead, and the flier who had brought Merrin back had one bare arm where he had torn off a sleeve to help tie somebody onto a stretcher. When he raised his hands, his piece of the shield reached right down over the flagship's guns. The other pieces came together farther out, until the ships all traveled in the blue light. In the last of the sun I could see straight through it to the guns of the next two ships.

The government vessels stayed where they were. As the shield went on up the valley one of them showed us the narrow end of its hull and the guns that lay along it. The flier with the bare arm moved out ahead of the flagship. The blue went with him. On my ship the people nearest the windows were pulling themselves up to look. Rell stopped beside me where she could see the fliers. I had one finger bent, ready to turn the leading gunship, and could see that she had looked at it when she came up. She kept her arms at her sides.

We could see three people on the bridge of the nearest government vessel. One of them stood looking down at us until another came and put a hand on his shoulder. Then the ship began to move. Its guns slid across the view in the windows and passed behind us. The second government vessel came away from it slowly, as though the length of the line it had held were still in its keeping. Our last ship went through with the fliers still above its bow.

I put out my hands, palms upward, as I had done when she gave me the letter. Rell closed her hands around my wrists. Through the shield I could see the brass mouths of the guns traveling beside us. I kept my hands open in hers and let the ships go on.
