I allowed one photographer at the Halvek Exchange on the sixth day of the ceasefire. She wanted me beside a family about to go home, to show that the Machine Princess was letting people go of their own free will. I told her to get the picture from the iron deck the instant the family ship came through the cordon. From there she could put the League's escort and the government ships in the same frame. The difficulty with standing beside a family is that anybody can stand beside a family. I had undertaken to get these people out of the valley.

The photographer had a permit for the stone quay. I went out to the iron deck with her so she could have the use of mine. On the far side of the carpet I could feel the iron trembling in the pins of my ankles. A porter asked if the tree in its box was going to stay behind. It weighed nearly as much as the man who wanted to take it. The man held out the permit for his wife, who had died while the city was under my occupation. I asked him whether she had grown it herself. He left the box on the deck and pulled back the sacking to show me a shred of green at the end of one of the little arms. I had them carry it on with his family.

My engines hung above the deck with their wings tucked under them. I lifted one finger and the nearest rose so the photographer could see out under its belly. By then the League's fliers had taken their places around the family ship. I'd learned the colors of their flights from every continent by watching them come over the tops of my ships. I could tell the ones who'd come straight out of summer by the white of their sleeves. When they raised their hands, the shield came in clear blue sheets, with a seam where each flier's light met the next. The government vessels at the valley mouth opened their line for the shield and everything it contained. The ship went through. That made four civilian ships through in six days.

The photographer turned to thank me. The departure bell sounded under the red clock at the top of the stairs. Its second note had been cracked since before the war and carried twice as far as the first. By the time she looked out again she had missed her picture. I told her to stay where she was, since I would be bringing more of my people out that evening.

From halfway up the steps I saw a courier in government gray call Commander Rell away from the doors of the waiting room and put a letter in her hand. She kept him outside with her where he couldn't disturb the negotiators. She read all the way to the end of the page before she took one step sideways, out of the wind.

At the top of the steps the cold came off the stone all the way to my shoulders. The deputy commander was holding one of the waiting room doors open with his heel. I knew him as Merrin, because on the first day of the ceasefire he'd gone the whole length of the Exchange introducing himself to the families. He had started giving the porters names for the journeys they made up and down the steps. Carrying a child down asleep was going by moonlight, and if you took more than four bags at once you had chosen the royal road. I had heard two porters say these things to each other by the second day, without once looking to see where he was.

“I booked us six o'clock at the barber,” he called to Rell. “I told him exactly what happened to the back of my head last time and he says he'll let you watch him this time. You can back out now if you like, but I'll have to show both sides of my face to the diplomats and ask them to settle it.”

Through the door I could see the negotiators at their table. Ours had spread a length of paper across it so there would be room for the shape of the valley and all the routes out. One of the government clerks kept standing up to see where she had been asked to draw a line.

Rell showed Merrin the letter. He took his heel away from the door before he started reading. In the several seconds it took him to read, the door swung to on the strip of weather that blew through with us, and I could still smell wet coats from inside.

“Varda, are you going to show this to the fliers?” he said. “Read it to my flight yourself, at least. I'd like to be standing beside them when they hear how many of us are supposed to make it back.”

Rell put the letter in her breast pocket. “Bring them within call. If someone wants to sit this out, tell them they can come and say so to me. I need your flight near the steps. If it comes to fighting, get the families back on the stone.”

Two people hauling a trunk between them had to turn it sideways to get around Merrin. He stepped up against the wall to let them pass. “I'll need the steps clear all the way up to the waiting room.”

She looked at me. “We can speak down on the quay,” she said.

She led me along the stone quay, away from the foot of the steps. The joint with the iron deck ran beside us. In winter it was wide enough to lose the heel of a shoe in. Air sharp enough to stir the plates of my skirt came up from either edge of the carpet. Rell stopped just beyond the carpet and held out the government's letter. I turned my bronze palm up and she let the folded paper drop the last inch.

“Look at what's underneath the order,” she said. “They want me to take you while they've still got your fleet in this valley.”

The order was to neutralize the Machine Princess immediately, before agreement could be reached on the departure of her forces. Below the order the casualties were set out by flight. They expected to lose more than half the fliers they had brought to Halvek. I knew the name of the flight in the white sleeves. The government had put down three to come back. Rell was looking out toward the government vessels while I read that part. I was able to read right down to the last little number in the margin without being interrupted.

“I'll take you in alive,” she said. “Land your gunships and leave them here. Anybody in the machine quarter who wants to go can come out on a passenger ship, under the shield we put around the last one. That includes anybody who worked for you. I'd put that in front of the people at the table with my name on it.”

With my first finger I pressed the old crease back into the letter, keeping the rest of my hand quite still. A ship swung to face the quay. It was the one carrying my flag, the longest ship in my fleet, with enough room inside for its guns to sit back from the windows. The afternoon I sent it into Halvek, I'd ordered it to fire on the houses nearest the Exchange. I took the quay before the fires were out. Rell watched it turn and looked straight back at my hand.

“We sent two passenger ships down this valley last winter,” I said. “The government had given us leave to take out people who couldn't get through the siege lines on foot. I put the sick aboard first. We had brass blankets sent up from the machine quarter because it was so cold that morning and the youngest ones were getting stiff waiting to go. At the mouth of the valley a government officer came aboard to count the passengers. The crews were made to bring both ships down at a different landing from the one we'd agreed. By the time I got word, the people had been taken inland and the officer could no longer remember promising to let them through. I sent for the officer three times. Then I took the crews of Halvek's town ferries, before the morning boats could leave. I wanted their absence to be noticed at both ends of the valley. I sent the officer a list of their names and told him to send my passengers home. The day they came back I went down the list and opened the doors for the ferry crews. I would take those crews again.”

Rell was looking at the foot of the steps. A man in a checked coat had lighted a boarding lamp early and was holding it down close to the deck to pick out the tarred line to the Number Four, the passenger ferry waiting at the far landing. People had piled out of the stairway in the wrong order. He was sending two men with a long crate to the back so an old woman could have a seat.

“That's the captain of the Number Four,” Rell said. “Will you tell him you'd do it again?”

I remembered him standing in a room where there was nowhere to sit, asking if one of my guards could lend him boots to walk home in. They had taken him off his ship in the thin shoes he wore to fly.

“He asked me himself when I gave him back his ship,” I said. “I told him I would.”

The first note of the departure bell came from behind her, and then the second, which had a pull in it like a door refusing to shut. A deckhand sang out for passengers going to Orwick. The man with the lamp held it up. His coat had been mended at the cuff with thread several shades too pale, and I could see the light through the part of the seam that was beginning to come apart again.

“He comes across that carpet every morning with his lamp,” Rell said. “Sometimes I go down there to tell him he can't take his ship out. He always asks me to tell the people waiting. He says they should hear it from whoever's keeping them there. I'd like to go down there tomorrow and tell him he can fly.” She put her hand out for the letter and I gave it back to her.

“They are afraid of what you'll do with those ships once they can't reach them,” she said. “So am I. I've stood in a street under those guns. I kept a door up while the people inside came out under my arm. By the time they stopped coming out the ship was turning on the next street. I can reach your ships here, and if you leave them in the air, I'm going to try to take them. You can still put them down. The two of us can stand here until the last person on that deck is through the cordon.”

“Six days, Commander Rell, and four ships through. Come spring there will still be people waiting for the privilege of being counted. I can get them out tonight in the ships that brought me here. They'll leave with their guns. Give the people who sent you a chance to answer me in the hearing of their own delegation and mine.”

The door of the waiting room had opened while I was speaking. The government clerk brought the map down in a roll, with the pen she had been using still in one hand. She spread it on the stone rail and I held down an end for her. “Show me where you'd have them open the cordon,” she said. “I can put the route in front of the whole table and ask for an answer.” I traced the route the family ship had taken.

Rell leaned over the map to hold down a corner. “Will you keep your ships at the Exchange while the clerk takes this inside? Can you wait to take the passengers aboard?”

“The ships will wait here,” I said. “The people can wait better inside them.” I brought my flagship level with the outer landing. The man in the checked coat lifted his lamp to show the porter where to lay the boarding plank. There was a family standing just behind him. The two children were tucked inside the same blanket and one had to hold it low over his head so the other could see what the porter was doing.

The plank came down. The child with his hand on the blanket stepped onto it. As he brought up his second foot, Rell looked past him through the open door, where the light now reached the brass of the guns. Then she looked back at the clerk's pen, still poised above the map. I slid my hand farther along the rail to give the clerk room to draw.

Rell lifted her hand. The corner of the map came up in the wind. She turned to the fliers waiting below the high windows. She used the voice I had heard her use when the cold was making people wait under the windbreaks and she needed those standing at the rear to make room.

“The government has ordered our League to neutralize the Machine Princess. She has refused the terms I've offered. I'm ordering the attack. Take the gunships. I'll take her.”

I had my hand up at *attack*. Rell caught my upper arm just below the shoulder and set both heels against the stone. I tried to turn, but where she held me I might as well have been part of the cliff. Then I bent my elbow. My fingers still obeyed me. The first flight went past us hard enough to raise the carpet. I closed three fingers and sent the engines above the Exchange down to meet them.

The great mistake people make in drawing that fight is to fill the sky with smoke right at the start. For a while there was wonderful light. The fliers went out three abreast with their shields joined in long sheets, and when the sheets turned they gave back the part of the sun that was already gone from the quay. I ran my small engines along those lengths of light until they buckled, and the fliers drove the light broadside through the engines. Parts of my fleet came down against the cliff in a downpour of shining pieces.

I had to watch the faces of the people on the deck and the gunnery windows of the engines I could still turn. Merrin had kept his flight low over the deck. Above them a sheet of League light was coming around for my flagship from the valley side. At the landing the porter put an arm across the children and backed them off the plank. As soon as he swung it clear, I pulled the flagship away and dropped it below the deck. I wanted to bring it up under the sheet of light, where the League would have no room to turn against its weight.

It came up among the fliers. Two struck the wall. I saw a League officer in white trying to lift another of them clear and then a whole part of the shield went dark. The ship's wing caught the outside rim of the deck as it rose, and the deck lifted with it. There was time, seeing this, to remember how well the flag on that ship used to come out in photographs. When the wing slid free the deck fell hard enough to pull its two struts out of the cliff. The sound reached me through the stone first. The deck was still attached to the quay, but at the far end the rail had dropped low enough that the landing lights were shining into the town below. The Number Four was wedged against the cliff with one wing broken off.

Rell shouted for Merrin and he came up from the tilt of the landing with the man in the checked coat in his arms. The man's lamp was gone. He hung on to Merrin until the stone was under him. As Merrin drew back, the captain's forearms stayed raised between them, palms up. Before the man had quite put his feet down, Merrin was looking back out over the deck. A woman on her knees was trying to get up the slope where the iron had buckled. Farther down a flier had caught hold of the rail and was reaching up toward her.

I saw Merrin make the turn, his heels lifting level with his head. He got to the flier just as the outer landing folded down. A slab of iron caught him across the shoulders and carried him over the edge with the flier. Two people slid past the place where the rail had been, and went after them.

The edge of the decking began to come loose from the stone several feet away. I had been thinking how far I would be able to get if Rell let me go, and which of my engines could carry me out from under the League's shields. I remember a man getting the front wheels of a baby's carriage onto the stone. The iron dropped under the rear wheels and the carriage tipped back toward him. Rell took her hand off me and was at the split in three strides.

She caught the lip of the iron, took its weight between her feet, and called again for Merrin. The iron pulled at every rivet around her fist but held where she held it. From beside her on the stone I could make out a blue sleeve far below, where it had caught on a torn length of iron. With her heels still on the quay she put her free hand out over the edge. She tried it twice, once with her fingers together and once with them spread, and on the second try she closed them on the air and laid the fist on the stone.

My big ship was clear of the deck again. I could have brought it round and made for the cordon with the rest of the fleet. I remember having the whole familiar order in my hand, down to where my thumb would go as the first ship turned. The man in the checked coat could have walked straight into the waiting room. He had gone back to the lip of the deck, where he knelt on the stone with a woman's wrists in his hands. She looked him full in the face. He had his mouth open as though the next sound he made was going to cost him dear. The day I let him out, I had made him wait while I read his whole name off my list. Now I watched him tighten his grip on those two wrists without once looking up at me. I opened both hands and brought the ships down to the underside of the deck.

The heat of the ships carried up the smell of the lacquer burning off their wings. The deck rose a little as each ship took its share. I took my time bringing up the far end. A shoe was lying down there against the rail and I had a very strong wish to lift the iron gently enough that the shoe would stay on it. When there was no more movement, the man in the checked coat laid the woman on the quay and went straight back for another. I looked up to find the high windows empty. Both delegations had come outside to watch the ships press their backs into the Exchange.

Rell kept hold of the deck until I tapped the iron with my knuckle. She took a long time lifting her hand, one finger and then the next. A crooked row of brown marks ran across her palm where she'd caught the lip. The first flier back from the valley told her where he'd seen Merrin and pointed out two others at the foot of the cliff. “Bring Merrin up,” she said. She called another flier down to where she stood so she could show him where to look for the other two. I slid a ship at a time out from under the deck and raised it to boarding height while the others held the iron up. A man pulled a length of carpet across the lip of the first ship. The woman who stepped across it lifted her skirts much higher than the carpet required.

Out past the town the government vessels still held the valley mouth. I had seven gunships left that could take people. I could send the flagship through the line first with the guns free to turn. When the loaded ships followed, anything left of the cordon would have a clear view of them. I watched one of our negotiators feel inside a child's coat for the two arms, so she could lift the child aboard my flagship. At its windows the people were packed all the way to the glass.

“Are you still prepared to take me in, Commander Rell?”

“I'll still take you in,” she said. Her hand went to where her coat usually buttoned. The letter was showing white through a tear in the cloth. “Every ship that could get us out of here belongs to you.”

“Come aboard the flagship. There is room in the hold for anybody you can bring up out of the valley. I want a League shield around all seven of my ships, with their guns aboard, from here until the government vessels are behind them. The fliers can do it if you ask. And I'll put my hands in yours when the ships are through. I make the offer to your League. I have heard from the government today.”

Rell looked at the top of the steps where her people had stood. There were five fliers still there, on the stone or in the air just above it. Her eyes passed the fifth and went down into the valley, where she'd sent the others to look for the fallen. When she turned back toward me, she motioned the delegates out from the wall. They came as far as the carpet.

Rell faced the people who'd been at the table. “I had the government's order in my pocket when I came down. The clerk was ready to take the offer back to you when I gave the order to attack.” She turned to the fliers. “I am asking for every flight that can still put up a shield. The Princess is taking the people out in her gunships and she has agreed to come into our custody. I need you to put the shield around all seven ships. Their guns are leaving with them. Go with them right up to the cordon. If the government ships stay where they are, you keep the convoy covered.”

The clerk asked to warn the cordon. Rell told her to do it while the League's fliers were still coming up. Then Rell left the carpet to stand on the iron, facing the flier bringing someone up in his arms.

Merrin had one shoe on. I had never seen him in his socks, although I had seen him half dressed more than once, with his coat on somebody he had brought out of a building. Rell put one hand under his head as the flier laid him down inside my ship. With her other hand she pushed his hair back from his ear. “You have to tell the barber to leave this bit alone,” she said. She kept her hand under Merrin's head until the flier came back with a blanket.

There were seventeen others laid out beside him by the time the last of the people stepped off the Exchange. The hold smelled of wet wool now as the waiting room had earlier. I saw the captain in the checked coat go aboard and send the people who could stand up to the far side, to make room for those who needed to be laid flat.

I looked down from the flagship as the last ship pushed the Exchange's iron deck up a foot and came out from under it. Then the iron went down and hung by a corner from the stone. The photographer was up beside the clock. She had her camera to her eye, and might have been taking a picture of the place where I had left her, or of the line of ships coming out from under it. I watched for the crack of light that would tell me she had taken one. We rose past her before I saw it.

The fliers came up along the cliff and took their places. Two had come straight from carrying the dead, and the flier who had brought Merrin back had one bare arm where he had torn off a sleeve to help tie somebody onto a stretcher. When he raised his hands, his piece of the shield reached right down over the flagship's guns. The other pieces came together farther out, until the ships all traveled in the blue light. In the last of the sun I could see straight through it to the guns of the next two ships.

The government vessels stayed where they were. As the shield went on up the valley one of them showed us the narrow end of its hull and the guns that lay along it. The flier with the bare arm moved out ahead of the flagship. The blue went with him. On my ship the people nearest the windows were pulling themselves up to look. Rell came to the window beside me. I had one finger bent, ready to turn the leading gunship. She looked at the finger and kept her arms at her sides.

We could see three people on the bridge of the nearest government vessel. One of them stood looking down at us until another came and put a hand on his shoulder. Then the ship began to move. Its guns slid across the view in the windows and passed behind us. The second government vessel came away from it slowly, as though the length of the line it had held were still in its keeping. Our last ship went through with the fliers still above its bow.

I put out my hands, palms upward. Rell closed her hands around my wrists. Through the shield I could see the brass mouths of the guns traveling beside us. I kept my hands open in hers and let the ships go on.
