# Provisional outline: The Angels Arrived

## The experience promised

The explicit request puts an unforeseen angelic arrival, the angels as "machines of our destruction," and "one man" with "a plan [to] stop the apocalypse" at the center. I infer a pleasure in salvation becoming terrifying, followed by human ingenuity taking a real chance against overwhelming force. "Apocalypse" supports civilization-wide stakes; the supplied image suggests immense, dark, prismatic machinery over ruins and a lone human beneath it. Awe, dread, urgency, and a counterattack seem appropriate interpretations, rather than prescribed tone. The man's profession, the angels' origin, any central relationship, and whether his plan succeeds are open. The later instruction distinguishes attached-document instructions from the user's request; it does not add a story event or theme.

## Chosen direction, still provisional

Close third person through **Sevrin Dass**, a first-contact engineer who helped welcome the arrivals. He believes he has found a way to board one and turn its own world-cutting power against the aperture supplying the invasion. He makes the plan and takes the decisive risk; surviving scientists, a rescue pilot, and defenders can help execute it. No particular intimate relationship is proposed as the dramatic center. His immediate attachment is to the people he urged to approach the visitors, some of whom are still alive beneath the city.

The angels are enormous autonomous dismantling engines: black pyramidal cores inside flowing, rainbow-dark wings. Their first apparently merciful acts clear obstacles and stabilize their work sites; humanity mistakes these acts for protection. They are preparing to take the entire inhabited surface apart. The machinery's ultimate makers remain unknown. An operator channel, discovered during first contact and tested again on a broken fragment, offers local manual control rather than a password that can dismiss the whole invasion. Using it subjects a human nervous system to an immense, unfamiliar body. These are chosen story rules, not prompt requirements.

## Possible scenes and turns

1. **Welcome, then the first cut.** A towering angel catches a falling passenger aircraft. Sevrin is among the cheering welcome delegation. Later the same careful wings lay a strip of inhabited city open, without anger or distinction between a tower and a person. The saved aircraft was in the way. On the contact platform, Sevrin briefly experiences a whole district as something to be folded and carried away before he tears himself free.

2. **A plan with evidence.** In a damaged contact laboratory, Sevrin uses the retained contact device and a broken angel fragment to move one small prismatic vane. It slices through the shielding human weapons could not penetrate, and completes the initiated stroke even when he tries to stop it. Meanwhile, observations from other cities show the same operation spreading in pulses shared with the luminous aperture above the first arrival. He proposes borrowing an angel's cutter to sever that aperture from inside its protective formation. The test demonstrates access and danger; it does not guarantee he can command a whole engine.

3. **Get underneath heaven.** While defenders draw the engines' attention and the pilot takes him across the ruined city, Sevrin reaches the first angel's contact cavity. The approach earns the supplied image's human-to-machine scale. A survivor's radio call interrupts the timetable; he directs the team toward the trapped people rather than using them as expendable cover, then boards without waiting for the extraction to finish.

4. **His plan meets the enemy.** Manual control gives him the angel's terrible reach, not mastery of the other engines. They identify the deviation and close around him. He must learn the wings through consequential action: first catch falling wreckage, then turn an attacker aside, then carve a path upward. Every movement risks the occupied ground. The original straight ascent fails when the others shield the aperture.

5. **Make the machine of destruction destroy its own heaven.** Sevrin deliberately lets the defenders drag his stolen engine into their harvest formation, using a constraint established in the fragment test: the cutting field cannot stop partway through an engaged stroke. With his wings already captured, he turns the final cut through the aperture's sustaining structure. The angels' combined pull supplies the force his damaged engine lacks. He must stay connected until the cut completes, despite the body-sized cost and the chance of being pulled through with them. The aperture closes; the other angels lose their supply and fall inert around the world. We witness consequences through the city and returning radio signals, rather than a declaration that all is well.

6. **After the end that did not happen.** The ruined city remains ruined and the dead remain dead. The rescue crew finds Sevrin alive inside the inert engine after dawn. They open its contact cavity to an ordinary sky. His plan has actually stopped the apocalypse; the ending rewards that costly, ingenious action and the continued lives it makes possible, without explaining away the destruction.

**Strongest creative risk:** the operator channel and aperture could become convenient weak points, or the climax could become a generic giant-machine battle. Protect the uncanny apparent mercy, establish the usable rule in an early, hazardous test, make each tactical turn depend on these particular angels' work, and keep the final reversal a development of Sevrin's plan rather than a newly discovered command.
