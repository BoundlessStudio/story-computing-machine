I paid for the test photograph from Paris. In the corner there’s a man trying to drink his coffee without holding up the procession of dress racks. He bends almost double to get his mouth to the lid. I used to be an expert at that kind of thing. You could stand in the corner of an embassy reception all evening, waiting for a man to say the name of a ship, and the one person who saw you would remember that you took your coffee black.

What I wore at the Palais de Tokyo was black, with silver bones laid over the outside. I want you to know about the bones because I took the trouble to learn to walk in them. In the Galerie Basse the catwalk went up a slant and turned around a concrete pier. All the chairs were on the outside of the bend, facing the pier. Behind the pier the audience lost you at once. On the way down, if you led with your head, the little skull on your shoulder swung forward and gave you a kiss with its lower jaw. A model with two silver wings over her shoulders had shown me that you could stop it by taking a slower second step. By the time I thought to thank her, her wings were going through the doorway one at a time.

The three designers sharing the bill at Paris Fashion Week had a jeweler in common. We’d traced a stolen experimental dart gun to his shop. It fitted inside a ring about the size of a sugared almond. The office wanted the Angels in the show, and our bookers had photographs from Milan and São Paulo to send the designers.

There was a man from the show taking test photographs against a drape in the fitting lane. When it was my turn he asked me to give him the shoulder with the skull. The second time he left a little space above my high black ponytail. On the proof monitor a silver cross showed against the braids at my temple, and the woman wearing it had the air of someone keeping a joke to herself. I asked if he ever sold the proofs. While he looked for his card my dresser put a square of violet velvet down and tapped the heel of my left boot. “Della, left boot again, please.” She bent to straighten a lace. You could feel the chill of the concrete through the velvet. It had rained all that last week of September. Wet wool hung on the rails and you got the smell of it every time somebody went past.

Beyond the rail Romi was coming through with two fists full of the sage-green train from her first look. Some of the silver stars had caught between her rings. “Could I borrow your wheels, darling?” she asked the young man with the coffee. “You see how they’ve given me the whole sky, and I have to get it down an aisle this wide without losing any of it. If you come a little to the left we might both get where we’re going.” By the time he understood what she wanted he was walking the rack around the pier for her. I was beginning to think we might do well enough to be asked back.

The bass came up on the other side of the drape. I felt it in the floor, and then the cross at my ear made the sound of a button being done up.

“Look Six. One of the women walking tonight has the ring. She’s being paid to fire it at you during the closing photograph.” The handler always sounded as if a delay had inconvenienced us both. “Twelve minutes, please. I’ll need her name.”

My dresser slid the boot under my foot. I slipped the photographer’s card into the pocket of her apron where she kept my earrings. On the wall over her shoulder the clock said 20:48. They were taking the closing picture at nine.

Nari was beside me in black chiffon, with wine-red webs of it trailing from her arms. She held one red nail beside her mouth and made the man with the test camera back up far enough to get the whole width of her sleeves. Then she put the same nail on the back of my boot. “Your left heel is on the lace.”

Romi lowered her train across my foot while I shifted it. “Say your boot has split. I’ll walk you out to our car before they put on the second record.”

“We’d lose her in the first one.” I could picture a woman stepping out of a dress and away from a rail, becoming one of a hundred people I’d stood beside all week. I’d spent enough of my life being that woman. “Get me close to her. Nari can tell me when.”

Nari touched her platform toe to the floor. “My camera’s down here.” Two taps came up through my own heel. “Wait for my hand.” She had used the same two taps with her knuckles once to keep me from lifting my head while a transport plane came apart around us and Romi tried to get three men in dinner jackets into parachutes.

At the entrance I waited for a woman whose gold earrings threw spots onto her bare shoulders whenever she turned. She went in sideways to take the length of her teal skirt past the pier. The caller waved me out into the space she’d made. The loose silver jaw kept time all the way up the slope.

* * *

I came back down the slope at 20:51 and went straight from the heat of the lamps into the smell of wet wool. The dressers had their steamers hissing right up to the slit in the drape. Nari was kneeling beside the last one with her phone low enough for me to look over her shoulder.

“I got one useful frame. They came past my foot together, the dragon and Mireille.” She widened the image with two fingers. A red jewel showed directly under the scalloped edge of a wing. A black slot went into the metal where you’d expect a claw to hold the stone. I followed the hand back to the wrist with my finger, but where I should have been able to see a sleeve I only had the silver underside of the wing. Nari took the phone back.

I went to the board. Tilda’s name was under a photograph of the dragon dress, its wings magenta and a silver serpent coiled across the front. The wings took up enough of the frame that they’d had to fit a second photograph in below it for the rings. Somebody had drawn a circle round the big red one and written in the margin that it was to come straight back to the rail. The man who’d designed the dress had been watching me look. He took the card off its pin, folded it so the note was inside and went off with it tucked under his arm.

Mireille’s card listed the red crosses hanging from her white sleeves and every last glass drop at her ears. There was no ring. I’d just seen her behind the pier with both arms out for a dresser. One hand had stayed well forward while the other sank into its sleeve.

I went to the dragon’s rail to see the underside of that stone. A woman coming the other way on clear blocks lifted a great drift of lilac gauze so I could duck under. I got to the rail as the dragon model’s dresser was lifting off the wing. The metal caught on an upright. I took the weight of it and the model ducked her head. Her freckles ran down to where the dress took over. She smelled of orange peel and hairspray.

“Pass me the ring too. She’ll want her hands for this bit.” I held my hand out for it.

She slid it off with her other hand. I saw the back of the stone for less than a second before she closed it into her fist. “They made me promise to give that one back myself.”

I knew how to open a hand in the time it took to ask a person to open it, and the glitter on her knuckles gave me a mark for my thumb. I’d spent all morning glad that somebody who had to get two wings through a door had stopped to tell me where to put my foot.

The caller shouted for Look Nineteen. My own face was on the card at the end of the aisle. A woman with silver stars running from her throat to the toes of her black boots was already at the slit in the drape, leaning forward to go on if I missed my cue. I set the wing down hard enough to make the rack ring.

My dresser got the red lacquer skirt over my hips with seven seconds left to put my arms in it. A seam of green metal ran from the high collar to the floor. As she lifted the collar the red took the light in a strip of bright orange. She snapped the left cuff shut over the spring plate I’d had fitted there. A hard bend of my wrist would bring the plate across my palm. In a trial it had knocked a knife out of a man’s hand. Here it had to look like the long point of a gauntlet. We were halfway into the other sleeve when the caller put out a hand to bring me through.

“Give me the rest of it,” I told her. She laid the unfastened length of lacquer over my right elbow and sent me out holding it like a stole. The woman nearest the light raised her phone to see how I had done it. I could have blessed her for that. By the turn I’d worked out how to let the red slip a little each time I stepped, showing a finger’s width of black lining.

Coming back past the pier I caught the third designer laying a new strip of tape for the photographer in the low pit. He waited till the other houses’ people were looking up the runway before he crouched. I heard him say the magazine had paid for a clear angle on the closing four, and that for once he wanted a picture nobody had walked across.

Romi was at the quick-change rail in silver net and starry boots, her long braid looped up so the dressers could get to the rings in it. Beside her the wing dresser had opened a velvet box with two places for rings. Both had worn the same little hollow in the lining.

“Best have them bring the rings back themselves,” Romi told her. “Let the other dresser send one across and you’ll be getting it in an empty powder tin next week. Which girl did you lend it to? I can fetch her.”

The dresser shut the box with her thumb and leaned in close to Romi. “Mireille.” Across the aisle Mireille’s designer was telling an interviewer how her own workroom had made every piece of her jewelry.

“I want you on the outside of the turn, darling,” Romi said. “I’ll send them past you close enough to need both hands on their skirts.” I told her about the designer and his tape. She tipped her head so the dressers could take the rings out of her braid as fast as they could open them.

Nari had her blue dress laid flat across her forearms. She turned back the left sleeve to check the little snare sewn into it. “I need the wrists together, within a hand’s breadth of my sleeve.”

The clock went to 20:58. My cross clicked. “Look Nineteen,” said the handler. “How are we doing?”

“Two women, one picture of a hand,” I said. “Give me the turn.” The next music was already coming up through the concrete.

* * *

Two minutes to nine, and even on the blind side of the pier I could feel the bass through both soles. A strip of light crossed the plywood at the bend, where the outside bank of chairs and the photographer in the low pit would have your face as soon as you stepped into it.

Romi picked a flared trouser hem clear of her acrylic platform and stepped to the edge. The designer was crouched beside the photographer with his roll of tape. When Romi leaned down toward them her sleeves hung in long sheets of copper and blue. The crystal at her throat swung through the patch of light the photographer wanted for the closing pose.

“Look up here a second, darling.” Romi swung a sleeve across the line of his camera. “Imagine that’s the dragon coming through where I am. How long before you can see past her? Give us two more feet at the turn and I can leave her the inside. We’ll pick up our hems as we go. You’ll see every face coming at you, and then you’ll have all four of us at the mark.”

The photographer stayed crouched. He put his eye to the camera and followed the pendant as it swung back. The designer climbed onto the plywood and peeled up the tape marking our turn.

“I had understood we were keeping the marks,” Mireille said.

Her dark hair was caught up under a crown set with red stones. She had enough blue velvet on each shoulder for a footstool, and she made it look natural to give an instruction by turning only her head. Her train dresser laid the wine velvet into her left hand a fold at a time, and Mireille pressed each fold into place with her thumb. A second dresser came up at her right with the ring box. “If you’ll leave the box open, I can do that one myself.” Mireille turned her head back to Romi, her thumb poised for the last fold.

“You’re very kind to think of the dress,” she told Romi. “We’ve practiced for two days to get the weight off the hem just as I turn. That’s where the border opens up for you. If my dresser ever lets me take it home I mean to spend an evening walking round my own furniture to see if I can do it again. I can give it to your photographer where we rehearsed it, and he’ll have every inch. From the edge he’ll get the three of you and a woman standing in a heap of her own clothes.”

Romi touched the new tape with the toe of her clear platform. “Here, love. That’s one good step. Pick up your own skirt when you get there and I’ll be behind you if it gets ideas.”

Mireille looked at her for the length of a phrase in the music. “I’d be glad to let you.”

The caller pointed me to the outside mark and told Tilda to come round me on the inside when she came up the slope. Nari came up to my right, blue-black hair sleek except for the two strands along her cheeks, her arms held out at the elbows to let the long drops of shot silk swing. The dress went from teal to violet when she leaned. She crossed her stiletto platforms as close to my skirts as I’d ever seen anyone put a foot.

“Keep that left cuff close,” she said. I let the other sleeve fall in behind me to give her room. Romi took the place nearest the photographer, alongside Mireille.

The dragon model had a blue stone the size of a plum set among the peach scales on her bodice. Her copper braids went up in a knot so the pale wings could open all the way below them. When she started up the plywood I could see the cape had sea dragons of its own, stitched in colors you had to catch sideways. I stopped with a heel on the outside mark. Her cape came within an inch of my boot. She turned to me and put both palms up to catch the folds. There was a pale groove around one finger of her right hand where the big red ring had sat. Her blue nails closed round the chain at the edge of the cape. She held the cape spread a beat longer than she needed and looked straight at me. I’d been ready to put my thumb to one of those knuckles. I gave her the whole width of the new mark. The last lavender dragon went round the pier and out of the light.

I was looking at Mireille’s hands when she came through the same narrow part beside my boot. She gave the velvet one good lift. Her left hand had hold of it and her right hovered over the floor, perfectly disposed to take the rest. The rest dropped. I saw the border turn under at her feet, all the tiny gold figures her dresser had laid out for the camera, and she let it go. In the white dress she had held her hand clear of the jeweled crosses as though making room for someone to pass. The courtesy was the same now. Her thumb slid up the side of the red stone and lifted it. There was a slot in the metal underneath.

Mireille stepped in toward me a pace before she was due to stop for the picture. Her heel caught the flared edge of Romi’s trousers, drawing the cloth tight from knee to floor. As Romi stopped, the photographer came up on one knee and swung his lens down after the snag.

Mireille drew the open ring back against her velvet. Nari sent the two taps through my heel. The photographer was looking straight at the place where her hand would have to go. I kept the spring plate flat and waited for her to find a way in.

The pier was one long step away. I could have taken myself out of the picture there, and if I dragged Mireille with me I’d take the open ring beyond Nari’s reach. Out here the man in the pit could see my whole face. My tongue was jammed against a tooth and I’d have liked to be able to turn my head. I put my foot down in the light, spending the weight the way the woman with the wings had shown me, and let my left wrist come out of the sleeve. I left it palm up between us.

Mireille looked down at the lens and took my wrist. She tugged the long point of the lacquer across her ring, turning the slot up toward the gap above my collar. She spread her fingers over the lacquer where the camera could see them.

Mireille put her weight on her front foot and leaned in under my cuff. Her other heel came off the cloth.

Romi drew her hem clear. “All yours, darling,” she called down into the pit, sweeping the copper sleeve across the photographer’s view of our hands.

Behind that bright fold Nari reached across my waist, her fingers smoothing my sleeve even as the filament drew tight around the two wrists. Mireille’s chin came up. The loop brought her hand with mine when I twisted it, and I bent my wrist hard so that when the dart came on the next beat of the music the plate was already across the stone. Through the lacquer it felt exactly the way a door feels through your knuckles when somebody knocks from the other side.

Mireille pulled once. I let my weight follow, and the two of us came up on the mark together. Romi finished her turn and let the sleeve fall. Nari raised her silver claws to her temples, framing her face between the long blue drops. The photographer got his four faces. Someone clapped out of time, and the rest of the room went with her.

At the pier I said Mireille’s name into the silver cross. The clock on the other side of the drape had just turned to nine. The handler thanked me in the same voice she’d used to ask. Nari slipped the ring off while Romi gathered the velvet to keep it clear of the service door. Two people from our car were waiting there. When they took Mireille’s arms, Nari opened the loop. Mireille laid her red nails against the velvet. “I’d be obliged if my dresser could have the dress before we go out. It would be such a shame to take it down the steps in the rain.” One of them went for her dresser.

I had the test photographer’s card back in my hand when the monitor came up with the picture from the pit. Romi’s copper was still in the air. You could see the green spine of my dress all the way to the floor, and Nari had got the light on her peacock blue. I could just make out the cuff under Mireille’s velvet. She looked down along her cheek at the place where she held my wrist, with the calm of a woman about to leave a dress to somebody in her will.

The magazine’s caption said the four of us had made a pact to share the last turn. The photographer had caught me looking straight down his lens, with my mouth open on the only laugh I’d had time for.
