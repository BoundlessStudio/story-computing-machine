title: Hold for the Photograph

I paid for the test photograph from Paris. In the corner there’s a man trying to drink his coffee without holding up the procession of dress racks. He bends almost double to get his mouth to the lid. I used to be an expert at that kind of thing. You could stand in the corner of an embassy reception all evening, waiting for a man to say the name of a ship, and the one person who saw you would remember that you took your coffee black.

What I wore at the Palais de Tokyo was black, with silver bones laid over the outside. I want you to know about the bones because I took the trouble to learn to walk in them. In the Galerie Basse the catwalk went up a slant and turned around a concrete pier. The chairs all faced the outside of the bend. Behind the pier the audience lost you at once. On the way down, if you led with your head, the little skull on your shoulder swung forward and gave you a kiss with its lower jaw. A model with two silver wings over her shoulders had shown me that you could stop it by taking a slower second step. By the time I thought to thank her, her wings were going through the doorway one at a time.

There was a man from the show taking test photographs against a drape in the fitting lane. When it was my turn he asked me to give him the shoulder with the skull. The second time he left a little space above my high black ponytail. I saw the silver cross against the braids at my temple, and a woman looking as if she’d found something funny and meant to keep it to herself. I asked if he ever sold the proofs. While he looked for his card my dresser put a square of violet velvet down by my foot. “Della, I’ll have that left boot again, please.” She bent to straighten a lace. You could feel the chill of the concrete through the velvet. A raincoat hung on the rail behind her with the smell of wet wool rising off it.

Beyond the rail Romi was coming through with two fists full of the sage-green train from her first look. Some of the silver stars had caught between her rings. “Could I borrow your wheels, darling?” she asked the young man with the coffee. “You see how they’ve given me the whole sky, and I have to get it down an aisle this wide without losing any of it. If you come a little to the left we might both get where we’re going.” By the time he understood what she wanted he was walking the rack around the pier for her.

Three designers had pooled their money for this Paris Fashion Week show. The Angels had traced a stolen dart gun the size of a sugared almond to the jeweler they shared. We’d done enough actual shows abroad for our faces to get us hired. I was beginning to think we might do well enough to be asked back.

The bass came up on the other side of the drape. I felt it in the floor, and then the cross at my ear made the sound of a button being done up.

“Look Six. One of the women walking tonight has the ring. She’s being paid to fire it at you during the closing photograph.” The handler always sounded as if a delay had inconvenienced us both. “Twelve minutes, please. I’ll need her name.”

My dresser slid the boot under my foot. I slipped the photographer’s card into the pocket of her apron where she kept my earrings. On the wall over her shoulder the clock said 20:48. They were taking the closing picture at nine.

Nari was beside me in black chiffon, with wine-red webs of it trailing from her arms. She held one red nail beside her mouth and made the man with the test camera back up far enough to get the whole width of her sleeves. Then she put the same nail on the back of my boot. “Your left heel is on the lace.”

Romi lowered her train across my foot while I shifted it. “I can have you out the delivery door before they’ve put on the second record.”

“We’d lose her in the first one.” I could picture a woman stepping out of a dress and away from a rail, becoming one of a hundred people I’d stood beside all week. I’d spent enough of my life being that woman. “Get me close to her. Nari can tell me when.”

Nari touched her platform toe to the floor. “My camera’s down here.” I felt two distinct knocks through my own heel, right under the weight. “When you get that from me, wait for my hand.” She had used two taps with her knuckles once to keep me from lifting my head while a transport plane came apart around us and Romi tried to get three men in dinner jackets into parachutes.

At the entrance I waited for a woman whose gold earrings threw spots onto her bare shoulders whenever she turned. She went in sideways to take the length of her teal skirt past the pier. The caller waved me out into the space she’d made. The loose silver jaw kept time all the way up the slope.

* * *

I came back down the slope at 20:51 and went straight from the heat of the lamps into the smell of wet wool and hot irons. The dressers had brought their ironing tables almost up to the slit in the drape. Nari was kneeling beside one of them with her phone low enough for me to look over her shoulder.

“I got one useful frame. They came past my foot together, the dragon and Mireille.” She widened the image with two fingers. At the top was the scalloped underside of one silver wing. Under it was a red jewel in a hand setting. On the edge of the jewel, where you would expect a claw to hold the stone, a black slot went straight into the metal.

I put my finger under the wing to follow its line. Nari took the phone back. “The wing is in the same picture. Look at where it cuts off the wrist.”

I went to the board. On the dragon model’s card there was a photograph of the silver dress with its magenta wings and a serpent coiled across the front. The wings took up enough of the frame that they’d had to fit a second photograph in below it for the rings. Somebody had drawn a circle round the big red one and written in the margin that it was to come straight back to the rail. The man who’d designed the dress had been watching me look. He took the card off its pin, folded it so the note was inside and went off with it tucked under his arm.

Mireille’s card showed her in pearl white with red crosses hanging from the sleeves. It listed the crosses and earrings down to the last glass drop. I’d just seen her out by the turn, opening both arms to let the dresser come in under the chains. One hand had stayed well forward while the other sank into its sleeve. On her card there was no ring.

I went to the dragon’s rail to see the underside of that stone. A woman coming the other way on clear blocks lifted a great drift of lilac gauze so I could duck under. I got to the rail as the dragon model’s dresser was lifting off the wing. The metal caught on an upright. I took the weight of it and the model ducked her head. Her freckles ran down to where the dress took over. She smelled of orange peel and hairspray.

“I can take the ring too,” I said. I held my hand out for it.

She slid it off with her other hand. I saw the back of the stone for less than a second before she closed it into her fist. “They made me promise to give that one back myself.”

I knew how to open a hand in the time it took to ask a person to open it. I looked down at her knuckles and saw the glitter her dresser had painted across them. I could remember her lowering that great shoulder to tell me how to take the slope. Now she watched me hold out my hand, waiting to see what I would ask for next.

The caller shouted for Look Nineteen. My own face was on the card at the end of the aisle. A woman with silver stars running from her throat to the toes of her black boots was already at the slit in the drape, leaning forward to go on if I missed my cue. I set the wing down hard enough to make the rack ring.

My dresser got the red lacquer skirt over my hips with seven seconds left to put my arms in it. There was a seam of green metal running from the high collar to the floor, and when she pulled the collar up it looked as if she’d set a torch behind it. She snapped the left cuff shut over the spring plate I’d had fitted there. A hard bend of my wrist would bring the plate across my palm. In a trial it had knocked a knife out of a man’s hand. Here it had to look like the long point of a gauntlet. We were halfway into the other sleeve when the caller put out a hand to bring me through.

“Give me the rest of it,” I told her. She laid the unfastened length of lacquer over my right elbow and sent me out holding it like a stole. The woman nearest the light raised her phone to see how I had done it. I could have blessed her for that. By the turn I had found a way to let the red fall a little each time I stepped, until I could see the lining showing black as the opposite side of a flame.

Coming back past the pier I caught the third designer laying a new strip of tape for the photographer in the low pit. He waited till the other houses’ people were looking up the runway before he crouched. I heard him say the magazine had paid for a clear angle on the closing four, and that for once he wanted a picture nobody had walked across.

Romi was at the quick-change rail in silver net and starry boots, her long braid looped up so the dressers could get to the rings in it. Beside her the wing dresser had opened a velvet box with two places for rings. Both had worn the same little hollow in the lining.

“Best have them bring the rings back themselves,” Romi told her. “Let the other dresser send one across and you’ll be getting it in an empty powder tin next week. Which girl did you lend it to? I can fetch her.”

The dresser shut the box with her thumb. “Mireille.” She said it into Romi’s ear. The designer who had dressed Mireille in white had spent the afternoon telling interviewers the metalwork all came from her own workroom. I could still see the dragon model’s fingers closing round the stone she’d kept.

“I can get you a look at both hands,” Romi said. “I need the outside of the turn.” I told her about the man in the pit and his tape. She pressed down on one heel and I felt a single knock through mine. “I’ll give you the beat once we’re up there.” Then she tipped her head so the dressers could take the rings out of her braid as fast as they could open them.

Nari had her blue dress laid flat across her forearms. She turned back the left sleeve to check the little snare sewn into it. “Bring the wrists together and I can get them both.”

The clock went to 20:58. My cross clicked. “Look Nineteen,” said the handler. “How are we doing?”

“I have two,” I said. “I need the turn.” The next music was already coming up through the concrete.

* * *

Romi reached the pit while the man with the tape was still down there. Her braid lay unringed over her right shoulder. She picked the hem of one flared trouser leg clear of her platform as she stepped up. The shoes were clear as glass. When she leaned out over the plywood, her sleeves hung toward the man in long sheets of copper and blue. The crystal at her throat swung into the light he was saving for the photograph.

“Look up here a second, darling.” Romi swung a sleeve across the line of his camera. “Imagine that’s the dragon coming through where I am. How long before you can see past her? Give us two more feet at the turn and I can leave her the inside. We’ll pick up our hems as we go. You’ll see every face coming at you, and then you’ll have all four of us at the mark.”

The photographer stayed crouched. He put his eye to the camera and followed the pendant as it swung back. The designer climbed onto the plywood and peeled up the tape marking our turn.

“I had understood we were keeping the marks,” Mireille said.

Her dark hair was caught up under a crown set with red stones. She had enough blue velvet on each shoulder for a footstool, and she made it look natural to give an instruction by turning only her head. She offered her left hand to the dresser coming up with the wine-colored train. The woman put the velvet into it a fold at a time. A dresser touched the red jewel on her right hand. “If you’ll leave the box open, I can do that one myself,” Mireille told her. She looked back at Romi. In the meantime the velvet had hardly moved in her hand.

“You’re very kind to think of the dress,” she told Romi. “We’ve practiced for two days to get the weight off the hem just as I turn. That’s where the border opens up for you. If my dresser ever lets me take it home I mean to spend an evening walking round my own furniture to see if I can do it again. I can give it to your photographer where we rehearsed it, and he’ll have every inch. From the edge he’ll get the three of you and a woman standing in a heap of her own clothes.”

Romi touched the new tape with the toe of her clear platform. “Here, love. That’s one good step. Pick up your own skirt when you get there and I’ll be behind you if it gets ideas.”

Mireille looked at her for the length of a phrase in the music. “I’d be glad to let you.”

The caller had heard enough. He put the dragon model at the head of the line and called for the last four behind her. Nari came up to my right, blue-black hair sleek except for the two strands along her cheeks, her arms held out at the elbows to let the long drops of lacquer swing. The dress went from teal to violet when she leaned. She crossed her platform boots as close to my skirts as I’d ever seen anyone put a foot.

“I can reach your left cuff from here,” she said. I let the other sleeve fall in behind me to give her room. Romi went past us on the side of the pit to come up on Mireille’s other shoulder.

The dragon model had a blue stone the size of a plum set among the peach scales on her bodice. Her copper braids went up in a knot so the pale wings could open all the way below them. When she started up the plywood I could see the cape had dragons of its own, stitched in colors you had to catch sideways. At the new tape she glanced back at me and turned both palms up to catch the folds. There was a pale groove around one finger of her right hand where the big red ring had sat. Her blue nails closed round the chain at the edge of the cape. She caught me looking and raised an eyebrow, then went through the gap she’d made for herself. The last lavender dragon went round the pier and out of the light.

I was looking at Mireille’s hands when she came beside me. She gave the velvet one good lift. Her left hand had hold of it and her right hovered over the floor, perfectly disposed to take the rest. The rest dropped. I saw the border turn under at her feet, all the tiny gold figures her dresser had laid out for the camera, and she let it go. In the white dress she had held her hand clear of the jeweled crosses as though making room for someone to pass. The courtesy was the same now. Her thumb slid up the side of the red stone and lifted it. There was a slot in the metal underneath.

She came at me a pace before the mark. The block of her heel caught the floor-skimming hem of Romi’s trousers. Romi stopped with her weight forward, the cloth tight from knee to floor. I felt her single knock through my heel. Then the photographer came up on one knee and swung his lens down toward the snag.

Mireille drew the open ring back against her velvet. Nari’s eyes followed the lens and the hand she’d raised for me came down. Two knocks. I kept my left wrist straight.

The pier was one long step away. I could have had a woman’s hand out of sight there before anybody in the front row saw it close. Out here the man in the pit could see my whole face. My tongue was jammed against a tooth and I’d have liked to be able to turn my head. I put my foot down in the light, spending the weight the way the woman with the wings had shown me, and let my left wrist come out of the sleeve. I left it palm up between us.

Mireille looked down at the lens and took my wrist. She tugged the long point of the lacquer across her ring, turning the slot up toward the gap above my collar. From the pit it must have looked like a woman trying to get a better hold of my hand. I could see the little gap she’d left for the dart.

She leaned in to come under the cuff. Her heel lifted. Romi pulled her hem free and came round toward the pit. Her copper sleeve shone between our hands and the lens.

Nari brought her left hand across my waist as if she meant to settle the fold of my sleeve for the picture. I felt the filament close round both wrists. Mireille’s chin came up. When I twisted my hand the loop brought hers with it. I bent my wrist hard and the plate came across the open stone. On the next beat of the music the dart struck it. Through the lacquer it felt exactly the way a door feels through your knuckles when somebody knocks from the other side.

Mireille pulled once. I let my weight follow, and the two of us came up on the mark together. Romi finished her turn and let the sleeve fall. Nari raised her hands just far enough to make a frame of the long blue drops. The photographer got his four faces. Someone clapped out of time, and the rest of the room went with her. It sounded as though we’d done a second turn to be kind.

At the pier I said Mireille’s name into the silver cross. The clock on the other side of the drape had just turned to nine. The handler thanked me in the same voice she’d used to ask. Nari slipped the ring off while Romi gathered the velvet to keep it clear of the service door. Two people from our car were waiting there. When they took Mireille’s arms, Nari opened the loop. Mireille asked if they would let her take the dress off before they took her out into the rain. She said it the way she had offered us the turn. One of them went for her dresser.

On my way back past the rail the monitor came up with the picture from the pit. Romi’s copper was still in the air. You could see the green spine of my dress all the way to the floor, and Nari had got the light on her peacock blue. I could just make out the cuff under Mireille’s velvet. She looked down along her cheek at the place where she held my wrist, with the calm of a woman about to leave a dress to somebody in her will.

The magazine’s caption said the four of us had made a pact to share the last turn. The photographer had caught me looking straight down his lens, with my mouth open on the only laugh I’d had time for.
