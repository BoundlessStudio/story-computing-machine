The first photograph from Paris is the one I had to pay for. In the corner there’s a man trying to drink his coffee without holding up the procession of dress racks. He bends almost double to get his mouth to the lid. I used to be an expert at that kind of thing. You could stand in the corner of an embassy reception all evening, waiting for a man to say the name of a ship, and the one person who saw you would remember that you took your coffee black.

What I wore at the Palais de Tokyo was black, with silver bones laid over the outside. I want you to know about the bones because I took the trouble to learn to walk in them. In the Galerie Basse the catwalk went up a slant and turned around a concrete pier. The chairs all faced the outside of the bend. Behind the pier the audience lost you at once. On the way down, if you led with your head, the little skull on your shoulder swung forward and gave you a kiss with its lower jaw. A model with two silver wings over her shoulders had shown me that you could stop it by taking a slower second step. By the time I thought to thank her, her wings were going through the doorway one at a time.

There was a man from the show taking test photographs against a drape in the fitting lane. When it was my turn he asked me to give him the shoulder with the skull. I got the angle right on the second try and saw myself on the monitor, looking as if I’d found something funny and meant to keep it to myself. I asked if he ever sold the proofs. While he looked for his card my dresser put a square of violet velvet down by my foot. “Della, I’ll have that left boot again, please.” She bent to straighten a lace. You could feel the chill of the concrete through the velvet. A raincoat hung on the rail behind her with the smell of wet wool rising off it.

Beyond the rail Romi was coming through with two fists full of the sage-green train from her first look. Some of the silver stars had caught between her rings. “Could I borrow your wheels, darling?” she asked the young man with the coffee. “You see how they’ve given me the whole sky, and I have to get it down an aisle this wide without losing any of it. If you come a little to the left we might both get where we’re going.” By the time he understood what she wanted he was walking the rack around the pier for her.

That was one of the three Paris Fashion Week collections we’d been hired to walk in. The designers had pooled their money for the show and were now negotiating everything down to the order of the names above the entrance. We’d followed a stolen dart gun the size of a sugared almond to a jeweler they all used. The three of us had walked in enough shows abroad to get the bookings on our own faces, which counted as unusual work for the Angels. I was beginning to think we might do well enough to be asked back.

The bass came up on the other side of the drape. I felt it in the floor, and then the cross at my ear made the sound of a button being done up.

“Look Six. One of the women walking tonight has the ring. She’s being paid to fire it at you during the closing photograph.” The handler always sounded as if a delay had inconvenienced us both. “Twelve minutes, please. I’ll need her name.”

My dresser slid the boot under my foot. I slipped the photographer’s card into the pocket of her apron where she kept my earrings. On the wall over her shoulder the clock said 20:48. They were taking the closing picture at nine.

Nari was beside me in black chiffon, with wine-red webs of it trailing from her arms. She gave the test camera a look that ought to have emptied the room. Then she put a red fingernail on the back of my boot and said, “Your left heel is on the lace.”

I moved it. “I’m walking.”

“We heard,” Romi said. She plucked the last silver star loose from her ring. “I can have you out the delivery door before they’ve put on the second record.”

“We’d lose her in the first one.” I could picture a woman stepping out of a dress and away from a rail, becoming one of a hundred people I’d stood beside all week. I’d spent enough of my life being that woman. “Get me close to her. Nari can tell me when.”

Nari touched her platform toe to the floor. She’d fitted a camera in the patent leather. “I’ll take the low view. If I send two taps, hold.” She had used two taps with her knuckles once to keep me from lifting my head while a transport plane came apart around us and Romi tried to get three men in dinner jackets into parachutes. I gave her my left foot to check the lace, and when the caller waved me out, the loose silver jaw kept time all the way up the slope.

* * *

I came back down the slope at 20:51 and went straight from the heat of the lamps into the smell of wet wool and hot irons. The people in charge of keeping the return clear had had to stand their irons somewhere. Nari was kneeling beside one of their tables, her phone at the level of her knees so I could look over her shoulder without the dressers getting a view.

“I got one useful frame. They came past my foot together, the dragon and Mireille.” She widened the image with two fingers. At the top was the scalloped underside of one silver wing. Under it was a red jewel in a hand setting. On the edge of the jewel, where you would expect a claw to hold the stone, a black slot went straight into the metal.

“The wing goes with the ring,” I said.

“The wing is in the same picture.”

I went to the board. On the dragon model’s card there was a photograph of the silver dress with its magenta wings and a serpent coiled across the front. The wings took up enough of the frame that they’d had to fit a second photograph in below it for the rings. Somebody had drawn a circle round the big red one and written in the margin that it was to come straight back to the rail. The man who’d designed the dress had been watching me look. He took the card off its pin, folded it so the note was inside and went off with it tucked under his arm.

Mireille’s card showed her in pearl white with red crosses hanging from the sleeves. It listed the crosses and earrings down to the last glass drop. I’d just seen her out by the turn, opening both arms to let the dresser come in under the chains. One hand had stayed well forward while the other sank into its sleeve. On her card there was no ring.

I could see the dragon model lowering that great shoulder to tell me how to take the slope. The freckles went down to the point where the dress took over. I had tried what she’d said, and it had worked. I knew where her house changed. I handed Nari back her phone and went.

A woman in a pearly gown was coming the other way with the whole of her skirt over one arm. The clear blocks of her heels came down so carefully you could see where she intended to put the next one. She had right of way, then a girl with gold stars at her ears swung a length of teal and bronze out of a chair and into the passage, and by the time I’d got round her I could see the empty hook where the silver dragon dress had been. Her dresser was down behind a box, looking for one of the peach platforms that went with the next look. I had got there too late even to offer to hold a wing.

Somebody began calling for Look Nineteen. On the sheet at the end of the rail that number was beside my own face, so large and unhurried it was hard to connect it with the woman trying to get round a girl who had decided to step into her star-studded black boots in the middle of the aisle.

My dresser got the red lacquer skirt over my hips with seven seconds left to put my arms in it. There was a seam of green metal running from the high collar to the floor, and when she pulled the collar up it looked as if she’d set a torch behind it. She snapped the left cuff shut over the spring plate I’d had fitted there. A hard bend of my wrist would bring the plate across my palm. In a trial it had knocked a knife out of a man’s hand. Here it had to look like the long point of a gauntlet. We were halfway into the other sleeve when the caller put out a hand to bring me through.

“Give me the rest of it,” I told her. She laid the unfastened length of lacquer over my right elbow and sent me out holding it like a stole. The woman nearest the light raised her phone to see how I had done it. I could have blessed her for that. By the turn I had found a way to let the red fall a little each time I stepped, until I could see the lining showing black as the opposite side of a flame.

Coming back past the pier I caught the third designer laying a new strip of tape for the photographer in the low pit. He waited till the other houses’ people were looking up the runway before he crouched. I heard him say the magazine had paid for a clear angle on the closing four, and that for once he wanted a picture nobody had walked across.

Romi was at the end of the quick-change rail in her second look, silver net pinned over a pale lining, her long braid so heavily ringed that she’d looped it over a shoulder to keep the bottom out of the dressers’ hands. She put one starry silver boot up on a box to make room for a wing dresser, who set a brown envelope down beside her.

“Well, take my word for it,” Romi was saying. “If she gave you two boxes at four, and you return one at nine, she’ll want to know who you let try on the other. You can say it was the house across the aisle. You’ll be saying it to the girl who opens the parcels, because the man who asked you to lend the thing will have gone out to dinner. I’d put it on paper while his people are still here.”

The dresser turned the envelope over. There were two red settings on the lender’s slip, drawn in pencil with the receiving look beside each one. Romi ran her nail along the second number. The house in white had borrowed a match to the dragon model’s ring and left it off the look card. Its designer had spent the afternoon telling interviewers that every piece of metal in the collection came from her own workroom.

“I can get you both hands,” Romi said when the dresser took the slip away. “I need the outside of the turn.”

I told her about the man in the pit and his tape. Down the rail they were taking the silver rings out of her braid as fast as they could open them. Nari came up with her blue dress laid flat across her forearms. The snare was sewn into its left sleeve. In the fittings we’d had to stand close enough for our cuffs to touch before it would throw a loop round two wrists, and Nari had made us do the same turn over and over until the cord would run without taking a fingernail with it. She set the dress down where nobody could get a shoe on it.

Behind us the clock went to 20:58. The next music was already coming up through the concrete.

* * *

Romi reached the pit while the man with the tape was still down there. Her braid lay unringed over her right shoulder. To speak to him she had to lean out over the plywood, with lengths of copper and blue trailing from her arms. The big crystal at her throat swung into the light he was saving for the photograph.

“We’ve worked out how you can have all four of us,” she told him. “You’ll have to let me put the turn farther out. The dragon goes round first, we leave her a gap, and everybody takes her own train up to pass the next girl. You can have the whole front of the dress as she comes towards you. I know what you promised the magazine, and I’ve seen what happens when a man pays for the front of a dress and goes home with four pictures of the back of somebody else’s head.”

The photographer stayed crouched. He put his eye to the camera and followed the pendant as it swung back. The designer climbed onto the plywood and peeled up the tape marking our turn.

“I had understood we were keeping the marks,” Mireille said.

Her dark hair was caught up under a crown set with red stones. She had enough blue velvet on each shoulder for a footstool, and she made it look natural to give an instruction by turning only her head. She offered her left hand to the dresser coming up with the wine-colored train. The woman put the velvet into it a fold at a time. A dresser touched the red jewel on her right hand. “I’ll put it back in the box myself,” Mireille said, drawing it clear. “We can leave the other house out of it.” Even with the weight of the folds I could see how easy it was for her to stand there and wait for the rest of us.

“You’re very kind to think of the dress,” she told Romi. “We’ve practiced for two days to get the weight off the hem just as I turn. That’s where the border opens up for you. If my dresser ever lets me take it home I mean to spend an evening walking round my own furniture to see if I can do it again. I can give it to your photographer where we rehearsed it, and he’ll have every inch. From the edge he’ll get the three of you and a woman standing in a heap of her own clothes.”

Romi leaned down and touched the tape a little farther out, with the toe of one copper platform. “If we give you more space here, will you take it? All I’m asking you to do is mind the train of the woman ahead of you. I’ll mind yours.”

Mireille looked at her for the length of a phrase in the music. “I’d be glad to let you.”

The caller had heard enough. He put the dragon model at the head of the line and called for the last four behind her. Nari came up to my right, blue-black hair sleek except for the two strands along her cheeks, her arms held out at the elbows to let the long drops of lacquer swing. The dress went from teal to violet when she leaned. She crossed her platform boots as close to my skirts as I’d ever seen anyone put a foot.

“I can get your left cuff from here,” she said. “Give me that much room on the turn.”

The dragon model had a blue stone the size of a plum set among the peach scales on her bodice. Her copper braids went up in a knot so the pale wings could open all the way below them. When she started up the plywood I could see the cape had dragons of its own, stitched in colors you had to catch sideways. At the new tape she glanced back at me and spread both hands under the folds. Her blue nails went all the way around a chain at the edge of the cape. I could see the dents left by the wire that had held the big red jewel in place on her first walk. She caught me looking and raised an eyebrow, then went through the gap she’d made for herself. The last lavender dragon went round the pier and out of the light.

I was looking at Mireille’s hands when she came beside me. She gave the velvet one good lift. Her left hand had hold of it and her right hovered over the floor, perfectly disposed to take the rest. The rest dropped. I saw the border turn under at her feet, all the tiny gold figures her dresser had laid out for the camera, and she let it go. In the white dress she had held her hand clear of the jeweled crosses as though making room for someone to pass. The courtesy was the same now. Her thumb slid up the side of the red stone and lifted it. There was a slot in the metal underneath.

She came for the space beside me a pace before the mark. The block of her heel landed on the copper train Romi had sent out to make the wider turn. Romi stopped with her body forward and the cloth running straight and thin to the heel. In the pit the photographer lifted his head, then came up on one knee and swung the lens down after the line of the caught train. I felt Romi’s single knock through the high block of my red boot, the beat we’d agreed to use. The hold came twice through the other heel. Nari kept her arm at her side.

I could see the join between the stone and its setting. Mireille had turned the slot toward the gap above my collar. The cuff of her gown slid back from a little groove the tight white sleeve had left in the skin. There was still time to turn my shoulder in and take her forearm against the hard edge of the collar. It would have sent her backwards over the train and I couldn’t see where the muzzle would go.

I put out my left wrist, palm up, the way she might have expected a woman to put out a hand to her at the end of a show. When I was learning to do it I’d kept trying to make it look as if I had chosen where to put each finger. The model with the wings had shown me how to spend a little weight on the foot first, so the hand would arrive on its own. I spent it. In the time it took my platform to settle I grew as impatient as I’d been at the monitor. I wanted to get to the next step and see what Nari would do with it.

Mireille reached across the last inch for my wrist. To do it she had to shift onto her front foot, and at the instant her heel lifted, Romi stepped into the slack and swept the copper free. Her pendant went up with the turn. The sleeve made a bright wall across the photographer’s view of our hands.

Nari’s left fingers came up across my waist, as if she meant to settle the fold of my sleeve for the picture. I felt the filament close round both wrists. Mireille’s chin came up. When I twisted my hand the loop brought hers with it. I bent my wrist hard and the plate came across the open stone. On the next beat of the music the dart struck it. Through the lacquer it felt exactly the way a door feels through your knuckles when somebody knocks from the other side.

Mireille pulled once. I let my weight follow, and the two of us came up on the mark together. Romi was still turning. When she lifted her sleeve past us the light fell back into the pit, and Nari raised her hands just far enough to make a frame of the long blue drops. The photographer got his four faces. Someone clapped out of time, and the rest of the room went with her. It sounded as though we’d done a second turn to be kind.

At the pier I said Mireille’s name into the silver cross. The clock on the other side of the drape had just turned to nine. The handler thanked me in the same voice she’d used to ask. Nari slipped the ring off while Romi gathered the velvet to keep it clear of the service door. Two people from our car were waiting there. When they took Mireille’s arms, Nari opened the loop. Mireille asked if they would let her take the dress off before they took her out into the rain. She said it the way she had offered us the turn. One of them went for her dresser.

On my way back past the rail the monitor came up with the picture from the pit. Romi’s copper was still in the air. You could see the green spine of my dress all the way to the floor, and Nari had got the light on her peacock blue. I could just make out the cuff under Mireille’s velvet. She looked down along her cheek at the place where she held my wrist, with the calm of a woman about to leave a dress to somebody in her will.

The magazine’s caption said the four of us had made a pact to share the last turn. The photographer had caught me looking straight down his lens, with my mouth open on the only laugh I’d had time for.
