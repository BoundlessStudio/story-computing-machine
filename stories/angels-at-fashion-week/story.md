Rafa Maret stopped Nera in the crossover and asked her to give him both sleeves. The last time he'd asked her for something, it had been three more seconds to photograph a missile being hoisted into a freighter. She'd given him five. He always counted slowly with his eye to a camera.

“On the turn,” he said. “When the red goes all the way out.”

He showed her through the gap in the curtains. Under the rain-dark glass of the Grand Palais, the runway ended in a wide T. Two black broadcast rigs stood between its crossbar and the photographers' pit. Rafa pointed out a little riser with a tape X on top. He'd shoot from below the crossbar for the solo walks. At the finale flash he was allowed to step up onto the riser and get his lens above the rigs.

“I'll need room,” Nera said. Empty, the gown had looked like the shell of something that lived under a volcano. On her, its red lacquer plates were held in place by a spine of blue-green metal. With her elbows bent she could keep the long panels of her sleeves clear of her shoes. In motion they took up half the runway.

“That's what I want a picture of,” he said, and went down to the pit.

The overture started and the broadcast clock lit up at 12:00. Nera's right earring spoke in the voice of a woman she'd been taking orders from for nine years and had never seen.

“Angels. We have the commission. One of the women walking this show has been hired to kill on the final flash. Twelve minutes from this bar.”

Nera could see several candidates already, if the criterion was what they looked like they might do to the person in front of them. A woman in a corset of silver ribs was conducting a dispute over the width of a doorway. Two dressers were passing a crowned head through it sideways.

“Who is she killing?” Tala murmured. She was beside Nera in liquid blue enamel that gave off green and violet when she moved. The wings of her sleeves hung nearly to her knees.

“That part of the message is missing,” said the voice.

Miri came up on Nera's other side. Scales the color of the inside of a peach climbed her bodice to a silver dragon, and a cape the color of an opal in weak sun followed her three feet behind. Nera and Tala moved automatically to either side of its hem. In Quito they'd done much the same thing with Miri's parachute as she ran for a plane, six governments' worth of secrets in her pockets. The other two were reasonably sure she'd forgotten how to fly it.

The Angels had prepared for the possibility of a hit somewhere during Paris Fashion Week. They'd spent six weeks getting themselves booked on the same show without making it look like a reunion. Tala was on a bus shelter outside the hall advertising perfume. A buyer in Milan had once seen a dress on Miri for eleven seconds and paid the asking price without knowing how it did up. Nera's old agency in Paris still knew her shoe size. The towers of red lacquer on her feet made her wish they'd forgotten.

The voice said, “I can get the house to stop the show.”

Out past the curtain the audience had gone quiet. One cue and they could put eight hundred people and a killer on the same narrow path to the doors. They had nobody to pull from the crowd.

“Put our people at the wing exits,” Nera said. “We can still see where everybody's going.”

She pressed the back of her right platform down until it answered with a tick. The three pairs of shoes were linked. A message sent heel to heel traveled very nicely under a roomful of bass. Tala rocked back on her own platform and the tick came again. Ready.

The dresser at the curtain showed Nera two fingers. She put her face on and went out.

The first look was supposed to give the audience time to discover how much of the red was hard and how much was cloth. Nera thought she understood by the silence when she reached the T. She let it last for a beat. Then she turned her shoulders into the sleeve sweep, and for a moment she could see the red reflected all the way down the gloss of the black runway. People lifted their phones too late and laughed. Miri was already coming the other way. She slipped past Nera's outstretched sleeve with one inch to spare. Nera felt the draft of her cape as it followed.

That inch had taken them twelve tries in rehearsal. The new weave buried between the two layers of Nera's sleeve weighed almost nothing until it caught something. For rehearsal Tala had thrown a ball bearing at it. Nera had still been holding it when the dressers came to help her change.

Back in the wing the fitting rails shook with the music. Nera found Tala watching a silver dragon poised on another model's shoulder. It was one of Bréval's. Its wings had bright purple membranes, and it wrapped its tail around the model's waist. Bréval had both hands up her back, working at something underneath the shoulder while the model tried to look at him without turning her head.

“Go round her,” Tala said into Nera's earring. “I need to plant my heel on this side.”

Nera went to admire the dragon. She asked if it would get caught in the model's hair. Bréval looked up with the offense of a man who'd been sewing hair out of the way since dawn. Behind him Tala stopped in the cramped space between a steamer and a rack of empty garment bags. Her heel was close enough to listen for a powered circuit. There were dozens in the hall. The receiver only made sense of the ones she could get within a step of.

The dragon twitched. Bréval put his hand over its shoulder.

“A charge,” Tala said after they passed. “Enough to put a spring under load.”

The wing on the other side was as loud and crowded. Miri got them across by announcing that she needed someone tall enough to stand behind her and lift the cape. Every dresser within reach wanted to be needed by Miri. She drifted along the resulting procession until Nera could see into the second house's fitting bay. At the curtain, a woman was sliding a box into the waist of the bone-armored look. The model held her rib cage open with both hands. Something knocked inside the box and the woman looked quickly over her shoulder.

Miri took the cape back. “If I had something to pass to a model,” she murmured, “I would do it while everyone was looking at the dragon.”

Six minutes left. On the runway a woman in a gown of smoke-colored stars made the turn in silence, as though the stars moved her. In the fitting bay Vardelle, the designer of the bone look, took a garnet from an open case and held it to the light. She palmed it when Nera came close.

“I could use a pin like that,” Nera said.

“You can't have that one.”

“Where did you get it?”

“From the Duchess of Méroix. Who is going to see a very nice piece of colored glass from the second row and believe she's seeing her garnet.” Vardelle shut the case. “The insurers wouldn't let me take the real stones on the runway. Look at her waist from the side, if you must look at it.”

Nera could see the new red glass at the bone model's hip. There was still a hard rectangle under it. She let the woman go by. There were three minutes of solo walks left and if she stopped everybody carrying something a dresser hadn't signed for, she could stop the entire show by herself.

She went back to the house line to look at the marks. Of the three designers on the bill, Arquent had the longest slot and the finale. He had redone the layout between rehearsal and doors. His dressers had spent half the overture trying to get the other two houses' models to go to their new places without explaining why. Nera looked at the monitor over the wardrobe rail. From the house camera the new marks put the Arquent dresses in front of everybody else's. As a conspiracy it was written in letters two feet high.

At the end of the T, a strip of black tape had come up at the corner, exposing an old yellow mark. Aveline Kern, the woman in Arquent's pearl gown, stepped right past her new mark to take her turn on the old one. From the house camera she looked as if she'd arrived half a step late. Her nearer sleeve was strung with rubies and little gold chains. When she raised it into the sweep, one of the crosses at her wrist held still. Everything else took a moment to catch up with her.

Nera asked for the high camera above the curtain. From there she could trace a line from the old mark, through a gap between the broadcast rigs, to the X in the pit. Aveline could give Rafa a full sleeve with the light coming through it. It would be a handsome photograph. Nera had negotiated for more or less the same thing before the show.

Tala found her in front of the monitor. “The dragon's wing opens. I got Bréval to show me the motor. There's space under it for my fist.”

“And the pearl cuff?”

“I haven't been near enough.” Tala looked at the layout, then up at the clock. She ran her fingers along the blue seam that held the prototype snare. “Get me inside that turn. The power's in the wrist if it's there at all.”

The dressers were calling the final lineup. Nera watched Aveline glance once at the monitor, where the riser sat empty. On the other side of the curtain Rafa would be changing lenses. He'd changed lenses at the port fence, too, in the dark with one of the guards close enough to hear him breathe. Those photographs had kept six freighters' worth of missiles out of a war. She remembered putting a hand out where he could find it as they ran.

Miri came through the line, lifting her cape. A dresser asked her to be careful of the pearl. She looked at Nera and slowed.

“Aveline keeps the old mark for Rafa's shot,” Nera said. “Or for the man who'll be standing behind the camera. I want to see what she does if you give her a better picture and take the old line away.”

“And I want the inside of her wrist,” Tala said.

Miri watched the Arquent dresser put a strip of tape on the curtain to show who went first. She had thirty seconds to talk the woman out of it. “How much room does it take to open a dragon?” she asked.

The final procession was supposed to break into three groups at the crossbar, one for each house. Miri stopped where the curtain opened. Her cape was spread behind her, right over the tape for the next two models. The dresser whispered that she had to move. Miri looked through the curtain as though she'd been given the wrong table at dinner.

“I'm disappearing behind the pearl,” she said. “Bring her beside me. We can have both sleeves and the cape in one picture.” She put the dragon and the bone look on either side of that picture, and put Tala and Nera close enough to make the new arrangement look balanced. From out front the music urged them on. The dresser took one look at Miri standing immovably across her doorway and began to send the women out in the order Miri wanted.

The bone model saw the close camera waiting for her and put a hand over the new stones as she passed. Bréval's model made for her own house's lens. She turned at the front of the stage and the dragon's wings opened, purple gleaming between silver spars. Aveline stepped forward in pearl. Miri beckoned her over with a slow movement of the hand. The photographers loved it. They could see where the two women were meant to fit.

Nera took her new mark at the front edge of the T, a step closer to the cameras than Aveline. She saw the opal cape sweep between Aveline and the X. It would be in that line for a count of three. Aveline turned her face out of the house camera, then out of the pit's. She put her arm past the moving edge of the cape. The rubies fell away from her wrist, leaving the rigid gold cross pointing through the gap between the rigs. The riser was still empty.

Nera struck her heel down twice. Aveline. Tala came up beside Aveline on the inside turn and planted a platform close to her toe. The pearl sleeve almost brushed Tala's face. Nera saw Tala's chin lift when the receiver found the charge. A pinpoint of light ran along the underside of the opal fold. From where the audience sat they'd see the cape give back a dozen colors. Nera had the one angle from which the dot stayed sharp, like the head of a pin.

Rafa stepped up onto the riser. His shoulders rose into the gap and his camera came up. From the house speakers came the pickup to the last beat. Through her earring Nera heard the little breath Miri had been taking for years just before she did something too fast to explain. Nera reached up for the red as if she had all the time in the world.

Aveline saw her start the turn. She slipped her wrist lower and back, trying for a shot under the rising sleeve. Nera pivoted on the ball of her foot and let her elbow drop. The lacquer shifted against her ribs. Six feet of red opened from her shoulder to the edge of the stage.

The beat hit and the pit went white. Nera felt a tug through her sleeve, almost delicate, and then the hard little weight in the fold. She kept turning. Tala's blue wing came up against the white sleeve. A filament finer than the threads that held the rubies shot from its seam and cinched Aveline's wrist down. Aveline pulled. Tala held her place on those ridiculous shoes. From the pit it would look like two sleeves laid together on purpose.

Miri put a hand on Aveline's free shoulder and pulled her into the line. Rafa's camera was still going. Aveline glanced once at the lenses. For the length of the photograph she looked magnificently bored. Miri held the four of them there for one more beat. Then she led them back up the runway. Nera turned the dart into the fold as she gathered her sleeve. The red ran smoothly over her fingers.

In the dark past the curtain, the two people waiting for Aveline put out their hands the way dressers do for a particularly heavy train. Tala lowered her sleeve and let them take the wrist with it. On the other side of the curtain, Bréval and Vardelle and Arquent went out to take their bows.

By the time Nera got one boot off, she had a picture from Rafa. The dragon had its wings out in the background. In the foreground, four gowns opened from the same point in red and pearl and blue and a spill of pink light. The flash showed the underside of Nera's sleeve in every detail, down to the last gold stitch. It was the first picture of her in nine years of working with Rafa that he'd been able to put his name to.

*Got them both,* he wrote.
