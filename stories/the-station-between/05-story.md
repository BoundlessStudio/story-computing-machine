# The Station Between

The first thing I knew after dying was that the bench had been designed to
prevent sleep.

A steel ridge divided every seat. It pressed between my shoulder blades when I
tried to lie back, so I sat up. The floor beneath my bare feet was white tile
veined with old gray mop water. Institutional lights hummed overhead. There
were no doors I recognized, no windows except the long black panes beyond two
sets of tracks.

Above the nearer platform, a board read:

**HEAVEN — 8 MINUTES**

The board over the opposite track read:

**HELL — 15 MINUTES**

On my lap lay a cream paper ticket. My name, RUTH, was printed at the top. A
strip of black film covered the destination field, with one corner lifted just
enough to invite a fingernail.

I remembered the kitchen tiles rushing toward me. I remembered trying to call
out and having no breath for it. Then voices above me, urgent and receding, and
someone saying a number that was answered by silence.

“Am I dead?” I asked.

The woman behind the information window looked up from a ledger. She wore a
gray cardigan and a brass badge stamped HESTER PIKE. Nothing about her was
winged or radiant. A pencil held her hair in a loose knot.

“Yes,” Hester said.

I turned the ticket edgewise so the black field faced the floor. “And those are
what they say they are?”

“Those are the names on the boards.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“It’s what I can answer.”

Behind her, a hairline rectangle interrupted the yellowed wall, the shape of a
door painted over too many times. Hester closed the ledger and came around the
window.

“I can tell you what I have seen here,” she said. “The trains arrive. People
present their tickets. The gates admit them or do not. I have not traveled on
either train.”

The ticket was stiff enough to cut. I flipped it over.

**VALID THIS SERVICE ONLY**

**NO REISSUE**

Two thin black borders ran around the warning, one inside the other.

“There’ll be another service.”

“For other tickets.”

“There are always other trains.”

Hester’s expression did not change. “Here, I have seen no expired ticket
restored.”

The heaven board clicked down to seven minutes. The sound was absurdly small.
It reminded me of a clock in a hospital room, its hand jumping once each
second while Asha watched the dark window.

Stay until morning, she had said.

I pressed my thumb against the unopened film. If the word beneath it was
HEAVEN, then perhaps Asha had gone there. Perhaps I could find her. If it said
HELL, then perhaps leaving her had been weighed and found heavier than
everything else I had ever done.

Either answer would make one night into a verdict.

“I don’t have to look,” I said.

“No.”

“Then I haven’t decided.”

Hester glanced at the boards. “Not looking is something you can do. It will
not stop the clocks.”

I moved to the far end of the bench, away from her, and put the ticket into my
pocket with the black film unbroken.

***

The other passenger sat down where I had been.

I had not seen her arrive. She was perhaps my age, perhaps younger; death had
smoothed the useful measures out of both of us. A blue elastic circled her
wrist. She gathered her loose dark hair at the nape of her neck with hands that
shook only once, then let it fall again.

Her ticket lay open across her knee.

SABINE KROLL, it read.

Beneath her name, in hard black letters, was **HELL**.

“You’re holding yours backward,” she said.

“On purpose.”

“Clever. I looked straight away, like an idiot.”

“You’re not an idiot.”

“You don’t know me.”

The brittle brightness in her voice asked me to laugh. I did, a single startled
breath. Sabine smiled as if she had won something, then held up the ticket.

“You’d think eternity could afford an appeals desk.”

Hester had returned to the information window. “There is no appeals desk I can
direct you to.”

“Even living courts let the accused speak.”

“I don’t know who assigned the word.”

“But you’ll enforce it.”

“I don’t control the gates or the trains.”

Sabine rose and crossed to the waist-high gate beneath the heaven sign. When
she put her ticket against the brass plate, the gate gave a soft red pulse and
stayed shut. She tried again, holding the ticket flatter.

“The door on the train opens on the platform side,” she said.

Hester’s hands settled on the counter. “Do not board that train.”

Sabine looked over her shoulder. “You just said you don’t control it.”

“I said I do not control the door. I have seen three passengers enter a train
that did not match their tickets. The train began to move. They did not
continue on it.”

“Where did they go?”

“To the other train.”

“Alive?”

The corner of Hester’s mouth tightened. “They were dead when they arrived.”

“You know what I mean.”

“They remained themselves as far as I could see. They were conscious. They
resisted.”

Sabine looked down at the word on her ticket. “And you watched.”

“Yes.”

“Did any of them have a chance to explain what they’d done?”

“Not to anyone I could see.”

The heaven board clicked to four minutes.

Sabine came back to the bench but did not sit. “If that word is right, they
should be able to tell me why. If it’s wrong, I’m not volunteering to make
their mistake convenient.”

“What are you going to do?”

“Get on the other train.”

Hester said, “I have told you what I have seen.”

“And I listened.” Sabine’s eyes stayed on mine. “The gate won’t open for me,
but the carriage door will. I need someone on the platform to keep it from
closing.”

“It moved them,” I said.

“Maybe they let go.”

“She said they resisted.”

“Then maybe I’ll resist better.”

There was humor in the angle of her mouth and terror beneath it. I could have
said no. I could have told her the test was too expensive because she was the
one paying for it.

Instead I pictured the black field on my ticket. If Sabine remained on the
heaven train, the boards would become labels, nothing more. Asha might be
reachable no matter what was printed under the film. I could have uncertainty
without loss.

“I’ll hold it,” I said.

Hester’s voice sharpened. “Ruth.”

It was the first time she had used my name. I mistook the shame I felt for
proof that I was doing something brave.

Sabine held out her hand. I took it. Her fingers were cold, but no colder than
mine.

“If it works?” I asked.

“Then one ugly word gets smaller.”

***

The rails began to sing.

Light gathered in the black window beyond the heaven track, first a pinprick,
then two steady lamps. The train arrived without a rush of wind. Its silver
cars eased alongside the platform as quietly as an elevator, bright windows
showing blue seats, brass luggage racks, and paper cups standing in molded
holders. People were already inside, their faces turned away or blurred by
reflections.

When the doors opened, warm air touched me.

It smelled faintly of lemon lotion and clean cotton warmed in a dryer.

Asha had kept a tube of lemon lotion on the hospital table because the air
cracked her hands. I had rubbed it into her knuckles on the last evening. She
had watched me make careful circles with my thumbs and said, Stay until
morning. Not dramatically. She had been tired, and afraid, and trying not to
make either thing my fault.

I had promised.

At midnight I had gone home for a shower. At two, her final call lit the room.
I silenced it before I was properly awake. By dawn, there was no one left to
call back.

The carriage warmth drew across my face. Somewhere inside, passengers murmured,
and one brief rise of laughter had Asha’s cadence only because I wanted it to.
My hand went into my pocket. I found the lifted corner of the black film and
set my nail beneath it.

“Ruth.”

Sabine stood at the closing gate. Her own ticket had produced another red
pulse. The carriage doors sounded a warning tone.

I let go of my ticket.

There was a narrow service gap beside the gate where its arm met the wall.
Hester had not mentioned it, and she made no move to block it. Sabine turned
sideways and forced herself through, scraping one hip against the brass post.
The carriage doors began to meet.

I caught one with both hands.

It pushed steadily, without violence, and I leaned my weight against it.
Sabine seized the rail and swung aboard.

“Come with me,” she said.

For a moment I saw the empty seat behind her, its fabric warmed by a light with
no visible sun. The lemon scent deepened. I could peel the ticket. I could
know.

“Doors closing,” Hester called.

I stepped back.

The panels met between us. Sabine pressed her palm to the glass, the blue
elastic bright on her wrist. The train slid forward.

Nothing happened at first.

One window passed, then another. Sabine kept pace with me from inside, moving
down the aisle as the carriage carried her away. Her mouth opened in a
triumphant, disbelieving laugh.

The opposite rails rang with a blow like an iron bar dropped on stone.

The hell train entered the station.

It was dark red, nearly brown, with riveted sides and windows filmed by smoke.
Dry heat pressed from it before it stopped. Its wheels complained against the
track in a long metallic cry. I turned toward it for one second.

Sabine struck the heaven-train window.

The destination field on her ticket had darkened to ash gray around the black
word HELL. The rest of the cream paper remained unchanged.

Shadow was collecting under the blue seats. It rose in flat bands and wrapped
her ankles. She kicked free of one and caught the brass luggage rack with both
hands. More darkness drew across her waist and shoulders, not hiding her but
pulling her sideways through the moving carriage.

“Ruth!”

I ran along the platform. Her train was gaining speed. Sabine’s shoes skidded
over the bright floor. She locked one arm around a seat back. The shadow
stretched, tightened, and dragged her down the aisle while the other passengers
remained shapes behind reflected light.

“Hold on,” I shouted, as if I had not been the one who held the door.

Sabine’s grip slipped from the seat. She caught a vertical rail with her right
hand, the blue elastic cutting a line across her pale wrist. Her face was awake,
furious, fixed on mine.

The wall behind her split into a seam no wider than a closed curtain. There was
no view through it, only a depthless strip of dark. The bands pulled. Sabine
braced both feet, screamed my name once more, and was drawn through.

The seam closed.

The heaven carriage continued past me, empty where she had been.

Across the platform, a hand struck the smoky glass of the hell train.

The blue elastic was still around its wrist.

Sabine stood inside the other carriage, bent forward and breathing though
breath could have meant nothing there. The same loose strands of hair crossed
her cheek. She beat at the window with both palms, turned as if searching for a
door, then saw me.

The heaven train vanished into the black beyond the station.

In my pocket, the ticket burned cold.

I pulled it out. The outer of its two borders had faded from black to gray.
The film still hid the destination.

“That route has lapsed,” Hester said behind me.

The hell board read:

**HELL — 5 MINUTES**

***

Sabine came to the window opposite me.

Smoke moved across the glass between us, thinning and thickening. When it
cleared, I saw the pressure marks the luggage rail had left across her palms.
She held up her ticket. Its destination field remained ash gray around the
unchanged black word HELL.

“Can she get out?” I asked.

“I have not seen anyone get out after a transfer,” Hester said.

“You knew that would happen.”

“I warned you that I had seen it happen.”

“You could have stopped us.”

“I could not have stopped the train. I might have tried to restrain her. Or
you.” Hester looked at Sabine rather than at me. “I did not.”

The answer gave me no clean place to put my anger.

The platform gate beneath the hell sign stood closed, its brass plate dark.
Heat lifted from the train in dry, unsteady waves. Behind Sabine, the carriage
was dim but material: wooden benches, leather straps, brass bolts dulled almost
green. I could not see who else sat there. I could not see what waited beyond
the tunnel.

“If I board it,” I asked, “will it take me?”

“Present your ticket and the gate will respond.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is what I know.”

“What if mine says heaven?”

“That service has gone.”

The words struck harder than the iron noise. If Asha was anywhere beyond that
warm carriage—if the scent had been more than memory, if there had been even
the smallest chance—I had let it leave. No punishment could be as precise as
not knowing whether I had missed her.

The board clicked to three minutes.

“And if mine says hell?”

Hester’s gaze fell to the covered field. “Then the label would match this
train. I cannot tell you whether the label is just. I cannot tell you what the
destination contains.”

I could peel the film and surrender to whichever sentence waited beneath. If
it said hell, I could call boarding honesty, or punishment, or the courage I
had failed to show beside Asha. If it said heaven, I could spend whatever
remained knowing that absolution had once been offered and that I had thrown it
away.

Both fantasies asked the station to finish my guilt for me.

Sabine struck the glass once.

The sound was muffled, but I flinched. She did not point to the closed gate or
beckon me toward the train. She only looked at me. The fury in her face was
not a message about my destination. It belonged to her.

I went close enough that the heat tightened my skin.

“I helped you because I wanted you to prove it could be escaped,” I said. “I
wanted you to find out for me.”

Sabine’s mouth moved. The window gave me no sound.

“I’m sorry.”

I did not ask whether she forgave me.

The final minute disappeared from the board. A warning bell rang inside the
hell train. Its doors folded shut.

Hester said, “If you let this service leave, the other border will lapse. I
have seen no later train here accept a ticket after that.”

My hand closed around the paper. I wanted to open it so badly that my thumbnail
creased the black film. I wanted a word to hate. I wanted one to obey.

I kept my thumb still.

The train moved.

Sabine walked with the window as long as the carriage allowed. I walked on my
side, neither of us looking away. Her palm stayed against the glass, the blue
line around her wrist visible until smoke covered it, until the last car passed,
until the red lamps at the rear became two embers and went out.

The inner border on my ticket turned gray.

Then the ink of my name, the warning, and both borders drained into the paper.
The black peel-back film lightened last. In a few breaths I held a blank cream
rectangle.

Behind me, both passenger gates locked with the sound of bolts driven home.

***

I tried them anyway.

The heaven gate did not light when I pressed the blank ticket to its plate. The
hell gate did not light. I shook each until the brass bars rattled in their
housings.

“Another service,” I said. “You said there are other tickets. Let me take
another service.”

“Not with that ticket.”

“Give me a new one.”

“I cannot.”

“Who can?”

Hester picked up her ledger. “No one I have seen.”

The boards went dark. Without their words, the station became only tile, iron,
and the stale smell of cooling brakes. There was no revelation in it. No voice
told me that refusal was noble. My blank ticket did not become a key to
something hidden.

Hester crossed behind the information window and pressed her palm to the
painted rectangle in the wall. A narrow door opened without showing what lay
beyond; there was only a wedge of ordinary yellow light and the edge of another
gray wall.

I hurried after her. The opening narrowed before I reached it. I wedged the
blank ticket into the gap. The door pushed the paper back without bending it
and shut.

“Hester.”

She looked at me from the other side.

“How long am I here?”

“I don’t know.”

“What am I supposed to do?”

“I don’t know that either.”

The door sealed, leaving only the hairline in the paint.

I was alone.

I thought of Asha’s room after I left it: the chair pulled close to her bed,
the cup with its bent straw, the phone close enough for her to reach. I had
imagined that room so many times that memory and invention had worn into each
other. I did not know whether she had been awake when she called. I did not
know whether she had been afraid. I knew only what I had done.

I sat on the bench and placed the blank ticket beside me.

The cold worked through my clothes. There was no sunrise beyond the windows.
There was no clock now that the boards were dark. I counted my breaths until I
understood that counting would not make time answer.

Then the board mechanisms stirred.

Letters flipped into place above both tracks. New minutes appeared. The blank
ticket beside me remained blank.

At the far end of the bench, a woman gasped.

She wore wet slippers and a hospital wristband. Her hands clutched the front of
her gown closed. She stared at the boards, then at me.

“Am I dead?”

The old impulse rose whole inside me: step away before fear became a request.
Find the painted door. Find the darkest part of the platform. Let her peel her
ticket and carry whatever word waited there without making it mine.

I picked up my blank ticket and moved toward her.

“Yes,” I said.

She began to cry without sound.

I sat at the other end of the bench, leaving the seat between us open. After a
moment she crossed that space herself and caught my sleeve.

“What happens?”

“I can tell you what I’ve seen here.”

I told her about the gates and the one-service warning. I told her I did not
know who had made the labels, whether they were fair, or what waited beyond
them. When I reached Sabine, I did not say the train had taken a passenger, as
if she had been luggage. I said her name. I said I had held the door. I said I
had watched her dragged from the moving heaven train into the hell train, and
that she had remained herself all the way to the last window.

The woman listened with both hands around her unopened ticket.

“Which one was yours?” she asked.

I showed her the blank paper. “I didn’t look.”

“Can you still go?”

“The gates won’t open for me.”

She glanced toward the tracks. “Was it worth it?”

The question had no answer that would not pretend I knew more than I did.

“It was my choice,” I said. “And Sabine paid for part of it.”

The woman looked down. With one careful pull, she peeled the film from her
ticket. Her shoulders shook when she read the word, but she turned it so I
could see.

**HEAVEN**.

“Is it?” she asked. “Heaven?”

“That is where the sign says the train goes.”

“But is it?”

“I don’t know.”

The rails began their distant song. She tightened her grip on my sleeve.

“Will you stay until it comes?”

My sister’s voice did not enter the station. No warmth passed through me. There
was only this frightened stranger, the pressure of her fingers, and the
approaching light.

“Yes,” I said.

The train drew in with its clean windows and ordinary blue seats. Warm air
crossed the platform when the doors opened, carrying lemon and cotton and a
hundred other things I could have made into promises. The woman presented her
own ticket. The gate lit green.

She kept hold of my sleeve until the opening bars made her release me. I walked
beside her to the carriage door, but I did not touch it. She stepped aboard by
herself and took the nearest seat. I remained on the platform.

When the train moved, no shadows rose.

She watched me through the glass until the carriage entered the dark. The
warmth went with it. The gate closed before me, and when I laid my blank ticket
on the plate there was no light at all.

The station offered no thanks. Across the tracks, the hell board continued its
countdown for someone who had not yet appeared.

I returned to the bench. I sat at one end and laid the useless ticket on my
knee, leaving the place beside me clear.

The cold came up through the tiles. I stayed where I could be found.
