# The Unrehearsed Minute

## Promised experience

“My super power isn’t actually clairvoyance” promises a reversal in which something impressive turns out to be more extraordinary and more costly than it looked. The speaker has lived the futures they seem to predict. Death returns them to the previous morning, and their experience of this one day amounts to three hundred years. “Why are you telling me this now?” calls for a particular listener and a reason this admission matters today. The casual “Groundhog Day” and the exclamation invite wit under pressure. I read the payoff as seeing the confession change what the speaker can do with all that experience. The relationship, mood of the outcome, and whether tomorrow arrives are open. “try again not enjoy the airplane story...” asks for a fresh story. This one makes an old deception and a public reckoning the business of the loop.

## Takes considered

- **The Unrehearsed Minute** plays the confession straight. A celebrated stage oracle tells the former partner who kills her every evening how she really gets her predictions, then risks her name on a performance neither of them has rehearsed. The scene I want is two people facing a room that used to take one of them on faith.
- **The Late Caller** starts tomorrow with a phone-in host playing her recording of a caller who predicted every word of her half of the interview. The host slowly realizes that the stranger asking for one favor thinks of her as an old friend. The scene I want there is live radio with dead air she has always dreaded, while the caller waits to see what she chooses to say.
- **Three Hundred Years at the Blue Wheel** is a comic caper told by the croupier whom a customer keeps beating. When the customer lets a lifetime's winnings ride on one unpracticed bet, the croupier has to decide whether to help the one person who knows what the house does to winners after closing.

The theatre gives the greatest force to the reversal. Vella has made a separate living out of convincing people that there is a fixed future. This time, with a real audience and her own death approaching, she needs to show that it can change.

## Provisional direction

Vella Pike and Ivo Rusk used to play the oracle and her skeptical foil at the Gannet Theatre. Years ago Vella accidentally started a backstage fire and died the first time it burned. She woke that morning, caught the fire early, and was hailed for foreseeing it. She kept her own responsibility secret and swore she had seen Ivo start it. He went to prison and she got star billing. Ivo came back last night and laid a death mark on her. It kills her at 10:02 p.m. wherever she goes, unless he takes it back. Whenever Vella dies, she wakes in the last morning she lived, carrying her memories. The mark is already in place every time she wakes on this day.

Vella has spent three hundred years seeking a way past the mark. She has fled, brought witnesses, and offered apologies and public retractions. In the last iteration the audience took her rehearsed confession for a stunt. Ivo recognized the way she kept feeding him his old cues, down to when he was meant to contradict her. This time Vella needs him to see the extent of that rehearsal. She tells him how she gets her knowledge and offers him her stage, with the right to stop her act and ask his own questions in front of the town. In the show she spends one last astonishing prediction to win the room's attention. Then she uses it to show how readily a purported certainty can be undone, before telling what she did to Ivo. Her livelihood and the chance of surviving the hour both hang on a performance whose outcome she has deliberately made unknowable.

## Craft plan

- **Teller.** Try Vella's first-person present, brisk and ingratiating until she cares too much to keep up the patter, and close third past with Ivo, whose attention is on the wretched business of returning through the stage door. Vella gives direct access to years compressed into a sound or a gesture. Ivo lets the reader share the pleasure and unease of recognizing her knowledge in real time.
- **Vella Pike.** Wants another day and wants the house to keep loving her. She notices who is about to laugh and offers an insinuating little run of exact details while her listener is still deciding whether to interrupt. Under threat her jokes get bigger and the thing she wants gets smaller. She can tell the fire story in the order it happened once Ivo gets her to quit trying to make it the story of how she saved people. She knows dozens of ways he has refused to lift the mark and thinks his refusal means he came solely to see her die.
- **Ivo Rusk.** Wants the people who heard him called an arsonist to hear how she did it. He wants to be back in a room where he once loved working and is ashamed of wanting it. He is attentive to hands and timing. He will let Vella spend a good deal of breath, then take issue with one material fact in workshop language. When his temper goes he gives a specific, sometimes funny account of what one of her neat phrases cost him. He knows what he did last night and has no reason to believe she's ever died from it. He comes in expecting her to make him part of an act again.
- **The conversation owed.** Backstage, Vella has to tell Ivo what it has been like to die on this day, why his own words are sometimes already in her mouth, and why she is asking him to help now. He has to be able to answer as a man hearing it for the first time. Her account goes from the fire to this day's first death to the most recent show. The supplied dialogue sets the temper. Vella, Ivo, and Brindle are original to this story.
- **Place.** The Gannet Theatre in the market town of Brindle, late autumn, from the evening call to just past ten, with a glimpse of the morning. The Rook Street radio studio could give her an invisible public. The Button Fair tent would give the crowd room to disperse. The Gannet's close galleries, the only door between its backstage corridor and the stage, and its public clock above the side boxes make it hard to hide a conversation or fake the hour. Musicians tune in the gallery as dressers work behind the stage. The old curtain sticks a foot short of the floor, which leaves people's shoes visible when they think they've gone unseen. Wet wool steaming in the close house, rosin on the fiddle bows, the scuff of a boot audible through the curtain, a crack in the gold clock face, and the varnish ridges at the downstage edge. The audience can see one another react as readily as it can see the oracle.

## Scenes and ending

1. **The same boards, late in the previous attempt and at the next evening's call, about 12%.** Vella has failed to win Ivo with a beautifully timed public apology. At 10:02 she tries to keep her place in the show through one more beat and dies. A recurring sound brings her back to morning. Summarize the hours until call. The reader sees what Ivo has yet to hear. He walks in carrying the years that preceded this morning.
2. **Behind the curtain as the house fills, about 48%, the long scene.** She wants Ivo to stay and listen before he recognizes familiar patter and walks. He wants her to say what part of the fire she played and to stop answering for him. He changes the place where he usually stands when she unnervingly mouths an old answer with him. As the musicians interrupt with successive checks, she tells him that death returns her to morning, that she's done this for three hundred years, and why the old apologies failed. She offers him the right to change the performance even if he leaves the mark in place. He agrees to come out on his own cue. Keep the true account and his resistance in the room for as long as they need.
3. **Onstage under the clock, about 40%.** Vella knows where a man in the gallery will interrupt and what ridiculous words he will use. She speaks them a breath ahead, then invites him to ask something else. He has seen Ivo waiting at the edge of the curtain and asks about him. Vella brings Ivo downstage and lets his questions establish the part of the fire she has kept to herself, including the fact that she owed her first escape from the building to him. People argue over what they heard. She stays in sight and answers when there is no useful anticipation left. Ivo lifts the mark while the house is still listening. The admission has reached the people she once made his accusers. Under the clock she waits through the familiar last minute and lives into the next. Another hand goes up. She waits for the person to speak.

Load that ending with Vella jumping a cue and losing her breath in the first scene, then filling in Ivo's words in the second. Stay in the body at 10:02 as she waits for a pain she knows in detail, and in the room for the ordinary sound that occupies the beat after it. Leave the later legal and personal consequences to the day the two of them have yet to live.
