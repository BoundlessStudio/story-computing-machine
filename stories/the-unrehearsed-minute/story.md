# The Unrehearsed Minute

The editor of the Brindle Mercury has found a joke in my apology. He looks down from his box to see who else has got it, and his pencil is making a little circuit of its own on the page. If I’d gone out there with a joke prepared I’d have had to fight him for half a smile. That’s the Gannet. Even in the penny seats you can see the faces of the people who’ve paid more, and it’s a powerful thing to be able to check whether the town thinks you ought to laugh.

“Because I said Ivo Rusk started the fire,” I say, “Ivo Rusk went to prison. I want everybody here to know that I was wrong.”

It took me most of a winter to get the right weight on *wrong*. In the end I let it drop however it likes and put the weight on his name, which takes the better part of the next winter. I’ve had that applause on its own. I’ve had it accompanied by a man calling for the fire brigade, which I suppose was funnier when the fire was only five years ago. Tonight somebody makes the noise you make when you drop a lozenge and find yourself still needing it.

Ivo stands where I asked him to, in the wing. From my spot I can see the cuff of his coat and the foot he turns out when he’s ready to go on. I give him the nod we used to give each other. The foot goes back.

Oh, I wish he’d done it sooner. I had a whole long passage about our old partnership I could have spared us. Now the clock above the side boxes gives a little dry swallow as the minute hand moves, and my heart stops. I put my palm on the floor. It’s a relief to feel the ridges in the varnish. There are four, if you have time to go looking for all four. I have my hand flat over the first two when Ivo comes out. The boards give a sound I remember from the fire, which is the sound he makes with one knee down and all his weight on it, reaching.

In bed I bring my hand close to my face. The skin is smooth. Down in the street the baker’s axle has begun to complain about its first turn of the morning, and I have until he passes the house to decide whether I’m going to want the curtains open.

I get up and open them. He takes his cap off to me, lets it catch on the ear he’s got folded over, has to do a whole shaking business to get clear. At one time I used to signal the folded ear to him before he got this far. It gave him such a fright that the bread got to the subscribers cold.

I keep thinking about Ivo coming out after I’d given up on him. Ordinarily I’d have this evening arranged for us by noon. I try, out of habit, to work out what I could say that would bring him to me at that same speed, and the first three things I think of are things I’ve said to him already. I put on my coat. I can at least be at the stage door when he gets there.

* * *

By half past six the dressers have propped the stage door open to air out the costumes. The wet comes in off Gannet Lane and so does Ivo, knocking his heels together to get the leaves off. He put down the mat himself years ago when the owner said it was cheaper to pay a girl to clean the stairs. I can see the place where the coconut fibre has gone down to the backing. He can see it from the lane.

“This wants turning,” he says. “It’s good for a fortnight yet if you turn it.”

I get there with him at *fortnight* and say the rest with him. It has been a long time since he stood at this door and there was anything cheaper I could give him than a laugh. I used to get one when I said it with him. One year he laughed so hard he upset the girl with the programmes and we both went after them under the bench. He straightens up this time without taking his eyes off me and gets one boot under the mat. He pushes it out of the way so I have to step forward, off the dry square where I’ve been waiting for him.

“Did you have any idea I’d say that before you saw me?” he asks.

“Yes. I was standing here listening for it.” I can see him looking down at the mat again, and I’m afraid if he goes to work on how I might have done it he won’t come inside at all. “Will you come in where I can talk to you? It’ll only take a minute to get past the part you’re going to want to walk out on.”

“Will you let me decide when I get to that part?”

He picks the mat up from where he’s pushed it. He flips it over and works out the curl with the heel of his hand. I stand with my arms out of his way until he has all four corners down.

Inside he turns towards the stage, where someone is trying the low part of “The Herring Lass” on a fiddle. The player stops just short of the good note and Ivo supplies it under his breath, more air than whistle. He glances back at the stage entrance when he catches himself. I put a hand on his coat, and when he looks down at it I lift it. I have to ask him again to come into the tiring room. There are two girls at the long mirror, batting a powder puff across the glass from one to the other. I carry a chair out into the corridor instead, and he turns it to face the trunk where I sit. He has to sit a little forward so people can get by behind him.

When he took my wrist last night I thought he wanted to see the gold bracelets the Gannet gave me after I got my name up over the door. He put his thumb against the inside, where the one with the loose clasp always leaves a mark, and what looked like ink began to spread out under it. It is still there, a little oval with ridges in it the colour of a bruise. I pull my sleeve back to show him. His cuff is muddy, as if he’s come a long way on foot before coming the length of Gannet Lane.

“I told you last night what would happen at two minutes past ten,” he says. “I did it so I could have one day in which you’d take me seriously. The hand that made it can take it off. I came to see if you had anything you’d like to say to me while I’m here to hear it.”

I’d forgotten how much he wanted that sentence to sound businesslike. I put my hand back in my lap. “My super power isn’t actually clairvoyance. I don’t see the future, I live it. Whenever I die I can Groundhog Day and wake up the previous morning in a time loop.”

He looks along the corridor. The girls are still arguing over the good puff. There’s a little spit of heat from their curling tongs every time they set them back in the heater. He goes over to their door and shuts it far enough for the latch to catch, then comes back and draws the chair nearer.

“Why are you telling me this now?”

“Because I’ve been stuck on today for three hundred years!”

It comes out at the volume I use to reach the standing room. Somebody over our heads takes three steps and stops. The sound travels behind us along a beam and makes me think it has come from the mirror room, where I can suddenly imagine two girls pressing their cheeks to the cold inside of the door.

“I went off to North Market in the first week. I had everything for the journey except dry shoes, and people were very kind to me about the shoes. I sat in a station café in a pair of slippers somebody had lent me, looking at the time on a clock with a round green face. Your mark stopped my heart at ten-oh-two there as well. In the morning I was back in my bedroom. Here I could smell the yeast through the floorboards. I used to lie with my eyes shut, afraid I was still in some bit of dying I hadn’t managed to finish. Then I’d smell the yeast and know where I was, and for a second I was glad of it, every morning. When I thought what I’d have to do to get out of the bed I could have done without the second.”

Ivo reaches towards my wrist, and I draw it in against my stomach before I can help it. He puts his hand back on the chair. There’s a faint hollow in the wood just where he settles his thumb, where somebody used to stand on it to reach a peg. I can feel the pulse in my own thumb and want to push it down into that hollow myself. I’m going to have to say something that will keep him in this corridor.

“Do you remember lending me your lamp the night before the fire? Everybody was tearing down the set for the morning rehearsal and I wanted to get my shoes out of the scenery loft. I left the lamp burning up there. I came in at my usual time next morning and the fire was in the loft already, only I could go right past the stairs without seeing it. By the time I went up for a scene cloth there was smoke all the way down to the bottom step. You got hold of me by the back of the dress. You got me down the stairs and you carried me right across the stage to the flags outside. I remember you trying to get my hair out of my mouth. I thought if I could only spit it out I could save you the trouble. I died before I did. I woke up, got to the theatre so early the scene crew were still drinking their tea, and took them upstairs. The fire was small enough then. I made it look like a miracle, the way I knew where to send them.”

“You stood up in court. You took an oath that you’d seen me carry it up there.”

“They brought down your lamp, and that was the story I gave them.” The fiddle starts again, the same slow turn into the low part, and this time the player goes straight through it. I can’t remember her ever doing that during this conversation. Of course I’m here earlier, and when I work out by how much I’m surprised to find I’ve been calculating instead of looking at him. “It was your lamp and I knew I could make them believe me about where you’d taken it. They wanted a man to blame and a woman in a dress they could put in the paper. I liked the dress. I liked going through town in it. I could see girls coming out of shops to get a look.”

The chair gives a little when he leans on it. “The man from the paper came to the court every day. You could tell when he thought someone was going to be worth writing down. He’d lift his elbow off the side of the seat so he had space. When I stood up he never moved his elbow once. I could look straight at him and tell him I’d lent you the lamp and anybody in Brindle who’s ever taken a thing into a loft could have told him why I might lend it to you. He had room for what you said. He had the verdict across the front before they got me to the jail, and I was still wearing the good shirt I thought I’d get to go home in.”

For a long time I have depended on the fact that the editor likes the part where Ivo turns on me. I would wait to apologize until just when the man had tired of his seat, until he wanted one thing to laugh at and go home. I can always get Ivo there. On a good night I can get him there while he’s still got something to say, so that I can take what he says and turn it to account.

“I’ve said I’m sorry and had the whole house look at its feet,” I say. “And I’ve said it so well they clapped. Once I stood out there and told them about the lamp, while you were in the stalls. I’d picked a seat where you could see their faces. By the time I asked if you were satisfied you’d gone. I’d have noticed you going if I hadn’t been watching the editor. Last time I had you in the wing and was waiting for you to come out on my nod, and you left me to it. Then you came. I was dying by then, and you got down on one knee and put your hand out. I woke up thinking I’d give everything I have to see what you meant to do when you got down there. After that business with the fire they never gave you a chance to finish anything. I’d like to see you finish what you came here for.”

“Vella, I think I’d bend down if I saw what that mark did to you. I put it there and I expect it would be worse than I’ve allowed myself to think.” He drags the chair sideways to let a girl carrying two white hats get past. One of the feathers touches the back of his hand. “It didn’t occur to me you’d be up in the morning.”

The girl takes the hats into the wing. One of them is mine. In a minute she’ll be back to tell me I have to go and put it on because they’ve started sending people right down to the front. I’ve been having a little idea about the editor and how he might open his notebook for Ivo. The first two versions of it require Ivo to start on the same foot he always starts on, and I give those up. I get to a version I can imagine telling him about.

“I can get the house listening. Let me do that much. If you want to come out after that, do it when you please. If you want to ask me what I said to the court in a nice clear voice with my hands folded so they could all see, ask me. I’ll answer you in front of the man from the paper. I don’t know if I can make him write it. You might have better luck with him than I’ve had if I can let you get a whole sentence out.”

“He’ll be in the first box.”

“Where he won’t have to look past me if you go on from that wing.” I point at the further entrance. “You know how much of it he can see from there.”

Ivo gets out of his chair. He’s looking through the stage door to the wing, to the length of boards he’ll have to walk. “Leave me room to get there on my own.”

I want to ask whether he means to take his mark off, and find I’m leaning on the chair with the same hand he used earlier. I still want to ask him when I take my hand off it. From the stage the girl with my hat calls my name.

* * *

I go out a little before ten in my white hat. By now the Gannet has the smell of a wet dog resting in front of a stove. There’s always somebody in the front whose coat you could wring out. Tonight it’s the woman holding the empty seat beside her for someone who comes in late, and I can see exactly how much of her sleeve she’s given up to keep the seat.

“Well,” I say, which is how I start on any night people have been good enough to wait for me. “You’ll have heard that I can answer a question before it’s asked. Shall we see about that?”

The editor has his usual box, just below the clock and over the woman with the wet sleeve. If I were doing the whole business he’d write down what I said about answering questions, put one fingernail in his collar, and wait for me to look at him. I look straight up at his box and give it as close to his way of saying it as I can.

“Miss Pike, my dead uncle left me three pounds and a glass eye. Which of them does he want back?”

He’s already halfway out of his seat with his mouth open. I’ve always watched his mouth for the end of *eye*. Tonight I look at the three coins laid out end to end in his palm. I wonder how many evenings I let him bring the coins all the way here for that joke. He closes his hand, then opens it again. Somebody behind the woman with the wet sleeve says that sounded exactly like him.

“I’ve heard you ask me that, sir. Give me one you’ve never asked me before.”

From where he’s standing he has a view past me into the far wing, since the stage light goes just beyond the last length of curtain. I can see Ivo’s foot, the one he turns out. I’d told him where he could be seen, and he’s found a place further forward than that. The light reaches him nearly to the throat.

“Is that Ivo Rusk?” says the editor. “Can he come out here?”

For a second I’ve got my head turned to give Ivo the old nod, and he’s coming across in the middle of it. He passes me by far enough for the editor to see his whole face. Ivo looks down at the woman with the wet sleeve. She draws her arm off the seat and puts it in her lap, so you can see that it’s empty beside her.

“Where did you leave my lamp, Vella?”

I’d thought it would be about what I said in court. There’s half an answer to that in my mouth, about how beautifully I’d said it and where they’d put the witness chair. I can see the loft plain as if I’m standing at the top of the stairs. I’d balanced the lamp on the edge of the shelf, because I meant to go down with my shoes and come right back, and if I could just hold Ivo here while I found a way to make that sound like what anyone would do, I could perhaps tell it to the whole room.

“I left it in the scenery loft. Ivo, I left it burning. The next morning I took the scene crew up there and they put out the fire where it had started, on the shelf with your lamp. I swore I saw you carry it up there the night before. I stood at the bottom of the stairs with that lamp in my hand and watched you leave by the stage door. I lied in court.”

The boards give under somebody on the far side of the stage. I glance that way because I owe a great deal of my living to knowing when a person is about to come and relieve me of an awkwardness. The boards go back up. Whoever it was has stepped off again.

“Is that what you’re saying now?” the editor asks. He’s got out of his box and is coming down the aisle. “That you left it there and gave evidence against him?”

“That I left it burning. I knew he’d gone out.”

I see him write it that time. He puts the last word down and lifts his elbow, holding the book out where the light will show him the pencil strokes. Ivo watches him the whole time he’s making room. I’d seen him write all the way through my evidence. I’m trying to remember whether I saw the good shirt while I was speaking or only when the officer took Ivo away.

“Mr Rusk,” the editor says, “what did you tell the court?”

“I told them I’d lent it to her. She was standing there in her stocking feet, asking me for a light to find her shoes. I’d already got my coat on. I showed her how to open the little door on the lamp if it went out, and put my hand underneath hers until I knew she had the weight. Then I went home. I said it three times and the last time one of the men from the court asked where I’d got my coat, because he could remember the coat and couldn’t remember the lamp.” Ivo puts his thumb in the cuff of the coat he has on now. The best of the stitching is coming out of it. “You can write it once if you’ve got it.”

“The coat too?”

“Print why I lent her the lamp. The coat’s mine to worry about.”

There’s a half laugh in the middle of the room. Somebody calls out that the charge was arson, and a woman says yes, that was the charge, as if the first person was a bit slow at catching up. The woman with the wet sleeve has gone back to holding the seat, with the other hand this time. The editor looks up at Ivo. “I’ll put both of you in the morning paper, Mr Rusk.” He closes the notebook on his pencil and puts it in his coat. On his way out he puts a hand on the back of the empty seat to steady himself. Then I see the outside door swing shut behind him.

I turn upstage. I keep expecting a baton against a music stand, or to find somebody already down on one knee for the finish. Standing next to Ivo without anything to say is a new and very public position for me. I can see his shirt cuff where he’s pulled the stitching loose, and I want to ask if he needs somebody to put it right. I put three fingers to the brim of my hat, the way I do when they want a second trick. I take it off carefully, with the lining towards me.

Ivo holds his hand out. I turn my wrist so the mark faces up. His thumb covers it exactly, as it did when he put it there. Then he takes his hand away and there’s the little white place where he pressed and I can see the colour coming back into it, the very same pink as the rest of my wrist. I run a finger over the skin. All this time I’ve been able to stretch it and see the little lines of his print draw apart, like the wires of a cooling rack when you hold it too close to your eye. I stretch it again.

The clock gives its small swallow at two minutes past ten. I wait for the empty kick in my chest. In some of the other evenings I sat down in good time and in others there was such a rush to get somewhere that I was halfway through a breath when it came. Those were the worst. I put my hat down on the stage and let out what air there is in me. I take half a breath so I’ll have another half to take if I need it. My heart goes on working through all of this. It keeps at it even when Ivo stoops to put my hat out of the way of my feet. I find I want to say his name aloud, just to see if I can get through it.

At three minutes past ten the clock makes exactly the same sound. There’s a hand up in the second row. I can see a little ring on it and a place where a stone ought to be. I have the first word of an answer ready, out of habit. I stand where the light holds me and wait for her to speak.
