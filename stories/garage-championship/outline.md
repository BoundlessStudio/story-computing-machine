# A Little Play

Provisional direction: first-person mechanic Wes Dace, from the night before the final through the finish, with short, concrete glimpses of the six-month season. He wants to win the world lift-racing championship in the red-and-white bike he and his sister Cora built in his home garage. Cora's hands, judgments, and exasperation are present immediately; his machine has also become part of their ordinary life, inconveniently occupying the garage between a shepherd's bed and a cat's preferred warm shelf.

The bike is underpowered but light. Its front and rear lift units swivel separately through a mechanical steering linkage; the nose can point toward the next gate while the tail is still coming around. A compliant rear joint gives Wes unusually tight turns and some forgiveness, but the tail takes time to settle straight, delaying acceleration. Early disbelief became respect over six months of earned placings, mistakes, travel on prize money, and victories on technical courses. Champion Saskia Renz and her billion-dollar team are excellent: they have studied his lines, improved their own articulated design, and learned to deny his inside entry into a bend. Their extra power needs extra motor and battery mass; his residual advantage is low mass, not an uncopied secret. The final's long straight favors them. The six season qualifiers race for the championship outright in this final.

Tonight Wes has an adjustable rear damper bought with prize money. Stiffening the joint will shorten the lingering tail swing and get him accelerating sooner, but resist articulation and cost some of his tight-turn speed. They compare the settings in the permitted morning shakedown. The total laps are too close to call. On the old setup he gives a short opposite steering correction after the nose clears to arrest the trailing pod's swing; on the stiff setup the tail has already followed, so that habitual correction steers twice and loses alignment. Cora wants to race their proven setup and argues from the results. Wes chooses the stiffer one because Saskia has learned to deny the inside route where the old setup earns most of its advantage. This is his contested inference, not a test that certifies a winning configuration. No miracle spare, hidden power, disallowed shortcut, or opponent's crash will solve the final.

The race occupies substantial page space. Establish its last left-right gate pair and long straight before the decisive lap. Wes loses time to the setup's actual disadvantages, uses the bike's low mass to pass capable opponents, then catches Saskia. In an early attack a tempting inside gap triggers his old steering sequence under pressure; she denies the line and his unnecessary correction worsens his exit. He has failed to act on the timing he already knows from shakedown. Cora's gap, pursuer, and defensive-position calls materially help him. In the last lap, instead of repeating the blocked pass, he concedes the first apex and makes a wider entry to align the bike sooner for the following gate. That route has always been possible; with the old loose tail its exit loss made it unprofitable. He deliberately withholds the old correction and feeds power once the rear has already settled. The quicker response lets him recover the sacrificed first corner and carry a small lead onto the straight. Saskia's faster machine closes it. The result remains uncertain until the official display: intended outcome is an outright championship by a very small margin, with her finishing cleanly beside him.

Possible ending: Cora reaches him after shutdown. She has spent the race contesting his choices and trying to win, not acting only as a frightened relative. His hands are shaking too much to release a simple glove fastening; hers are no steadier. They laugh there against the warm, scuffed machine. End in that physical joy, without an explanatory speech, conversion of the crowd, or a second ending back in the garage. Names and local technology are new, non-canon story material; no established world figures or systems are borrowed.
