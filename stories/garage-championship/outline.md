# A Little Play

Provisional direction: first-person mechanic Wes Dace, from the night before the final through the finish, with short, concrete glimpses of the six-month season. He wants to win the world lift-racing championship in the red-and-white bike he and his sister Cora built in his home garage. Cora's hands, judgments, and exasperation are present immediately; his machine has also become part of their ordinary life, inconveniently occupying the garage between a shepherd's bed and a cat's preferred warm shelf.

The bike is underpowered but light. Its front and rear lift units are mounted on an articulated spine, using an old vehicle differential to divide the steering motion. The nose can point toward the next gate while the tail is still coming around. The compliant rear joint gives Wes unusually tight turns, with enough forgiveness for a former mechanic who learned to race it himself. It also takes time to straighten after a turn, costing speed on the straight. Early disbelief became respect over six months of earned placings, mistakes, travel on prize money, and victories on technical courses. Champion Saskia Renz and her billion-dollar team are excellent: they have studied his lines, improved their own articulated design, and learned to take away his entry into a bend. The final's long straight favors them.

Tonight Wes has a better rear damper bought with prize money. Tightening the joint will stop the lingering tail swing and get him accelerating sooner, but remove the forgiving delay in the controls. He is reluctant to disturb the machine that got them here. Cora insists on testing the actual choice: they compare the two settings in the permitted morning shakedown. The faster setting requires him to give up an old hand movement and put the bike precisely where he intends it. No miracle spare, hidden power, disallowed shortcut, or opponent's crash will solve the final.

The race occupies substantial page space. Wes loses time getting used to the exact setup under pressure, uses the bike's low mass and two-part steering to pass capable opponents through linked gates, then catches Saskia. She counters the line he used to beat her earlier in the season. In the last lap he must stop trying to repeat his old winning corner: he concedes its apex, makes a wider entry to align both units for the following gate, and uses the quicker-settling rear to carry speed onto the straight. Their last sprint is close enough that the final result remains uncertain until the official display. Intended result: an outright championship by a very small margin, with Saskia making him earn it and finishing cleanly beside him.

Possible ending: Cora reaches him after shutdown, initially angry because his radio went unanswered through the final section. His hands are shaking too much to release a simple glove fastening; hers are no steadier. They laugh there against the warm, scuffed machine. End in that physical joy, without an explanatory speech, conversion of the crowd, or a second ending back in the garage. Names and local technology are new, non-canon story material; no established world figures or systems are borrowed.
