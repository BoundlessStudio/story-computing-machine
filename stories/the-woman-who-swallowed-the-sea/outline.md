# The Woman Who Swallowed the Sea

Provisional direction, 2026-10-11.

During a storm supper in a grand house above an enclosed salt sea, Belise asks her husband Jowan to stay for a pear. He chooses two, peels hers badly, and exhibits an irritating, recognizably affectionate habit with the skins. When a wave breaks through the windows, he is trapped beneath fallen banquet furniture. After the other guests escape, Belise kneels in the hall with his sister Utta. She wants to reach his body and cannot bear the sea continuing to touch him. She takes salt water into her mouth, discovers that the swallow does not end at a mouthful, and deliberately keeps drinking. She sees a vessel's light pass inside her and still takes another swallow because the falling water now exposes Jowan's sleeve; she knows she has taken a boat before she knows she has taken people. Eventually the entire sea has entered her. Floating vessels and creatures come with it; Jowan's pinned body stays behind. This is one impossible, literal act in a fairy-tale register, with concrete consequences rather than a general magic system.

Jowan is dead. Belise has him back and still kneels beside him, but a lamp moves inside her chest: a living boat trying to navigate the storm she has swallowed. Her breathing drives its wind, speech produces violent weather, and an involuntary movement nearly founders it. She and Utta can see its people. Utta wants a turn holding her brother's hand, which Belise has denied while treating her as help. She is grieving, practical, and angry, not a source of supernatural knowledge. Belise has to discover through small bodily trials what her own mouth can return; an ordinary shell crosses her lips at its original size.

Belise first assumes the boat wants out and tries to push its water toward her throat. Its crew turn back against her imposed current to fetch a sailor whom her movement has thrown overboard. Their refusal changes the action: she must stop forcing the rescue she thinks they need, hold still while they take their own risk, and follow their lantern when they choose to leave. Her wanting Jowan does not vanish during this delay. A sob she cannot suppress would change their weather.

Belise must ask Utta to take Jowan and hear her actual answer before moving to the open terrace above the sea's old shoreline. Returning the water is ugly and exhausting rather than a pure song or noble sacrifice. The old storm will still exist outside, and the house must be above its returning reach. The likely ending is Belise's return to the hall, where Utta holds Jowan. They may share his weight without Utta absolving her. He remains dead; swallowing is no longer an ordinary act for Belise, but there is no manufactured magical penalty or cure. Protect the inner boat, the specific marriage, Utta's independent grief, and the image's contact between a kneeling woman and a drowned man. Length, sequence, and final movement remain open to discovery.
