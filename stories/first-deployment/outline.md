# Room on the Train — provisional outline

The explicit request promises an old combat robot's interview about its first deployment against the infected, roughly twenty years after the Zombie war ended. I infer the distinctive pleasure of a machine veteran's firsthand battle account: perceptions and capabilities a human soldier would not have, rookie assumptions tested in combat, and a later voice able to distinguish what it recorded from what it understood. The interview supplies a present listener and the distance of surviving the war. Tone, scale, interviewer identity, any intimate relationship, and a moral or trauma arc are unspecified. My proposed tone is dry, candid, increasingly urgent in the remembered action, with an earned warmth at the close; that is a choice, not a requirement.

My invented direction: BR-41, now called Forty-One, gives an oral-history interview for the twentieth anniversary of the armistice. Its first deployment was a counterattack meant to reopen an evacuation railway at Bellwether. A rookie combat machine, it possessed judgment and attachments before combat; this is not the day a program becomes a person. Its mistake was believing immunity to infection made it exceptionally safe. It can destroy infected at a rate humans cannot, but mass, damaged joints, ammunition, and heat still matter. Its governing relationship is with the people it is deployed beside and trying to get out, rather than a secret connection to the interviewer.

- The interview opens on a small error in the public record: the celebrated photograph of Forty-One outside Bellwether station is captioned as its arrival. It was taken near the end of the operation. The interviewer asks what came before it. The old robot answers with the loud, absurdly confident first minutes: a deployment crate opening, soldiers cheering the machines, and its first attempt to distinguish weapons fire from railway noise.
- In the station approaches, Forty-One and its section fight infected at close range. Their immunity allows them to advance through places human troops cannot hold, but the infected barely notice a lone machine until it obstructs their pursuit of living people. Concrete fire, movement, pressure, and an early damaged arm overturn the rookie's idea of a clean firing exercise. Its machine senses make the battle legible and strange without reducing it to statistics.
- A last evacuation train is trapped while infected pour into the tracks and platforms. The sound that organized the evacuation is drawing them toward the passengers. Forty-One recognizes that the enemy's indifference to it can be used: it can carry the loudest signal away from the train. It takes an evacuation loudspeaker onto the freight-yard approach, transmits through its own power, and commits to being the moving decoy while the remaining section clears the train's route.
- The lure works, then becomes a battle for distance and time. Forty-One must keep fighting and moving as bodies restrict its retreat and its damaged arm loses useful motion. It uses its gun and strength to bring down a loading gantry across the pursuit route, buying the interval the train needs. The train's departure is caused by its judgment and consequential combat power, together with the section's work, not by a civilian revealing a cure or teaching it compassion.
- The photograph catches it reaching the clear track as the last car passes. In the moment the official account calls triumph, a passenger is struggling to force an enormous suitcase out through the carriage door to make space for the robot. The offer cannot realistically fit Forty-One, but the passenger keeps trying. Forty-One had already chosen to protect them; the surprise is being wanted aboard. It survives and rejoins its section, with the broken arm becoming one of the old repairs visible in the interview.
- Back in the room, the interviewer revises the photograph's caption rather than extracting a speech about war. Asked what detail to keep when the account is shortened, Forty-One chooses the suitcase. The final image stays with the machine remembering the invitation after twenty years of peace; the combat has made that small gesture possible and given it weight.

The exact railway geography, supporting names, and balance of transcript with continuous recollection remain open to discovery in prose. The principal risk is sentiment or a generic lesson overwhelming the distinctive combat testimony; the sustained deployment action and Forty-One's tactical agency must earn the final memory.
