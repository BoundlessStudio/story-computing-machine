# The Long Way Through

The children at the starting rail could do a fair imitation of Corl's engine. They'd had all summer to practice. When he gave them a burst of the real thing, a boy dropped his paper flag and clapped both hands over his ears, laughing. Ione leaned out of her sled and handed it back. MAGIC was printed on one side, STEEL on the other. The boy held it up with MAGIC facing her.

Beside him a girl was running a pair of toy racers round the course on the back of a program. A pinecone stood in for the last turn in the road. As the girl pulled her little car in close, Ione found herself leaning. Across the grid, Corl had tipped his helmet just the same way. He caught her looking and sat up.

Five months after the crossing opened, there were still three competing translations for bread. Corl's people had shown up with a film of cars sliding in the snow, though, and someone had put it next to a racing print as old as Ione's grandmother. Even the crowds in the two pictures stood the same, leaning out over the rail to see who'd come out of the turn.

Now there was a silver cup between two officials, one with a stopwatch and one with a brass clock that struck the hours whether you wound it or not. They'd raise yellow for a Magic win and blue for Steel. The heats had been races in their own right. Only the final ran the whole course. The clock would keep running while she changed from sled to skiff, skiff to wing. She could see the white stripe of the finish on the grass below the grandstand. She'd have to go all the way around the lake to cross it.

Ione had imagined lifting that cup from the moment the silversmith had set it in his window. She wanted to know how cold it would be through her gloves.

The flag fell. She closed her hand on the sled's pull ring and gave its weight to a stone up the road. A white vein came alive in the stone. The sled fell toward it as though the road had stood up on end.

She let go before she struck and caught the next rock. The runners rang as she hauled herself up over the broken steps of the quarry road. Behind her, four driven wheels clawed at the loose stone. The engine climbed half a note, dropped as the tires caught, climbed again. The instant Corl found good road she felt the note in the floor of her sled.

The last hairpin turned them downhill. Ione caught the old black marker stone on the inside, a landmark with a stripe worn in it from two centuries of race paint. She cut in close enough to touch it. For a delicious second she had the whole road to herself. Then the stone was alongside her, pulling hard across her runners. She let go and braked to keep from sliding into the gravel.

Corl had been way out by the rail where the quarry fell away. He came across the road behind her, late enough in the bend that his front wheels were already straightening as he passed her elbow. He put his foot down. The note went up and stayed there. By the time Ione had a pull on a stone below the bend, she could see clean air between his back tires and her toes.

She'd seen what he was doing. There had been a moment, when his car was still wide, when she could have made the same choice. She'd been too pleased with the empty road.

* * *

At the lake she left the sled ticking in its catch rack and ran down the boards. She got into her skiff with one boot still untied. Out in the bay Corl's boat rose on its foils until she could see daylight under its belly. The wake narrowed to a white pencil stroke. Her own skiff would go that fast if she could find a rock to pull her. There were rocks all along the shore and precisely none in the middle of the bay.

She hauled off the quay hard enough to stand the skiff on its tail, let go and shot out over the water. The instant she cut her line she could feel the lake getting its hands on her hull. Corl was opening up a lead big enough to fit the whole grandstand into.

He had to come inshore for the rock channel. Ione could pull straight to the red entry post, as she had done in the heats, and turn when she got there. She remembered Corl's front wheels coming straight under him at the bottom of the bend. She looked past the post to a low gray slab farther along the shore.

The slab took her weight. For a breath she was going the wrong way, skimming across the mouth of the channel with the tether pulling at her broadside. The skiff came onto one edge. The heel of her loose boot filled with cold water. She hung on until the bow swung into the gap between the rocks. Then she opened her hand. She was going faster when she passed the post than she had been when she first saw it.

She could catch Corl here. White veins lit along the channel in quick succession as she drew herself through. His boat came down off its foils to turn. Ione could see the side of his helmet and the little round mirror in which he'd have seen her coming. He looked away from the mirror toward a cleft in the rocks. The water there was pale as bottle glass. He'd ground the foil to pieces if he took that gap at speed.

He pulled a lever. The foil tucked up against the hull. He threw the boat sideways through the cleft on its bare belly and kicked up a fan of spray that struck both rock faces. On the far side the foil dropped, bit, and lifted him clear again. Ione took the bend at the end of the channel with both hands on the ring. Corl had been out in the bay when she reached her skiff. At the wing racks he was only a few strides ahead of her, one wet trouser leg flapping as he ran for his plane.

Her own boot came off on the ladder. She left it.

* * *

Her wing was ash struts and cloth, with a seat slung between the spars and a pull ring where her right hand fell. She belted herself in. The launch attendant had barely cleared his hands when she gave her weight to the first pinnacle and went up so fast the seat took the breath out of her.

The next stone burned white above her. She went up again. In practice it had taken Corl most of the lakeside strip to get that little plane of his off the ground. Here it had a downhill rack to launch from, but it still climbed on a slant. She could climb straight up. By the time they reached the last of the high rocks, he was level with her along the course and low enough that she could see the white stripe down his helmet through the cockpit glass.

Two spires stood at the edge of the high ground. The aerial gate hung between them. If you flew square through the gap, the cliff rose dead ahead. The finish was downhill to the left, so racers usually went through and banked hard as soon as they were clear. Ione had once clipped the cloth on a practice run there. When she went to retrieve it, a little triangle of yellow had been snapping in the wind a hundred feet up the stone.

Corl dropped his nose well before the gate. His dive took him out into the open, past the stones she would have needed to make the same line. For the first time she heard the propeller from above, a thin fretful sound as he built up speed. He had the short way to the gate. He could afford to use it. His propeller would still be pulling after he banked, out where there was nothing left to catch.

Ione raised her hand to take the usual anchor on the left spire. She could have been through the gate in two heartbeats. She could feel the turn in her wrists already, the tug as she came alongside the stone and had to kill her speed. There was just enough room to catch the base of the spire and haul herself round a second time. She watched Corl going down toward the gate and remembered the strip of empty road he'd left between his tires and the old black stone.

She opened her hand and banked wide to the right. The grandstand slid out from under her wing. A pale shoulder on the left spire came into view, high up on the face that looked into the gap. She caught it and put her nose down.

For a second the wing kept going wide. This high up there was a long way to fall. She let the wing fall, the pull coming sideways now as it had on the lake, bringing her round without asking her to give up the speed of the dive. Below her Corl went through the gate first. His left wing dropped as he turned. Ione came at the opening slantwise from the right. As the line drew her round, the cliff slipped out of view and she could see straight through the gap to the grass. She held the ring until the finish stripe lay directly between her bare foot and her boot.

She let go.

The line vanished and she shot diagonally between the spires. The wing gave a crack like a sail in a gale. Grass came up so fast she lifted her toes inside her one boot. Corl's propeller came back into earshot. He was still gaining. The shadow of his wing nosed into the edge of hers, and the stripe ran under her feet.

She had to land without looking behind. The wing skipped twice, then dragged to a stop in the long grass. Corl's wheels hit the ground just beyond her. She looked back at the grandstand. Both flags were yellow.

* * *

The cup was warm. The official who gave it to her had been standing with it in the sun. Less than half a wing, he told her. Ione took her gloves off anyway. There was hardly room to breathe around the silver for all the people who wanted to touch it.

Later she found Corl back at her wing, looking at a wrinkle where the cloth had pulled against the spar. He made a little swooping motion with one hand.

“Here?” he said.

She set the cup in the grass and took a piece of race chalk from her pocket. She drew the two spires on the wing cloth, then the long arc she'd made past them. He borrowed the chalk and drew a line farther out than hers. He put the chalk down at the point where he would open up the engine. She leaned close to see if it would work.
