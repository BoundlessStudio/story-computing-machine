I'd like five minutes alone with the man who drew me this chart. See the arrow he's put through the reef, right where there's a gravy spot? You could sail two ships abreast through that, if he were to be believed. I can see the real opening now, with just enough daylight to tell which part of the black water I'm likely to strike a rock in. I put the chart away. If I come through, I'll want it to show him where he can go.

On the deck beside me there's a little screw I keep meaning to put back in a hinge. It slides away toward the scuppers and I pin it with my boot. That's a poor use of a good foot in this sea, but I put every screw in this boat myself. When I bought the hull there was a fire laid in her, with the sticks all the right way up, ready for somebody to get warm on the beach. The man who sold her to me took the sticks out first. I think he saw that I'd have kept them.

There's a shove in the tiller now, right where I left the applewood knot. The current at the gap is running to my left, and if I aim straight through it, I'll have time to say the name of my ship before I hit. I let one big swell pass. The next comes in under the stern and I put the bow at the reef on the right and let the water carry me across. Here's the bit I like. For a few long seconds there's a rock in front of the *Spite* and another one coming up beside her, both white to the top with water. Then she gathers herself in the cut and tears through to the basin. The end of my braid blows into my mouth. I taste salt when I spit it out and shout loud enough to turn a whole shelf of birds out of bed.

The tender comes through on its towline with two empty sacks tumbling around in it. The water inside the reef lies dark and easy. At the far end of it, where the shale of Adder's Kettle goes straight down into the sea, an arch spits white water as though somebody in there is throwing it. Right where the man with the chart said. I push the screw toward the hinge with my toe and get up to let go the anchor. I bundle the sail as the chain runs out. The wind off the Kettle smells of wet fish bones. The sealers left their boiling pots here before I was born. I had to show the man with the chart the island's name in an old whaler's book, though he'd pulled a gold candlestick up in his nets off the reef.

I haul the tender alongside and look at those empty sacks. There's a heavy ship in the convoy with a red gallery at her stern, all glass. Last time she went by I got close enough to see a man with a napkin at his neck lift the lid off his supper. I want to come up on that side of her again, with three fast boats spread out behind me, and have him see a little brown sail he remembers before his captain sees the guns on either side. I mean to eat at that man's table with my boots on.

I climb down into the tender, take up the oars, and let the last of a surge wash me under the arch. The whole cave booms with it. The water comes up over a shelf of rock and falls away again to show an old iron cleat. I catch it with the bowline and get a knee onto the rock. It's cold enough to hurt. There's still a rind of black fat in the bolt hole and I get a smell of old seal oil on my hand as I make the tender fast.

When I hang my lantern on the wall, something yellow lights all the way up from the wrack at my boots to the split ribs of a ship propped against the back of the cave. I take out my cutlass. There's a chest with a lid swollen out of its frame and I get the point under the corner. It lifts and the coins come away from the lid a few at a time. Then the bottom gives out and the whole weight goes through a crack in the wood and down a slope of gold dishes and broken blue cups. Coins spin on the stone. One goes under a wrist bone with a bracelet still around it and stays there, shining up through the bracelet.

I get down on one knee. There are pearls in the palm of a broken silver hand, and rings with stones the size of the knuckles they were made to go over. I brace the guard of the cutlass against my knee and put my other hand flat in the gold just to see how far my fingers go in. The pile is warm near the lantern and cold a finger's length down. Under it, barnacles on the wood are sharp enough to draw blood. This was under the sea long enough for things to grow on it. Any pirate could tell me what that means. What the ocean claims, the ocean keeps. I've heard men say it after they'd lost something in two feet of water and couldn't be bothered to get their stockings wet.

I pick a gold coin that has been worn down at one edge, so the king on it looks to be getting out of his chair. I hold it up to the arch where the next breaker is shouldering in. Then I find the pocket of my red coat and put it in.

The breaker falls. I can hear the coin go down the lining and catch on a stitch. I wait for the sound of the water hitting the cave and it never comes. A whole sheet of it is running off the shelf below my boots without making a sound.

“You may take whatever you can carry, little thief. But I will take something of equal value.”

The voice comes from the water below the shelf, where the lantern hasn't got a bottom to shine on.

I put the point of the cutlass in the sea. I can hear the drops when I lift the blade, and the oars knocking on the boards where I left them. The tender is going up and down on a swell that ought to have drowned the sound of a gun.

I lay the cutlass on the shelf and get the coin out again. The king has left a cold place in the lining where he was.

“Who puts a price on a thing after a woman has it in her pocket?”

“I was waiting to see whether you would put it there. You had it up in the light long enough for both of us to look at it.”

I turn the king over. The edge has been clipped thin enough to cut with. I wouldn't have given a full coin for him at a counting house. “Suppose I went away with this one?”

“I would have the copper you wear in your ear. In the left ear, where you have the hole a little too close to the edge.”

The water on the shelf stands up beside me. It comes up past my shoulder in a ribbon and bends toward my head. I clap my palm to my ear so hard that all the water and the gold go sideways for a second. When I take my hand away the copper is there and the ribbon has folded itself into the sea. I can feel the thin end of the hook sticking into my finger. It always sticks there.

The copper was a halfpenny when I took it. I got my hand into a captain's pocket at a christening and held my fist closed all the way back across the harbor, even when it was my turn at an oar. I had felt silver in the pocket and thought I had it. Back ashore I opened my fist and the other girl from the boat saw what I had. She laughed herself sick. We could have put it together with what she had and got something to eat. I went home hungry. I beat the halfpenny thin between two stones so I could wear it and leave both hands free for the next time.

I take the copper out of my ear and hold it by the hook. The king is getting warm in my other hand. I put him away and feel for the little hole until the hook catches.

There are pearls between two ribs of the wreck, threaded on a cord that's gone soft. I turn one of my rings inward to get a finger in the loop and draw the pearls out a little at a time. One of them is a dull grey until I have it between me and the lantern. Then there's such a play of color on it that I turn my head, thinking there might be more light coming in than I know about.

“I could sell this to a woman I know who gives her daughters a pearl each time they tell her what the other one has been up to. She'd get quite a lot of news out of it. You could have my sword for the pearls and the coin. I took it off a man who said you could lay any blade of his across a log, give it a crack with a hammer and have it come up straight. I made him do that with this one. He was as pleased as I was.”

I pick up the blade and set it across the rock where the water can reach it. As soon as I let go I want it back. That ought to be a point in my favor. A breaker comes in as high as the arch. It lays a thin mat of weed over the place where I came up out of the tender, and goes out with a piece of a king's silver plate that I had been wondering how to get past the ribs. The plate goes down turning, the light on its underside going from a strip to a little bright edge and back again. Then the light goes too.

“That is a fine blade,” the sea says. “You'd have another before the end of the week. I have watched you step off a ship without so much as a shirt of your own and come back with everything you left without. You are a very difficult woman to take from.”

I pick up the sword. I like hearing that last part so well that it takes me a little while to look past the shelf and see that the water in the middle of the basin is higher than the water at the edges. It has risen in a long rounded ridge underneath *Spite*. The ridge has clear faces, like good glass, and along the one nearest me I can see her all the way down to her keel. The anchor chain slants back into the basin and her head hardly moves.

There is a patch on her port side cut out of the door of a pig shed. It is lighter than the rest of her. I had to bend the board a little at a time or it would split and I'd be back on the hill in the dark undoing hinges. I slept with it clamped between the two uprights of the bed. For three nights it sprang straight when I got up. When it held at last I was half sorry to take it down. I liked to lie under it with my hands behind my head and look up and see the shape of the part of the boat I'd been missing.

A dark line of water curls up under her bowsprit. It runs the length of the hull without breaking. It's the way you put a finger along a piece of cloth you'd like to buy while the man's still telling you the price.

The cord breaks. I'm down on my knees getting what pearls I can before the water takes them, and one goes right over the edge. I put my hand out after it and get my sleeve full of cold water. I ought to laugh at myself for minding when there's a cave full of them. I can still see the place the pearl went in and I sit there with my cuff dripping long after I've lost sight of it.

I open one of the meal sacks. In go the pearls and a fistful of gold to hold the mouth open. There's a chain I have to haul out of a goblet link by link. The clasp catches on the rim. I get it loose and see that the red stones in it are as thick as the first joint of my thumb.

There are three cutters laid up below the sail loft, fast boats built to chase people like me. The yard man let me go over their decks once. When I made him an offer he took me back onto the dock and told me to take my time admiring them. I rub the clasp on my sleeve. A sackful like this and I can buy all three. I start to laugh, thinking of the convoy captain running out his ladder to welcome the customs men aboard. I'd like to be halfway up it before he thinks to look down.

I fill the sack until I can't lift it clear of the shelf in one hand, then put things back until I can. I sit with the sack between my knees. Out in the basin, the ridge under *Spite* is going down. She swings till I can see the strip of bare wood where I take hold of the stern rail to come aboard. The paint went first. Now the wood is worn smooth as a button. I could empty the sack, leave the king with the rest, and have my hand on that rail before she swings the other way.

I get the king out of my pocket. I hold him in the palm of my hand as though I'm about to lay him down. The clasp is still warm where I rubbed it clean, there at the top of the sack. I can't keep my other hand off it. I put the king away.

“For what's in this sack,” I say, “it would have to be *Spite*.”

The sea lets her down the rest of the way. The chain dips and she turns a little. One of the gaskets holding her sail flicks in the wind.

“I have ships that came to me with everything still on the supper table,” the sea says. “One has an orange in the captain's desk. He put it in the drawer for a child who couldn't eat it just then. I have whole ships, Ness Calder. They come to me at the end of what they know how to do.”

A draught across the shelf brings me the bird stink again. The bottom of the sack is wet through where I have set it on the rock, and I get it up onto my dry knee.

“She's very fast in that wind,” I say.

“I saw. You brought her through the reef as though you would be sorry to go around it. If you leave me the vessel, I will take her when you are in your tender.”

I put my heel down on the mouth of the sack. From where I sit I can see the cut in the outer reef, and the water is going across it faster now that the tide has turned. I would get an oar blade in twice before the bow of a loaded tender went broadside to that current. I could turn around and take the king out of my pocket and put him back and go home with the hands I came with and the ship that knows them. If I can make a fair show of getting up to do it, the sea will have to see how close a thing it is.

“There's a good place to get on a reef with a sack of gold in a tender.” I raise myself on the sword hilt. “Or you could hold the water in the cut for me, and I could bring you the *Spite* with her sail full. I'll stay aboard long enough to put her through. I'll have the tender on a line behind me and the gold in it. When I've got into the tender and cut the line and have the whole length of the reef behind me, you'll get a ship with all her way on her. You can take her straight down if you like. I want to see if she can do it.”

The sea takes its time. I can see a bit of the old cleat through the water and each time the surge comes in the tender comes closer to banging against it. I get the rope free of the weed while I'm waiting. The knot is cold and stiff. I work it gently from two sides, with the hilt of my sword between my knees. The second sack is still empty, blown into the bow of the tender. If the sea says no, the one I've filled will go in beside it just as empty. I'm trying to imagine picking up that weight of gold a second time, to put it back, when the sea speaks.

“I'll give you deep water through the cut. I shall wait for you to cut your tender free beyond the last of the white water. I would like to see how much sail she can carry.”

A small wave breaks at my feet and makes a noise like a coarse shirt being torn. Then I can hear the whole weight of the sea again. I put the sword back through my belt, tie the mouth of the sack and get it onto my shoulder. It's heavier than when I lifted it standing still. I can feel the king pull at the corner of my pocket when I stoop for the tender. I put my free hand up against the arch and push myself and everything I'm taking out into the light.

* * *

By the time I get back to *Spite*, the sun has come high enough to get into the bottom of the tender. I wedge the sack under the thwart and make it fast there. I loosen the mouth just far enough to draw the clasp through and see the stones in daylight. I tie the sack shut again with the chain caught in the knot and the clasp hanging down outside.

I let the tender back on its towline, weigh the anchor and get sail on. The wind takes the mainsail so hard that the last turns of the sheet knock against the hull before I get them in. There is room in the basin to bring her round under the arch, and for a while we go along the shore close enough to see the nests built one on top of another in the cracks. I give her the jib too. All that canvas makes a deep smooth sound when it fills, a sound I can feel down to my knees where I have them braced against the seat. The tender comes along after us in little jumps.

At the opening in the reef I can see what the sea has given me. There's a long belt of water where the reef used to show through, green as a bottle in the places where it was too shallow to go. The current is running across the belt fast enough to carry a piece of foam out of sight while I watch. The bow of the tender swings out first. I feel it pull at *Spite*'s stern, and then the tiller begins to kick where I have my thumb. A big swell is gathering outside the cut. I have to get through ahead of it or it'll catch the tender sideways and take us both around.

I take the braid out of my mouth and put the tiller over. The bow comes around toward the first reef tooth and keeps coming. If it were just the cutter there would be room to put her head down sooner, but the tender is still sweeping out behind us. I hold her there with the tooth in front of me, close enough to see the yellow in the stone, until the towline comes tight again. The stern lifts. I give the sail the wind and the next stretch of water comes at us so fast I can't make out the shapes of the rocks as we go through. The back of the swell runs under us out on the far side. The tender comes through after us with a bucketful of foam in the bow and the sack still under the thwart.

I could keep on like this for miles. There's a screw lying against the hinge on the deck in front of my boot. I can see a white fleck of paint on the head of it. I ought to put it back in.

I lash the tiller at the angle that holds her to the wind and haul the tender in till its bow knocks against *Spite*'s stern. My sleeve catches and for a breath I have my hand round the tiller again, as easily as if I had come back to it after putting the kettle on. The knot fits in the soft part of my thumb. If I let my fingers stay there, I can feel the exact instant the sail lifts the lee rail. I feel it once and turn to get my foot over the stern.

I put one boot on the tender's thwart and hang on to *Spite*'s rail until my other leg is clear. The rings on my left hand scrape the wood as I let go. I go down hard into the tender, both knees hitting the sack. The shock of it goes up my spine. I get on my feet with the cutlass out. There is no more white water behind me. I get the blade under the towline and cut it away from us.

The sea opens on a long slant in front of *Spite*. Water draws back on either side and the loose end of the towline whips out behind her. She goes down the slant with her sail full, so fast that I nearly give her the shout I give her in a race. I have a clear view of the pig shed plank and of the iron heads of the nails where it joins the one above it. One of them stood proud and for a whole winter I would run my hand down the hull when I went by in the tender and know I had the right spot because that nail had a burr on it. I can see just how far it sticks out now. The green water comes up over the board. I lean away hard, where I'd put my weight if I were aboard and wanted the rail to stay clear. The tender goes down on that side and takes a little water over the lip. It runs under the sack and comes cold through the stitching in my boot.

I sit down and take up the oars. The ocean closes over *Spite*'s deck. I expect the canvas to fall in on itself when the sea gets to it, but the water comes together over the gaff and she goes on with every inch of sail drawing. I can see the whole spread of it until she turns. By then I'm pulling for open sea. The red clasp hanging from the sack knocks against my heel every time I pull. Far below the thin white line of my wake, the green peak of her sail crosses to the other side.
