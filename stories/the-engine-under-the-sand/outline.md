# What the Iron Takes

Provisional direction: close third person through Sivet, a veteran scavenger; roughly 3,500 words over one day. Her adult daughter Anka has bought passage on a trading caravan. Sivet is supposedly repairing Anka's handcart but has quietly removed its axle, making the promised departure impossible without admitting she wants Anka to stay. Anka has plans and salvage skill of her own, and suspects the delay.

At dawn, while the two strip a buried wheel housing for a saleable bronze part, an inner engine begins turning. A concealed source of power remains unexplained, but its immediate action is visible: a great magnetic rotor gathers speed and pulls ferrous metal toward it. Bronze, wood and flesh are unaffected; iron objects tremble before they slide or fly. Their generation has made its ordinary life from this iron. A cooking knife, a cart part, then roof ties become dangerous. The field strengthens as the rotor accelerates; it does not distinguish a cherished object from scrap.

The engine belongs to a monumental wheeled machine still held down by an old cable running to an overturned wreck. Its powered wheels strain toward a buried route, grinding in place while its magnetic assembly drags the settlement's metal back. There is no awakened voice, readable enemy, or complete account of the war. The mother and daughter can discover enough of the old physical arrangement to act without recovering its purpose. Anka tests the pull and insists people evacuate with only what they can safely carry. Sivet tries to arrest the rotor with salvage; the failed attempt costs metal and precious time, and Anka learns what happened to her axle.

Sivet's workable choice is to sever the remaining cable so the machine can move beyond the settlement, carrying its dangerous field and much of their salvaged life away with it. Cutting means surrendering the iron she has accumulated and the tools by which she expected to keep Anka close. Anka supplies the way to approach and cut; they act together without resolving their quarrel into instant forgiveness. The engine travels into the waste, hauling a ragged train of scrap. Its future destination and original mission remain unknown.

Afterward, Anka still takes her passage. Sivet gives her a wooden tool that cannot be recalled and tells her plainly about the cart. Their parting happens in the metal-stripped home, with the far-off engine still moving. The ending should hold the daughter's chosen departure and the mother's reduced, continuing life without a speech or a new communal maintenance ritual.
