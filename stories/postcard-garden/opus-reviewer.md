# Opus review: The Address of Here

## Verdict

- Dialogue: PASS — every exchange passes the Ground check, the speakers are easy to tell apart, and the humor comes from the situation rather than from comebacks; one weakly grounded cue line (D1).
- Prose: PASS — clean, concrete, and varied, with no systemic tic; one over-explained recognition beat and two word-level clarity slips.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt's promise, the required line, the length (about 3,440 words) and the date arithmetic all hold; two minor slips (the sleeve, a stray "them").
- Edit scope: polish
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; agree, though it did not note the small narration and continuity slips below.

This story avoids the collection's house voice. Nobody corrects anyone's wording as a conversational rhythm, nobody states the theme, and the most important lines are small and ordinary ("The tomatoes are mine."). Zinnet asks short questions that run slightly ahead of her evidence. Ovolo thinks in frames and crops and deflects when caught out. The two strangers are chatty or half-busy with their own work, and Agata's recording rambles the way a person holding a camera does. Friction travels through gesture rather than argument: the blossoms cropped closer, the six exclamation marks, the edited city view she declines to use. The edit pass should touch dialogue only at D1 and otherwise leave the exchanges alone. The main risk is an editor "sharpening" lines that work because they are plain.

## Edit guardrails

- Keep the postcard's exact words "Wish you were here." (L37) and the card's lack of address, signature and stamp.
- Close third on Zinnet, past tense. The prompt's length band is 2,500–4,000 words and the story is at about 3,440, so fixes should trim or swap words, not add scenes.
- Zinnet is a young gardening android (she has been alive for nine festivals and will be twenty in eleven years). Keep her reference-image traits: ivory and gold body, amber eyes, dark gathered hair with small flowers, and the shoulder solar panels.
- Keep the evidence limits. The card's writer stays unknown, the arrival packet proves arrival but not present survival, and no reply arrives. Do not retrofit the tin's brown seeds or the white tree into proof.
- Keep the date arithmetic intact: launch 412 years ago, a 48-year voyage, an 11-year signal, archive receipt 353 years ago, 11-year delivery and a 22-year round trip.
- The tone is broadly accessible and funny. The story is set in a magicless technological era and contradicts no LOCKED or CANON universe entry. Keep it free of magic, crossovers and water infrastructure (it currently has none).
- The dialogue already works. Do not add wit, corrections or explanatory lines, and do not have anyone say what the postcard or the recording "means".

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · Weakly grounded question cues the ships

- Where: L53–L57, Zinnet and Ovolo over the card on the roof
- Text:
> "Humans went there?"
> "Humans were everywhere. Before the ships."
- Problem: Ground/uptake (mild) and Exposition cue. The cabinet downstairs carries human handwriting (L29), and Zinnet learned in lessons that humans lived in the cities before the ships (L57). Her surprise that humans "went" to the southern terraces therefore has little basis. The verb also blurs "were humans there?" with "is that where humans went?". Ovolo's reply works for either reading, so the exchange does not break, but the question mostly exists to cue "Before the ships" and the lesson paragraph after it.
- Fix: Make the question about this card's place or person rather than humans in general. Her excitement then has a basis, and Ovolo's reply can still introduce the ships. Alternatively, cut the pair and let L57 follow from her looking at the card. Illustrative only: "A human stood there? In the terraces?"

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Provenance checklist stalls the recognition beat

- Where: L211–L213, arrival recording, Zinnet recognizing the older Agata
- Text:
> She brought up the old portrait beside it, then the packet's dated crew list.
> Both belonged to Farleaf's voyage record; the arrival packet carried the ship's identifier and its new position.
- Problem: "The grin narrowed her eyes." (L211) already lands the recognition, and L199–L203 established that this is Farleaf's arrival packet. At the story's emotional peak, the crew list, identifier and position read like an evidence audit. "Both" also has no clear antecedent: it could mean the portrait and the crew list, or the index and the list.
- Fix: Cut the crew-list clause and the "Both belonged…" sentence. Keep the passenger-index line, the side-by-side portrait, "Agata had survived the crossing," and the sentence comparing the two faces.

### P2 · Minor · Two word-level clarity slips in narration

- Where: L101, the terraces, just after "by the thousand"; L185, the information table
- Text:
> The three trees, which a moment earlier had seemed so distinct, now looked distressingly repeatable.
> She put the card beside the portrait on her display.
- Problem: L85 never counts the trees, so "The three trees" arrives unannounced. "Distinct" (separate) is also standing in for "distinctive" (unmistakable). At L185, "her display" could be Zinnet's own viewer or the museum's wall display. The next sentence's "the two images" suggests a digital side-by-side, but the reader has to guess.
- Fix: Give the count at L85 ("the three white trees") or drop "three", and swap "distinct" for a word meaning unmistakable. At L185, say where the two images sit: in her vision, on a handheld screen, or held up against the print.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · The green sleeve points at the photographer

- Where: L33, the postcard first described; L163–L169, the print, the caption and Agata's portrait
- Text:
> A sleeve at one edge suggested someone had stepped out of the photograph too slowly.
> The tree's white flowers were clear, the sleeve green.
> She had tied her green overshirt around her waist.
> That was who had stood behind the camera.
- Problem: L163 makes the stray sleeve green, and two sentences later Agata is given a green overshirt (she wears a green shirt again at L209). That invites the reader to take the sleeve as Agata's. But the caption makes her the photographer, and L169 says she stood behind the camera, so the sleeve cannot be hers. It may belong to the companion who took her portrait, but nothing signals that, so as written it reads as a slip.
- Fix: Drop "the sleeve green" at L163, give the sleeve another color, or let Zinnet register at L169 that it belongs to someone else. Keep Agata's green overshirt, because L209's faded shirt depends on it. Illustrative only: "The green sleeve at the edge, then, had been someone else's."

### C2 · Minor · "them" puts the seedlings on the ridge

- Where: L253, Ovolo's call, summarizing the recording
- Text:
> Agata took them out to the ridge.
> She carried the camera through the doorway.
- Problem: The nearest plural antecedent is the seedlings ("They're a bit leggy." / "I know."), so the sentence says Agata carried the tray up the slope. In the recording (L227) she carries only the camera, and the tray is never mentioned again. If "them" is meant to be the two viewers, it does not read that way.
- Fix: Name what she took. Illustrative only: "Agata carried the camera out to the ridge."

## Recurring patterns

- Dialogue tics: none recurring. Correction ping-pong, epigram closers, thesis lines and explanatory choruses are absent. The few neat lines (Agata's "It has a roof.", Zinnet's text "One day. I spent the other on pears.") each occur once, are earned, and fit the speaker.
- Drifting referents and counts in narration: L101, L185, L253 (P2, C2). When editing, watch for the same loose "them", "both" or "three".
- Provenance over-explanation: L213 (P1). L241's date paragraph is needed for the timeline and should stay.
