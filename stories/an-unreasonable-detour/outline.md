# An Unreasonable Detour

Provisional direction, 2026-10-16. A warm road story in close third through Princess Sereya, an adult who is practiced at being a pleasant guest. The reader should feel her small withheld wishes before she has language for the larger one. Three adventurers escort her across a mountain pass toward a wedding she has agreed to: Haddie, their practical captain; Osnet, an older scout with elaborate manners and appalling cooking; and Ket, a young hedge-mage whose narrow useful magic is not a solution to the journey. No connection to an existing kingdom or recurring character is intended.

The contract begins where the carriage road ends. They are paid to get her over the pass alive and on time; a substantial balance waits at the bridegroom's house. Sereya is careful to take up very little trouble. The party begins by making room for the princess's equipment and treating her as an easily damaged responsibility. Over several days, their assumptions inconvenience one another. She has blisters, dislikes a food they love, finds one of their running jokes profoundly unfunny, and eventually makes them laugh on purpose. Before the storm, she asks to sleep outside rather than at a comfortable inn. Haddie objects to the extra work, cold, and lost cooked supper; Sereya persists, and they make an irritated compromise. The night is chilly and she likes it anyway. Her curiosity about what this life feels like is already a wish, with a small consequence, before she receives the invitation. She does not discover exceptional martial or magical ability. A passing pack train gives Haddie's fear of horses a context; Sereya learns to accommodate it quietly. The princess's company starts to matter even when it accomplishes nothing.

A storm makes the direct road unsafe. Reaching an abandoned hilltop observatory requires an ugly, tiring climb; Osnet is hurt getting them there. Sereya spends part of the night with him while the others handle shelter and his injury. She is awkward at care and cannot deliver a polished inspirational tale. What begins as a bad attempt to distract him becomes an increasingly ridiculous story that she cannot quite finish. He wants the rest. The night is an actual shared ordeal, with cold, fear, irritation, and perhaps a brief clearing sky through the old observatory roof. Any magic remains small and clearly supplied by a living person.

Afterward they still have to get to the wedding. Their closeness should be visible in how they travel and disagree, not only announced at departure. Sereya's future husband has written considerate letters. They have discussed a small walled garden which will be hers, with no duties to receive visitors there; he has left its beds empty for her to choose. She has genuinely wanted that privacy and security, and can still want it. She meant to make a life with him; he is not secretly cruel, and the wedding is not the only barrier to a peace treaty. Leaving will hurt a person and disappoint people at home. It will also leave her without the comfortable position already prepared for her.

On the last ridge, within sight and easy reach of the wedding city, the party asks whether she would rather come adventuring. Haddie risks the unpaid balance by asking, and Osnet awkwardly reveals that the invitation was discussed without Sereya. They offer a real place among them, with uncertainty and ordinary work, rather than an escape they expect her to repay through extraordinary usefulness. She must choose without being assured the road will always feel like this journey.

Possible ending: she goes down to tell her intended husband herself while the party waits outside the city. We accompany her for a brief lived conversation. His disappointment cannot simply become wise absolution, and she must say she will not share the life they have discussed. She returns apprehensive and wanting to hear where the party is going. The unfinished story may resume during their departure, provided that does not erase the hurt or become an explanation of what freedom or family means. The unreasonable detour in the title is the one from her prepared life; the storm refuge need not be an irrational decision. The prose may find a better stopping point.
