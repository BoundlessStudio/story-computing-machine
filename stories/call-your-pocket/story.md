# Call Your Pocket

I’d made the tie on a Sunday when I had the shop to myself. The last good length of black silk was just enough for the wide end, and the tail took two scraps. I put the join where it would spend its life against my shirt. I liked that tie. I liked to have it straight.

By ten on Friday night the bench at the Bobbin was full behind me. I’d been watching Riva Tallis’s left hand at the far end of the table. When she took a shot that hand lay down on the cloth as flat and sure as if somebody had put a weight on it. There was a red rose inked above it. Tonight there was also a flash of red under her tartan where she leaned. My hand went to my knot a third time and she grinned at me over her shoulder as if she’d been counting. She had a patent heel on the bad board I knew to step over on my way to the chalk shelf. She pressed down. The board let out a squeak.

“Your eyes work,” she said. “That’s a good start. Do you know how long you’ve been standing there with your beer? Every time I look, you make as if you’re going to drink it. By all means go ahead, Jory. I’ll give you a second.”

She turned back to the ball. The strike was so quiet I could hear a hanger scrape on the coat rack behind me. Then the ball went down and the rest of the table made itself felt, the rails and the whole long stretch of green between us. I left my beer on the window ledge and went down the side.

“Eight-ball,” she said, and wiped the blue off the end of one finger with the end of another. “One game. If you win, you take whatever you want from me. If I win, I take whatever I want from you.”

I looked up at the lamp. I knew where to put my eyes with the lamp.

Its light made the green cloth the brightest thing on our side of Ternwick. Back when the Bobbin was still the Daker Mill club, you could see that green from the factory gate. You could see it farther down the road now with the mill windows dark. I’d been playing under that lamp since I was eighteen and had learned to look for the bulb where one piece of amber glass was cracked. Once I saw that narrow white line I could settle in for a shot. At thirty-five, I’d been playing elsewhere on Fridays for most of the summer on account of the woman who kept getting to the table before me.

“The shade,” I said. “I’d take the shade if it’s yours.”

“Oh, it’s mine.” She looked past me at the owner, who was stacking coasters by the till. “He’d only just got it rewired when he bet me the shade against my good cue. I put the eight away with him standing right where you are. Day after was my thirty-eighth birthday and he had a card for me with fifty inside. He waited till I’d opened it to tell me what he thought the fifty should buy.” She raised her voice so he could hear the rest. “I told him the shade could stay up. If he wanted it to be his again, he could try and win it off me.”

I’d heard a dozen plans for how to beat her. Nobody had done it yet and she was still taking every wager they brought her. The people who tried to pay her cash when they lost had to carry it home. The owner picked up the stack of coasters and straightened it against the bar. His ears had gone red. This one still had money in his wallet and her lamp over his table.

“I’d leave it there myself,” I said, “and I’d get to tell that story from now on.”

“You can leave it on the damn floor if you beat me.”

She drew herself up and I saw the second rose on the inside of her right arm, where my tailor’s chalk would leave a mark if I took her sleeve in. I liked where that white crewneck sat on her without any help from me. She set the triangle on the felt and looked at me over it. I put my thumb on the seam in my tie where I’d pieced the silk.

“I’ll break,” I said, and held my hand out for the rack.

Riva lifted the triangle by two corners and set it into my hands. “You’ve got one game to figure out where to leave your eyes,” she said. “I want to see what you do with it.”

The break cracked all the way through the clubroom and a solid dropped. On my next stroke I sank the ten in the side and took stripes. When I walked to the end to set up for the thirteen, Riva beat me to the foot rail and leaned over it with her back to me. She kept her palms on the wood and turned one hip out just enough that the tartan shifted over the black lace of her stocking.

“You could tell me to move,” she said.

I came up off the cue and let myself look properly. There were pink bows tattooed high on the backs of her thighs. On the one nearer me the ends of the ribbon curved all the way out of sight. She tipped her face till she could see mine past the sweep of copper hair on her cheek. I wondered if the whole bench could tell how glad I was she’d bothered to ask me to play. By the time I lowered the cue again I could see all the way to the corner. The thirteen ran straight down the rail and dropped.

“So you can look and shoot,” she said. “I was hoping I wouldn’t have to pick which I got to see.”

My next stripe went down and then a hard cut ran along the lip of a side pocket. Somebody on the bench behind the head of the table said I’d missed by half a finger. Riva made two solids down the same rail, the second with a stop shot soft enough to make the first sound loud. She walked up the side to see the angle for the next one.

The chalk shelf ran along the back of the shallow recess where the old club phone had been, a pace beyond the foot rail. Riva set her cue against the wall below its two bare screws and took the chalk I was holding out. She laid it on the shelf and reached for the knot at my throat. She stopped her hand where I could see it.

“Can I see what you’ve been doing to that thing all night?”

I lifted my chin. She closed a finger and thumb on the knot and lifted the wide end out of the way. At the shop I could fit a collar by having a man lift his head exactly this far, and most men would hold still as long as I let them see my hands in the mirror. I had to look right at Riva while she ran a fingernail over the join on the underside of the short end.

“That your stitching?” She rubbed her thumb over it again. “I figured you’d have the sense to buy yourself one good tie.”

“I did at Christmas. It looks good on the gentleman who bought it off me in January.”

“You kept this one. If a man offered twice what it cost, you’d send him to the one in your window.” She let the tie fall and turned her hip. With a finger she tapped the pink bow above the lace. “Put your hand here if you want.”

She took my wrist and set my hand at the edge of the stocking. I ran two fingers along the ribbon. Her leg was warm even this close to the draft from the door. I could see the five past her elbow, sitting right where she’d left it. I wanted her to go back to that easy shot with something from me still in her head. I laid my palm over the bow and held it there till she settled against my hand and looked back at me.

“You put your tongue in the side of your mouth when you’ve got a shot nobody else likes,” I said. “You did it just now. I could see that little bit of cheek move from all the way down here. I’d like both these bows over my shoulders, Riva. I’d like to find out what kind of noise you make when you quit thinking about who’s listening.”

Over by the bar the racks rattled as the empties went in. Riva turned in my hand and the stocking caught hard on the callus on my middle finger and let go. I saw the color come up all the way to the roots of her hair. She had her face close to mine when she answered.

“Both shoulders,” she said. “Listen to you. Better get a good grip when you get down there. I can tell you’re going to want to stop and straighten one of these fucking stockings. Leave it where it lands, Jory. I’d like to see what you can do if I don’t let you tidy up after yourself.”

She picked up her cue and fitted the chalk back in my hand. I had it upside down and got blue in the lines of my palm. On her way back to the shot her right heel struck the bad board and she made a short, surprised sound that was almost the start of a laugh. At the side rail she flattened out her left hand. Then she raised it and put it down again a fraction closer to her body. I could see the line from the five to the corner. I’d had that shot a hundred times under that lamp.

The five caught the near jaw of the pocket and kicked back out. She stayed down, watching it roll back along the rail, and I saw her hand open and close around the cue. By the time she stood up she could see the whole grin I was trying to get under control. Her own started to come back as she leaned her hip on the rail.

“Hell of a thing to do to a woman who’s just told you where to put your hands,” she said. “I could have made that five in the dark. You get over here and take your turn while I remember what else I can do with the lights off.”

Two stripes off the foot rail, and then I missed a long one. She put away the five and two more solids on her next turn. Between shots people started coming away from the bar with fresh drinks and taking their old places on the bench. The smell of orange peel from somebody’s old-fashioned reached the foot rail before she handed the table back. By then she had one solid left, the blue two, and I could see the black in the middle of the cloth.

After putting the fourteen away I went down to the foot to see the angle on my last stripe. The twelve was near a corner, with a cut thin enough to let the cue ball come back into the upper half for the eight. My hand was still on the rail when Riva’s heels came to a stop beside me. She was looking down the same line I was.

“You had one like that the night your phone went off on the bench,” she said. “You were already down on the ball and you stood up and went all the way over to shut it up. Then you came back and put it away. I wanted to know if you could do that twice. It was a pretty shot.”

“I nearly bought you another beer that night,” I said. “I kept trying to work out how many swallows you had left. I thought if I bought it at the right time you might come over to watch.”

I stepped back into the recess to chalk my cue. Riva followed and put her elbow on the shelf. I set the chalk down beside it. She turned the short end of my tie over, found the seam again, and slipped my top button through its hole. After that there was plenty of room for her fingers inside my collar.

I stood my cue up against the wall and put my hand on her hip. She tipped her mouth up and opened the hand at my collar enough to let me come the last inch myself. When she kissed me back, the cotton pulled tight all the way to the nape of my neck. I had one of her fingers on my pulse and a strand of her hair caught between my cheek and hers. The pleats of her skirt opened under my chalky palm as she leaned into it. Then she took her mouth away and kept me by the collar, close enough to speak under my ear.

“I want this tie, Jory. Picture me in it with everything else on the floor. Take a good look. I might even let you hold the wide end in your teeth while I decide where to put the rest of you.” She pulled her hand free and let the collar go. “I still want you to make that fucking shot. Give me some work to do for it.”

The knot sat low enough that the two points of my collar stood apart. Riva had one hip against the wall where the phone used to be. A walk to the bar and back would have given me time to get my shirt straight and my eyes back on the game. But I could already picture the stripe dropping and the cue ball coming back where I needed it. I wanted to put that twelve away while I could still taste her, with her right there to watch.

I picked up my cue and bent over the rail. I needed to catch the outside edge of the stripe and I saw it perfectly. In the last inch of the stroke my grip tightened until I could feel the points of my fingernails and the cue jumped in my hand. The twelve clipped one jaw, then the other, and sat in the mouth of the corner pocket.

I could still see how to make it. There it was a foot from the end of my cue. I could have picked that ball up and dropped it in the pocket with my fingers. Riva walked past me and around to the blue two. It landed in the side with a clap. She’d let the cue ball run wide and by the time it stopped she had a bank on the eight across the middle. If she missed, all I had to do was knock my stripe in and get back for the black.

I set my cue against the chalk shelf. Down at the end of the table I could still talk low and reach her. She got her shoe down on the bad board to give herself room for the shot. Her tartan shifted. I had the sudden, precise thought that the bow I’d put my fingers on would fit in the hollow just below my collarbone.

“I can get down a little lower if you want,” I said. “Give you somewhere to put those bows.”

The corner of her mouth lifted under the sweep of copper. She took her bridge hand clean off the cloth and stood, turning the whole of that grin on me. By the time she’d straightened my hand was halfway to my throat, and I left it hanging in the air.

“Stand right where you are,” she said. “I’ve spent half this game getting you to look at me and I want you to see this one. Side pocket.” She pointed to the one on my right.

She got down again and the rose above her wrist faced the lamp as she opened her bridge. She took two practice strokes. I heard a shoe scuff at the head of the table and a pint glass make a half turn on the bar. I could see where the black had to hit the far cushion and the white line in the amber glass above it. Next Friday I could come in under my own lamp, with this tie on, and see if she was there to win it back. I wanted the ball to run wide of the pocket. I wanted her watching when I made the twelve.

The cue clicked. The eight crossed under the lamp, struck the cushion, and came back at the place she’d pointed to. The white of the number went round and came up again. Somebody on the bench took a breath that carried. The eight went the last foot slower than I’d have dared to send it. It drifted into the mouth and dropped so cleanly I heard it roll the length of the return.

A palm hit the bench hard enough to set a glass jumping. I worked a finger in at my throat and undid the knot in front of everybody. The silk slid free with a little catch where I’d sewn it. I laid it on the rail. Riva took it with her left hand and looped it around her throat, loose over the white shirt. The short end hung with the seam out, where the light caught all my little stitches.
