# Right of Way — provisional direction

First-person comic family story, probably 2,000–2,500 words. The narrator has recently joined the Department of Moving Vehicles as a counter clerk and voted for the new broom-license law. Their grandmother and two great-aunts summon them to tea: the annual birthday flight is approaching, the law's grace period ends before the birthday, and the relatives expect the family's new official to arrange exemptions. The narrator wants to join that flight after missing recent birthdays, but is also ashamed of how readily the relatives expect them to bend the rules.

The witches have considerable skill and different wants. Grandmother objects to being granted permission for something she taught the narrator; one aunt wants a practical explanation of what the test measures and exposes a flaw in its ground-vehicle assumptions; the other is preoccupied with the birthday outing and takes an unexpected interest in having a photograph and card in her own full name. Their knowledge should complicate the narrator's case rather than merely supply funny objections. The narrator has to say plainly why they voted yes: cheap new brooms have filled the sky with riders who did not grow up knowing the same families, gestures, and flight customs. They explicitly refuse to arrange a family exemption. The test asks a rider to demonstrate common knowledge; it does not grant Grandmother her skill. Tea states that claim, and Grandmother rejects it: she knows how to ride, and strangers ought to learn what she learned. The flight must expose what that demand assumes. Any eventual test is administered by another employee; the narrator practices with them rather than examining or signing off their relatives.

Tea becomes an attempt to practice for the new exam, then a short shared flight while unlicensed flying is still lawful. The relatives can fly rings around the narrator. A specific test mismatch emerges through their demonstration, perhaps the illustrated right-turn signal disappearing beneath a rider's cloak when seen from behind. The narrator admits the fault instead of defending every departmental instruction. The practical aunt secures the obstructing sleeve, checks visibility from the following rider's position, and annotates the manual's missing view; the narrator undertakes to deliver that concrete complaint. Away from the familiar roofline, two incompatible but reasonable right-of-way customs bring everyone to a cautious, undramatic halt; there is no crash or rescue. The unfamiliar rider uses a published rule available to any newcomer; Grandmother's customary courtesy may be better in some situations but depends on belonging to the same teaching tradition. Grandmother deliberately yields with a legible shared/manual signal, giving up precedence without receiving a lesson in how to fly. Her skill resolves the encounter. A small, attributable reaction afterward connects it to the test: she wants assurance that unfamiliar riders know what to do, but recognizes that they need comparable assurance about her. A pamphlet offers instruction; the exam establishes demonstrated common knowledge and an enforceable shared obligation. That recognition, rather than defeat in the tea argument, earns her decision to book a test before her birthday.

The relatives choose to take the test, with reservations, rather than being defeated by an argument. The birthday flight remains the story's personal destination; obtaining the cards need not occupy a second full administrative plot. A possible final image is the narrator flying in the family formation again, one elder ostentatiously signaling a familiar turn. The gesture should register as both mockery and an invitation to keep riding with them. Find an ending in their actual conduct, without a final paragraph explaining the civic lesson. The provisional title may change when the prose shows what matters.
