title: The Wheel That Fled

The Frog wanted another day.

On the leaf beside him, the last tadpole in the country was considering its legs. It had four of them. It also had a tail, and the Frog would not relinquish his month while the creature was so plainly unfinished.

“You have another day,” said the Wheel of Ages.

“After that one.”

The Wheel turned a little on the horizon. Clouds passed between its spokes. It was made of living wood, pale where the bark had split, and its roots hung from its great rim into the earth. At its hub, a green eye regarded the leaf.

“Thirty days I gave you. Twenty-nine you have spent. If you want a thirty-first, you must ask the Wren to wait.”

“She is always in a hurry.”

From the knot-hole beneath the green eye, the Wren stuck out her head.

“I heard that.”

“Good,” said the Frog. “Then I needn't say it twice.”

But before evening the tail was gone. The small frog leapt from the leaf, missed the bank, and swam ashore with four furious legs. The Frog watched it take its first three steps. Then he gave a single, enormous croak.

“Keep the last day. I'm finished.”

The Wheel dipped its rim to accept the gift. An animal could give back days; it could not take more than the Wheel allowed. Nor could the Wheel make the Frog spawn, or the Bear wake, or the Spider weave. Within their months the twelve ruled, and even their sovereign had to abide by what they made.

“Wren,” said the Wheel, and the word travelled through its wood into her feet.

She sprang into the air and sang it to the country.

Summer opened. In a thousand hedges the young began shouting for food.

“There,” she said, alighting on the hub. “A whole day early.”

“And already you are late,” said the Wheel. “Listen to them.”

She pecked the bark beneath its eye. It creaked in the particular way that meant it was laughing.

She had known that creak for three hundred summers. In bad weather she slept in the knot-hole, where the wood was always warm. The Wheel knew seven hundred and six of her songs. Once, years ago, it had attempted to sing along. The note had frightened every horse in the country, and she had never let it forget.

That summer she felt a cold place in the back of her sleeping hollow.

She explored it with her beak. The wood flaked away. Beyond the flake was a crack, and beyond the crack something small and green lay curled inside the Wheel's heart.

“Leave it,” said the Wheel.

She withdrew her beak.

“Does it hurt?”

“Not when you leave it.”

So she lay against the cold place and spread her feathers over it, and did not ask again.

The Bear's berries followed her insects. Creatures ate until they were heavy with sleep. The Salmon reddened the shallows; the Spider slung the long grasses with silver. After them came the Wolf's first snow and the Hawk's hard, clear weather. The Wren sang every handover. Each time she returned to her hollow, the cold place was larger.

At last the Stag came down from the mountains, carrying the final month between his antlers.

He was taller than the oldest pine. Snow rested on his back, and there were pieces of other ages caught among his tines: a black shell, a stone arrowhead, a leaf that had never crumbled.

He stopped before the Wheel. The other animals gathered behind him.

“It is time,” he said.

The Wren stood in her doorway.

“For what?”

The Stag lowered his antlers. Between two tines was a place polished smooth as a cradle.

“For the hunt.”

There had been a hunt before this Wheel, he told her. He had carried this sovereign out of the last one's heart when it was no wider than a hazelnut. There would be a hunt after it. The old ring must be brought to rest; the heart must open; the living shoot must come out.

“And this Wheel?” she asked.

The Stag did not look away from her.

“This Wheel will be finished.”

“Can't you take the shoot without hunting?”

“Only the old heart can loosen its hold. If we cut it free, we kill it.”

Behind her feet the wood gave a small, dry knock.

“You might have told her less abruptly,” it said.

“You might have told her.”

The green eye closed. The Stag lifted his head.

“At moonrise.”

The animals went apart to prepare. The Wren stayed where she was, watching the shadows fill the hoofprints.

“Will the new one know me?” she asked.

The Wheel was quiet for so long that she bent and put her ear to the wood.

“No.”

She could hear sap moving. Slowly, with little catches in it.

“Could you tell it?”

“It is growing beside my heart, Wren. It is not my heart.”

She thought of seven hundred and six songs, and a noise that had terrified horses. The new sovereign might like no music at all.

“I don't want you to be finished.”

The Wheel opened its eye.

“Then give me your month.”

Across the winter fields, the Stag was walking toward the rising moon.

“It isn't time for mine.”

“I set the beginnings.”

“And when they hunt you through it?”

“You rule what happens there.”

The Wren looked into her hollow. Its back wall was split from floor to roof. The green shoot stirred behind it, separate and tightly furled.

“How long?”

“Thirty days. And when they are gone, thirty more, if you will stay.”

Its sap faltered beneath her feet.

“If your summer doesn't end, neither need I.”

She went up so high that the moon shone through her wings. Then, with the Stag's month scarcely begun, she sang the Wheel's command.

Summer burst through the snow.

Leaves struck out of branches. Flowers opened beneath the animals' feet. The Stag wheeled at the sudden smell of grass, and the Wheel tore its roots out of the horizon.

It came rolling down into the country, taller than the hills, with the Wren flying before it.

She sang the hedges together over its tracks. She filled the hollows with nesting boughs and drew a roof of leaves above its cracked hub. Under the leaves, sap began to run freely again.

The Wheel made its old laughing sound.

“Listen,” it said.

“I am listening.”

“No. Behind us.”

Far away, bewildered and loud, the young things of summer were calling for their mothers.

The Wren turned toward the voices. Her song thinned. The Wheel's roots struck the earth, and she swung back to open the next hedge.

They travelled west through her green cover. Where the rim passed, the ground dipped under its weight, then sprang up in fern. The Wren darted ahead, closing one gap, opening another. She had never made so much summer. The air tasted sweet enough to eat.

But a horn sounded behind them, and the Wheel's eye turned toward the setting sun.

“The Cat,” it whispered. “Give me her dark.”

“You haven't ended me.”

“I don't mean to.”

The Wren hesitated. One month inside another: summer leaves above winter roots, darkness laid over a day that had not finished. She had never sung such a thing.

The horn sounded again.

She sang the Cat.

Night folded over the Wheel. The thickets vanished. Even the shine of its green eye disappeared.

Then two amber eyes opened in front of it.

“There you are,” said the Cat.

The Wheel stopped so suddenly that its roots lashed forward.

“How did you find us?” asked the Wren.

“In my own dark?”

The Cat got up and stretched. She had been no larger than an ordinary cat when she first spoke; now her back rose against the stars.

“Come quietly. I have found a pleasant place for you to lie down.”

“Serpent,” said the Wheel.

The Wren sang before she could lose her courage.

Thaw rushed under the dark. Ice split with reports like falling trees, and the Wheel plunged through a shallow valley that had become a river. The Cat sprang back from the water.

Something long moved beside the rim.

“Heavy,” observed the Serpent.

The current bent around its body. The river turned with it, carrying the Wheel in a great loop toward the place they had just left.

“You brought the thaw,” said the Serpent. “You did not say where it should go.”

The Wheel drove its roots into the bank. The current caught beneath its loosening outer bark, drawing that ring downstream while the inner wood strained uphill. The Serpent wound the current tighter. Wood groaned against water.

“Only one way out of a tight skin,” it said.

The Wheel heaved.

A whole outer ring peeled from its body. The river took the empty circle and spun it away into the dark. Freed of its own bark, the Wheel climbed the bank.

Pale wood shone all along its flank.

The Wren landed on the hub.

“Are you hurt?”

“I am lighter.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Its eye searched for her.

“What was the song about the white thorn?”

She sang the first three notes.

“Ah,” it said.

It waited for the rest.

She sang that too.

At the next ridge the Fox offered them a road.

He was sitting where three paths met, his tail neatly over his feet. He did not try to look innocent.

“The left-hand one goes away from the Stag.”

“Why tell us?” asked the Wheel.

“I have always wanted to see you run.”

The Wren distrusted him. The Wheel, which had put more miles behind it in an evening than in the previous thousand years, took the left-hand road.

It brought them to the Stag's own hoofprints.

“Fox,” the Wheel commanded. “Give this road another ending.”

The Wren sang in alarm.

The Fox's month opened. Under his feet every road in the country grew a second destination.

“There,” he said. “Now I truly can help.”

He trotted ahead. The Wheel followed a winding track between flowering thorn trees, came round a bend, and reached the riverbank it had climbed. Below, the ring it had shed was still circling in the water.

The Fox laughed from the opposite bank.

The Wheel called the Boar, loudly enough to shake her perch. She sang the name, and the buried things woke: roots, seeds, stones pressed deep into soil.

“A straight road,” it commanded. “Make me a straight road.”

The Boar put his tusks beneath the path and lifted.

The false road came up like a strip of turf. Under it lay the old, direct way to the mountains.

And at the far end of that way stood the Stag.

The Wheel swerved into the trees.

“No more roads,” it said.

The Wren saw a gap where the antlers could pass. She filled it with flowering branches before the Stag reached it. Beneath her feet, the Wheel's sap quickened again.

On the second day, they hid in a valley where her summer lay deepest. Bees came fumbling out of the earth. Two cubs tumbled out of a cleft in the rocks, and the Bear, roused by their feet on her belly, followed them.

“Already?” she asked.

The Wren kept weaving branches over the rim. She had left a wide circle of flowers around the Wheel's heart to warm it. It smelled of fresh-cut wood and honey.

“Has the Frog finished?” asked the Bear. “I heard no rain.”

One cub lunged after a bee. The other followed. The Bear lumbered down the slope behind them.

Above the valley, antlers appeared between the trees.

“Hawk,” said the Wheel.

The Wren looked up.

“The leaves are hiding us.”

“They are following the green. Give me clear ground.”

She sang.

Frost struck the valley. Every leaf became a silver blade. The bees dropped from the air. A clear, savage wind tore the canopy apart, and the Wheel rolled out beneath it, scattering frozen branches.

The Wren shot after the hub.

Then a cub cried below her.

The flowers had drawn them far from their den. The slope between them and the cleft was now a slope of polished ice. One cub slid backward every time it tried to climb. The other had caught a paw between two frozen stems and was pulling with all its strength.

The Wren called to the Wheel.

It did not hear. Its roots were drumming the frozen ground, and it was looking for the next ridge.

She dropped into the valley.

There was still summer there. It was hers, wherever the Wheel had rolled. She called it up beneath the trapped paw, a tiny patch of green among the silver. The stems softened. The cub pulled free.

“This way,” she sang.

She warmed one foothold and then another. The cubs followed her, their bellies low, while the Bear came sliding down toward them. At last all three reached the cleft.

The Wren landed outside it. Her throat hurt. There were splinters of ice among her breast feathers.

The Bear put one cub inside, then the other. She blocked the entrance with her body.

“You sang summer.”

“Yes.”

“And frost.”

The Wren could not get any smaller.

“Yes.”

The Bear looked toward the ridge.

“Where?”

The Wren pointed with her beak.

Then she went up after her sovereign, without singing.

She found it where two rivers met. The frost had covered both. Without her voice, its call did not travel beyond the nearby banks.

“Frog,” it was saying. “Frog. A little water. Enough to float.”

The Frog sat on a stone inside a circle of mist. Wherever the small call had reached him, his month had begun. Rain drummed on the ice.

The Wheel lurched onto the river. Water rose around the lower rim.

“Deeper,” it said.

The Frog croaked.

Every drop leapt out of the river and hung in the air. The Wheel came down hard on the shallow bed.

“That was deep enough for me,” said the Frog.

“Salmon,” whispered the Wheel.

The Salmon came upstream through the hanging rain. She was scarlet along her sides, and she struck the riverbed with her tail. Water fell back into its channel.

The Wheel lifted.

The current took it upstream.

“The other way,” it said.

The Salmon struck again. Against the steep bank, the water climbed.

“You called my running,” she said. “I know where the headwaters are.”

It reached for the bank with its roots. The exposed wood began to split, and the Wren heard a cry she had never heard from it before.

She flew toward the sound. Halfway there, she saw the Bear on the bank ahead, black against the frost. The Wheel saw her too.

“Bear,” it called. “Let the hunters sleep.”

The Bear's month came softly into the valley. The cubs slept in their cleft. Sleep settled over the Wheel's eye, too, and its roots loosened their grip on the bank.

“Lie down,” said the Bear.

The Wheel wrenched its eye open. It turned back into the rising water.

“Spider,” it called. “Bind me. Please.”

The Spider descended onto the rim. She ran a white line across the split, then another, drawing the wood together. The Wheel's cry stopped.

“Thank you,” it said.

She went on weaving. Around a root, over a stone, across the river to a birch. The Wheel tried to move, and the birch bent.

The Spider fastened another line to the opposite bank.

“I must have something to stitch you to.”

The Wheel tore the birch out by its roots. Then the next tree bent, and the next. The Spider had been very quick.

Above them the Hawk circled in the clear air the Wheel had called for her. She cried once.

The Wolf answered from the bank.

He had been following the smell of sap across the snow, losing it at every river, finding it again where a root had struck the ground. Now he ran beneath the Hawk's shadow toward the headwaters.

The Wheel called his month to cover its tracks.

Snow came down. The Wolf opened his mouth and caught the flakes before they could fill the Wheel's furrow. On either side, new snow lay smooth. Ahead of him, one deep track remained open, lengthening toward the mountains.

The Wren watched him follow it.

There would be no losing them now.

At the head of the valley, the Stag lowered his antlers.

The Wheel struck them with a sound that shook the snow from the mountains. For a moment it drove him backward. His hooves cut long trenches. Then the Spider's lines tightened behind it, the Bear came against the lower rim, and the Boar heaved a shelf of earth beneath it.

The great circle stopped.

The river rushed between its spokes. One by one the hunters came up: wet, torn with thorns, rimed with frost. The Cat arrived last, walking on the dark beneath the trees.

The Wren alighted on the hub.

The Wheel's eye turned toward her. Through the cracks in its heart she could see the new shoot. A clear bead of sap trembled on its folded leaf.

“You went away,” it said.

“The cubs were on the ice.”

It looked toward the Bear.

“I did not see them.”

“They came out because I sang.”

Behind her, the Stag shifted his antlers. A spoke creaked.

“Wren,” said the Wheel. “The thickets.”

She looked across the country. Its summoned months lay everywhere in pieces: flowers in snow, ripe berries under a night without stars, rivers flowing in circles, roads that brought you back to your own footsteps. Far away, green leaves rose and fell on the same tree.

“We could stay in them,” it said. “You and I. There is room.”

“And the others?”

The Wheel's eye moved to the animals holding it.

“They needn't stay.”

In the cleft below, the cubs would soon be hungry again. No berries would grow there while the Hawk's frost held; no frost would end while all the months were tangled together. The Salmon's young would find no way to the sea. The Wren could go on singing summer over every wounded thing in the country, one small patch at a time.

She hopped to the edge of her doorway.

“How many days do I have left?”

The Wheel did not answer.

“You set them. Tell me.”

“Twenty-eight.”

She laid her beak against the warm wood.

“I give them back.”

“Don't.”

She had sung her month's departure three hundred times. The first note was very small.

In the valley, her leaves loosened. The branches sheltering the heart drew apart. Warmth left her feet, and the crack beneath her doorway whitened with cold.

The Wheel gave a shudder that ran from the hub to the caught rim. There would be no more thickening wood. Its heart had grown thin over an entire age, and now the last shelter was gone.

The shoot moved inside it.

“If it stays there?” she asked.

The green eye was fixed on the mountains.

“Then it stays.”

She waited.

Below them, the Stag held his head perfectly still.

“Will you sing to it?” asked the Wheel.

“If it wants me.”

A root at the lower rim tightened around a stone. It held through a long catch in the sap beneath her feet, then slowly uncurled.

“It would be a fool if it didn't.”

The bark beside her opened.

She had seen the Wheel split wood, tear roots, and shed a ring into a river. This was different. Each living fibre drew aside from the shoot without breaking it. Sap shone in the opening, amber and gold. At the centre lay a green hoop, scarcely wider than the Wren's head, with one folded leaf at its hub.

She stepped into the heart.

The old wood closed around her feet for an instant. She felt its warmth, the particular grain she had slept against, the small catch in its flowing sap.

Then it let go.

She put her head through the hoop and took its weight across her shoulders. It was awkward, heavier than it looked. A leaf pressed against her beak.

“One more call,” said the Wheel.

She climbed to her doorway.

“I am here.”

It gave her the names in their order.

The Cat, the Serpent, the Fox, the Boar. The Frog, the Wren, the Bear, the Salmon. The Spider, the Wolf, the Hawk, the Stag.

Not beginnings this time. Departures.

She sang them as she flew.

The Cat folded her dark and gave it back. The Serpent straightened its river. The Fox's extra roads closed behind the travellers who had taken them, and the Boar settled the broken earth. The Frog let the last rain fall. The Wren had nothing left to yield.

The Bear released the berries waiting under the frost. The Salmon sent her river toward the sea. The Spider unfastened her farthest lines; the Wolf lifted his muzzle and let the snow blow across his track. The Hawk brought her wind to ground.

At the final name the Stag shook the last snow from his antlers. Then he raised his head to meet the Wren.

She landed between his tines. Carefully, he took the green hoop from her shoulders.

The old Wheel's eye closed.

Its upper rim broke against the sky. Wood fell over the mountainside, long pale arcs crossing the paths of the hunters. The Stag bowed over the shoot until the noise had passed.

Then he laid it in the hollow on the horizon where the old roots had been.

For three mornings the Wren sat beside it. On the fourth, one fine root entered the earth.

The Cat's night came when the little green eye opened. A voice no louder than a seed cracking gave the first command. The Wren carried it as far as the sea.

The young Wheel grew through the thaw and the branching paths, through broken soil and the Frog's rain. By summer its rim was as wide as the Hawk's wings. The Wren built no nest in it. There was no hollow yet.

Sometimes it made a sound almost like a laugh. The first time, she turned so quickly that she hurt her wing against a spoke.

It watched her with its bright, unfamiliar eye.

“Did I call you?”

“No.”

“Then why did you come?”

She had no answer it would understand. After a while she sang the song about the white thorn.

It listened all the way through.

After twenty-nine days of her month, the young Wheel looked over the sunlit country. Everything was green, and the air was full of wings.

“More,” it said.

The Wren stood on the rim. Below her, the first brood of the new age were leaving their nests, one after another, for the wide, difficult air.

“I am finished.”

“But I can give you more.”

“I give this one back.”

For a little while the young sovereign said nothing. Then it turned its eye toward the Bear.

“Thirty days,” it told her.

The Bear rose, already smelling berries.

The Wren sang her summer out. Across the country the fruit began to fall.
