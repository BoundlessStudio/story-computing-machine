# The Golden Mechanism

In the ordinary way I have two jobs at the feast. I carry a brazier, and when somebody asks where I've put my worshippers I laugh. The brazier actually needs carrying. It's earthenware right through to the loop at the top, and if I use the edge of my sleeve to pick it up, the juniper and burnt wool get into the cloth and stay there till the next feast.

This year the brazier is on the stone beside me. I want both hands free. There is a band of gold that passes within reach of the Near Step and I've watched it go by until I can tell it's coming from the crosshatching of light on the spearheads. There isn't a place in Vessale where the whole mechanism will fit in your eye, but I can watch a piece at a time and I do. Last week a shell turned and I saw the underside of its ribs in violet light for most of one breath. There were grooves in there with little stars going round in them. I brought Orsen over later and he said, in the kindest voice imaginable, that he was pleased I'd found something of mine to do.

Here he comes now, lifting the end of the Step. There is solid rock under us where the Step begins at the city rim. A long way farther out I'm looking sideways at a white chimney down on Wren Street, and for a second I watch the stone in front of my feet climb the height of the chimney. I always look at that chimney. All around me the trays of clay weights clatter and the sellers lean back against the rail without looking down. They know how much of the stone's weight Orsen has taken away and how high he'll let the end of it rise.

Then he puts his palm flat to the stone and brings my knees down with the rest. I feel it through the fronts of my shins first. The hollows in the stone fit almost everybody. When I was very young I used to let my legs go loose just to see how gently he'd put me down. Now I smell juniper again because somebody has kicked the brazier over and the coals are rolling against the side of my boot.

Across the drop a gold sphere swings into sight through the openings in the others. One of the cages of light goes straight through a spar as wide as a house. Inside the cage I can see two cut ends of the spar and a curl of something white inside the gold. The gold is whole again where the cage has gone by. Then the first choir lifts its banners and I lose the spar behind them. They thank the mechanism for holding up the sky. The other choir comes in right on time to pray for the hour when the mechanism rolls up the sky and all its inhabitants go into the keeping of the Last Light. Each choir gets half the rail. The spearheads in it are the ones they used to put in each other.

The singing ends. Orsen lifts his palm and tells us to rise. There is a little interval here every year when the rest of us stand around waiting to hear whether a god has anything to say. I can feel my tongue sticking to the roof of my mouth. Orsen holds out a hand to me for the brazier.

“I mean to set the mechanism going,” I say. “Nobody's ever dared try. I'd like to see what it does.”

People laugh. Orsen has introduced me on the Step before as the goddess of any unoccupied seat. That's as near as I've got to a domain, and I suppose they think I'm trying out a better line. I still have a coal at my boot. I step over it and up to the end of the spearheads, where the turning gold comes close enough to warm the knuckles of my open hand. Nobody there is laughing.

Orsen comes after me at the same pace he uses when he is going to take somebody's offering from them. There is a little drop in the corner of his mouth where he has to work to keep a smile. He is good at making people feel they have all the time in the feast to find their courage and give him a clay weight.

“What have you got in mind to do to it, Edri? I take it you're going to ask us for something.”

“I'll have to do it with my hand. There's a band round the last sphere that comes this far out. I thought I'd give it a push. I'd take company, if anybody wants to see.”

The goddess of the Last Light comes through the line of Orsen's singers. She trails her silver veil across their mouths as she passes and they take a step back to let her through. Her priests have had to carry stools out for her on feast days since the year Orsen offered her one of his. Now she takes her place almost touching his shoulder.

“Tell her how far you'll let her go,” she says.

“Come back from the end, Edri.” Orsen's smile is gone. “If you put your hand on the gold, I will kill you.”

The gods have their own places in the front of both choirs. I see the Harvesters come away from the rail together. One of them puts a hand on the Last Light's shoulder and says that Orsen can speak for them. They understand one another at once. On all their faces I can see them waiting for me to come back and stand where it's safe. From closer in among them you might have thought somebody had made a place for me after all.

I'm looking at Orsen's hand. He has turned it sideways, the way he does when somebody has given him something he can't take just yet. I can remember thinking when I was small that I could get all my fingers between two of his. A line of light goes over the soft part at the base of his thumb. It keeps right on going down the side of the Step, where the light from the feast lamps stops.

“Are you sure about the danger?” I ask him. “Is there anything you've found out about what the mechanism does?”

“You need to think about where you are. Go down Wren Street at supper and you'll have to turn sideways to pass all the people on their front steps. There's mealtime at a thousand houses under what you want to move. If I told them that one of us meant to put a hand on that gold without knowing the first thing about what it might do, they would tell me to go up here and stop her. They'd be entitled to tell me worse than that.”

“I've looked down Wren Street. I can see people setting the tables when they leave the shutters open. What I'm asking is whether you know a single thing about what it's for. All these years people have been singing about it and I thought somebody might have asked before me.”

There is a sweep of light across the faces behind him and the hot feeling in my hand goes away. I can only see the back of the band now as it goes into the other spheres. It will take its time in there. I can put my hand on the top of the rail without its shadow falling on the gold.

Orsen comes up beside me. “The morning I first took the weight out of this stone, the rest of them stayed at the city end. By then we'd fought so many wars for the closest place to worship that some of us couldn't name the first one. Now we were going to have to learn to stand together on the same stone. I told them I'd go out to the end and see how close I'd brought us. I put the heel of my hand on that last spear there. There was room to get a finger out as far as the band. I remember the gold had dents in it. You can't see the little ones from where we stand to sing.”

He puts his hand beside mine on the rail. The flare on the passing shells has gone soft and I can see his fingers again.

“I thought I'd touch it,” he says. “I thought I'd come in and tell them what I'd found. Then I got to thinking that even if the whole Step came away, I could take the weight out of every stone and every person down there. I could bring them up hip to hip in midair. I tried to think what I would do if the sky itself began to change. And then I shut my eyes. I'd gone up to the end on both feet and I came back on my knees, with my palms flat to the stone the whole way. There are people in the choir who think I went down like that because I heard the gold speak. I let them think it. All I heard was my own breath.”

He lets go of the rail. Somebody way back in the crowd calls out that she's lost a shoe. The Last Light looks around sharply and in the quiet that follows I can hear a shutter knocking down on Wren Street.

“I wish I'd been here when you went out,” I say. “I'd have liked to look over your shoulder before you shut your eyes. You could stand with me now. You could have both your hands on the stone if you like and I'd try it. You could see what it does.”

He looks at me for long enough that I close both hands on the rail. I'm having a sudden wish that I had left the brazier upright. There's a good thing I could do right away which would happen to take me back to the rest of them. Orsen gives me time to think it over. I can still smell the burnt juniper and I think he would let me go as far as the smell takes me.

“Turn around now,” he says, with his voice as low as it will go and still carry to the city end. “You'll walk back beside me. Nobody will put a hand on you, nobody will ever be able to say we drove you off this stone, and tomorrow you can come out here again if all you want is the view. I'm offering my arm, Edri. I don't want you dead.”

He holds it out. I am very glad he says that last part and for a little while I can feel almost nothing but the wish to give him my hand. It has brought home what a difference he can make to me. We could go back down the Step, and after we bring up the brazier I could ask him if he ever wants to see the dents again. I try to imagine how much of the way we can go with our arms linked before our shoulders get in each other's way.

Over his sleeve, light spreads out in the fine strokes it makes on the spears just before the band comes back. There must have been thousands of times when somebody stood where I am and saw the light go like that. I could leave it to the next person who sees it. For all the time I've known to look, there has always been the thought that the next time the band comes out, I'd like to be close enough to try. It's hard to say what I could do with that thought tomorrow, out here to look at it again, after laying my arm on Orsen's and walking away.

I step around the spearhead. I have had the width of the gap wrong from behind the rail. I have to put my toes right out to the edge to get my hand where the band will pass, and from there I can see Orsen's dents, each with its grain running down to the bottom. I can feel something that might be the heat of this particular bit of gold. Orsen is still holding one arm out when I put my palm on the band and push.

I feel the band move in the direction I push it. It goes the breadth of my hand. Then the gold is going so fast the dents are gone and the heat goes right out of it. I take my hand away. I have a little crescent of dirt on the heel of my palm and in the first instant I think of washing it off and coming out to do it again.

The sphere inside the band goes end over end. It brings the next shell round with it, and for the length of the turn I can see right into the center, where the smallest sphere hangs with its point of light showing at both poles. The pure gold of it is hard to look at. All the cages go the same way at once. They fold through the spars and out across the sky in panes that spread the faster the farther out they get. I watch one thin edge travel the length of Wren Street and go out over the neighborhoods past it. Before my hand has cooled there is a line of light around the far rim of Vessale, and all above that line is black water.

The sea stands upright. I can see the waves go up it. At the height where I'd expect the evening star, a wave goes over and rips into white along a length of water bigger than any street I know. It leaves strips of foam. Below them, lower down in the water itself, there are lights spaced out in pairs and moving steadily across the slope of the next wave. When I see the first pair all I can think is that there must be another behind it, and when I see the next I start counting.

Rain hits the tip of the Step and hits me across the mouth. It's cold enough to hurt my teeth and it's salt. I put a foot back and get hold of the first spearhead I can reach before a second sheet comes through. Down on Wren Street the shutters are opening all together. Smoke still rises out of the white chimney. Drops of water that get as far as the smoke curve out of it and streak back upward toward the sea.

Orsen has both his hands out in front of him. I can see water on the inside of his elbows. He curls his fingers and pulls sharply down. Knees come down hard all the way back to the city. The shock goes through the Step and my arm takes my weight on the spearhead. There is a terrible noise from one of the clay sellers, a noise made with her teeth together. The rain keeps wheeling up. Some of it runs right past Orsen's knuckles and he turns his hand to look at the empty palm.

The weight goes out of my legs. For a breath I hear what I thought would be the sound of the sea. It's a tray of votive weights going over and spilling down the stone. People get up with a hand on the rail or on their neighbor. The goddess of the Last Light lifts the silver off her mouth. She has to pull at it. There's rain running along the edge of her veil in one long strand.

“Keep us in the dark to come,” she sings. There is a place after that where she waits for the answer. I can see the mouths of the singers she has come out from among. A few of them are wide open as if they might be singing already, and their eyes go right on following the white water. She draws the veil down as far as it will go. “Give them their knees, Orsen. Give them the weight they know.”

He lowers one open hand toward the stone. I see a man put his hand up to cover his wife's head as they go down together and then take it off again so she can see. The man and his wife are kneeling in the places worn for them. People are kneeling all the way down the Step, and every face I can see is turned up into the rain. A man whose knee slips on a spilled clay weight gets caught by the gods along the rail. They stay there with their arms round the people on both sides. I can see straight back to the paving at the city end.

I come away from the spearheads toward that paving. In the place where I knelt for the blessing I turn to see whether the first pair of lights is still there. It has come up nearer the surface. There's a dark shape below it and I think it might be carrying them. I put the hand with the little mark on it out toward Orsen and beckon.

“Come and look,” I say. “There are lights moving under the water. That one's coming up.”

He turns toward me. For a second I think he's going to give me his arm again. He fixes his eyes on the stone under my feet. I'd have to get down there myself to catch his eye. He puts his hand flat on the stone and the weight comes back into my shins. It has the familiar direction and for half a breath I think he is doing what he just did for the choir. Then my knee hits the back of the hollow and something in the joint goes with a snap I can hear through the bone.

I have both hands down. I can go the whole way to the city if he will stop for long enough to let me up. The paving is clean and dry at the far end, and there's a split in one of the flags that I can see from where I am. This is what I think of when I try to tell him. I'll step over the split. I'll keep right on walking.

“Let me up, Orsen. I've let go. I'll walk in.”

His hand stays flat. There's time to take several breaths in which nothing hurts any more than it did. I've got most of myself on my hands and there's one good foot. I want to tell him how much my knee hurts. I want to see the shape carrying the lights. If I slide one palm a bit farther back I can tip my face up without moving my knee. I get as far as seeing the foam overhead and the stretch of water where the lights ought to be. Orsen shuts his eyes. His fingers spread against the stone.

The whole weight comes down. I get my elbow up to shield my face and the middle of my arm makes a turn that an arm can't make. There is a white bit sticking through the cloth of my sleeve. I try to get the sleeve over it. The stone catches the side of my mouth. I can feel each of my front teeth move and then I have two of them loose against my tongue. The air goes out in one rough rush and I hear it come back to me in a wet patter. A bubble of blood sticks to my chin and bursts against the stone.

I am trying to take a breath under the weight. If I could get a finger under me I could leave a little room, just the width of a finger. The wrist I put against the stone has slipped in something warm. My lungs are trying to make room for themselves and all they do is push back against the stone. Orsen is still pressing. I know it because I feel the lowest of my ribs give and then the ones above it come apart one after another. The ends go in. My mouth fills again around both loose teeth and I have to try to breathe through it.

There is a strip of the sea I can see without lifting my head. One of the pairs of lights comes all the way across it, with an extra light moving a little way behind. I can't see what's carrying them. There is blood between my hand and the edge of the stone. It swells out, clings, and lifts in long strings into the strip of light. My right hand is lying open with the little dirty mark showing. I want to pull my fingers in. I get one of them to scrape the stone and I am surprised by how much I need to do the same with the others.

Orsen's weight breaks what's left of Edri's chest against the kneeling stone. Her heart stops while the sea is still moving above the Step.
