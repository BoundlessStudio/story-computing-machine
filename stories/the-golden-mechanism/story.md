# The Golden Mechanism

In the ordinary way I have two jobs at the Feast of the Turning. I carry a brazier, and when somebody asks where I've put my worshippers I laugh. The brazier actually needs carrying. Orsen wants it burning when the sun goes down. It's earthenware through to the loop at the top, and if I use the edge of my sleeve to pick it up, the juniper and burnt wool get into the cloth and stay there till the next feast.

This year the brazier is on the stone beside me. I want both hands free. I'm out on the Near Step, a shelf of rock that sticks out over the roofs of Vessale. At the far end a band of gold passes within reach. I've watched it come round often enough to know what the light does just before it comes. The light crosses itself in fine lines on the iron spearheads. There isn't a place in Vessale where the whole mechanism will fit in your eye, but I can watch a piece at a time and I do. Last week a shell turned and I saw the underside of its ribs in violet light for most of one breath. There were grooves in there with little stars going round in them. I brought Orsen over later and he said, in the kindest voice, that he was pleased I'd found something of mine to do.

Here he comes now, past the market stalls at the head of the Step, where the stone is still part of the hill. The streets go down in rings into the hollow in the middle of the gods' capital, and the gold turns over the innermost roofs. There's a white chimney on Wren Street just over the rail. Orsen takes the weight out of the far end of the Step with a lift of both hands. The rail rises against the chimney until just the pot is showing. I always look at that chimney. All around me the trays of clay weights clatter and the sellers lean back against the rail without looking down.

He keeps the Step level while a woman sets her clay weight on the offering stone. Then he puts his palm flat at his feet and gives us just enough extra weight to bend our legs for the blessing. I feel it through the fronts of my shins first. The hollows in the stone fit almost everybody. When I was very young I used to let my legs go loose just to see how gently he'd put me down. Tonight I keep one leg stiff so long that the heel of my boot catches the brazier on the way down. It tips. The smell of juniper comes up again with the coals rolling against my foot.

Across the drop a gold sphere swings into sight through the openings in the others. A spar reaches from it to the next shell, thick enough to fill Wren Street from one doorstep to the one across from it. One of the cages of light goes through the spar. Inside the cage the spar has two cut ends and a curl of something white shows in the gold. The gold is whole again where the cage has gone by. Then the first choir lifts its banners and I lose the spar behind them. They thank the mechanism for holding up the sky. The other choir comes in right on time to pray for the hour when the mechanism rolls up the sky and all its inhabitants go into the keeping of the Last Light. Each choir gets half the rail. The spearheads in it are the ones they used to put in each other.

The singing ends. Orsen lifts his palm and tells us to rise. There is an interval here every year when the rest of us stand around waiting to hear whether a god has anything to say. I can feel my tongue sticking to the roof of my mouth. Orsen holds out a hand for the brazier, with his eyes still on the gold.

“I mean to set the mechanism going,” I say. “I saw stars turning under the gold last week. We've come this close for as long as anybody remembers, and nobody has been willing to reach out. I'd like to find out what happens when somebody does.”

People laugh. Orsen has introduced me on the Step before as the goddess of any unoccupied seat. That's as near as I've got to a domain, and I suppose they think I'm trying out a better line. I still have a coal at my boot. I step over it and up to the end of the spearheads, where the turning gold comes close enough to warm the knuckles of my open hand. The laughter falls away so quietly I hear one of the clay sellers set a tray down.

Orsen comes after me at the same pace he uses when he is going to take somebody's offering from them. One corner of his mouth keeps dropping and he keeps lifting it. He is good at making people feel they have all the time in the feast to find their courage and give him a clay weight.

“What have you got in mind to do to it, Edri? I take it you're going to ask us for something.”

“I'll have to do it with my hand. There's a band round the last sphere that comes this far out. I thought I'd give it a push. I'd take company, if anybody wants to see.”

The goddess of the Last Light leaves her own choir waiting on the other side of the Step. She trails her silver veil across the mouths of Orsen's singers and they take a step back to let her pass. She goes to stand almost touching Orsen's shoulder.

“Orsen. Turn her back. Keep her off the gold.”

“Come back from the end, Edri.” He lets the corner of his mouth drop. “If you put your hand on the gold, I will kill you.”

The gods have their own places at the head of both choirs. They come out from under the banners, one or two at a time. The god with a sheaf at the clasp of his cloak puts one hand on the Last Light's shoulder. “You speak for the rest of us, Orsen,” he says. The other gods move apart till there's a clear way to the gap by Orsen's elbow. I'd have liked to see them do that on any other day.

I'm looking at Orsen's hand. He has turned it sideways, the way he does when somebody has given him something he can't take just yet. I can remember thinking when I was small that I could get all my fingers between two of his. A line of light goes over the soft part at the base of his thumb. It goes on down the side of the Step, where the light from the feast lamps stops.

“You used to give me two fingers to hold when I could hardly see over this rail,” I say. “I thought you'd give me your hand now. What do you know about the gold that would make you kill me for touching it?”

“You need to think about where you are. Go down Wren Street at supper and you'll have to turn sideways to pass all the people on their front steps. There's mealtime at a thousand houses under what you want to move. If I told them that one of us meant to put a hand on that gold without knowing the first thing about what it might do, they would tell me to go up here and stop her. They'd be entitled to tell me worse than that.”

“I've looked down Wren Street. I can see people setting the tables when they leave the shutters open. Would you go down there and tell them you know this thing holds up their sky?”

There is a sweep of light across the faces behind him and the hot feeling in my hand goes away. I can only see the back of the band now as it goes into the other spheres. It will take its time in there.

Orsen comes up beside me. “The morning I first took the weight out of this stone, the rest of them stayed at the city end. By then we'd fought so many wars for the closest place to worship that some of us couldn't name the first one. Now we were going to have to learn to stand together on the same stone. I told them I'd go out to the end and see how close I'd brought us. I put the heel of my hand on that last spear there. There was room to get a finger out as far as the band. I remember the gold had dents in it. You can't see the little ones from where we stand to sing.”

He puts his hand beside mine on the rail. The flare on the passing shells has gone soft and I can see his fingers again.

“I thought I'd touch it,” he says. “I thought I'd come in and tell them what I'd found. Then I got to thinking that even if the whole Step came away, I could take the weight out of every stone and every person down there. I could bring them up hip to hip in midair. I tried to think what I would do if the sky itself began to change. In my head I had them all waiting for me to set them down, and I couldn't find a place for the first foot to go. And then I shut my eyes. I'd gone up to the end on both feet and I came back on my knees, with my palms flat to the stone the whole way. There are people in the choir who think I went down like that because I heard the gold speak. I let them think it. All I heard was my own breath.”

He lets go of the rail. Somebody way back in the crowd calls out that she's lost a shoe. The Last Light looks around sharply and in the quiet that follows I can hear a shutter knocking down on Wren Street.

“I wish I'd been here when you went out,” I say. “I'd have liked to look over your shoulder before you shut your eyes. You could stand with me now. You could have both your hands on the stone if you like and I'd try it. You could see what it does.”

He looks at me for long enough that I close both hands on the rail. I can smell the burnt juniper all the way out here. I could go back to the brazier, set it upright, ask for some coals. I think he'd let me do that much without another word.

“Turn around now,” he says, with his voice as low as it will go and still carry to the city end. “You'll walk back beside me. Nobody will put a hand on you, nobody will ever be able to say we drove you off this stone, and tomorrow you can come out here again if all you want is the view. I'm offering my arm, Edri. I don't want you dead.”

He holds it out. I want his hand and I've opened mine on the rail to take it. We could go back down the Step, set the brazier upright, and then I could ask him if he ever wants to see the dents again. I try to imagine how much of the way we can go with our arms linked before our shoulders get in each other's way.

Over his sleeve, light spreads out in the fine strokes it makes on the spears just before the band comes back. There must have been thousands of times when somebody stood where I am and saw the light go like that. I could leave it to the next person who sees it. Tomorrow I'd come for the view and have both hands on the rail when the band went by. I'd remember how this hand was open and empty, close enough to put on the gold, and how I'd put it in Orsen's instead.

I step around the spearhead. I have had the width of the gap wrong from behind the rail. I have to put my toes at the very edge to get my hand where the band will pass. There are Orsen's dents, each with its grain running down to the bottom. I feel the heat in the middle of my palm. Orsen is still holding one arm out when I put my palm on the band and push.

The band moves the breadth of my hand. Then the gold is going so fast the dents are gone and the heat goes out of it. I take my hand away. I have a little crescent of dirt on the heel of my palm and in the first instant I think of washing it off and coming out to do it again.

The sphere inside the band goes end over end. It brings the next shell round with it, and for the length of the turn I can look into the center, where the smallest sphere hangs with a point of light at either pole. I look for the stars in their grooves and get a glimpse of a whole row in the turning ribs. All the cages go the same way at once. They fold through the spars and out across the sky in panes with rose and green at their edges and violet where the corners cross. I watch one thin edge travel the length of Wren Street and go out over the neighborhoods past it. Before my hand has cooled there is a line of light around the far rim of Vessale, and all above that line is black water.

The sea stands upright. The waves go up it. I tilt my head almost to my shoulder and the small waves begin to look as if they could break on a beach. The roofs down on Wren Street turn over in the corner of my eye and I have to straighten up again. At the height where I'd expect the evening star, a wave goes over and rips into white along a length of water bigger than any street I know. I wait to hear it break. Down here I can hear the loose end of a banner ticking against the iron of the rail.

The foam stays high up where the wave left it. Farther down there are two lights I take for whitecaps till the next wave takes them out of sight and I see them come out on the other side, as steady as before. There's a space of clear water ahead of them and I'd give a great deal to be able to put my face where I could see into it. All I can think is that there must be another pair behind them, and when I see it I start counting.

A sheet of rain comes across the gap and strikes the tip of the Step. It hits me across the mouth. It's cold enough to hurt my teeth and it's salt. I put a foot back and get hold of the first spearhead I can reach. The spray is already curving up when a second sheet comes through. Down on Wren Street the shutters are opening all together. Smoke still rises out of the white chimney. Drops of water that get as far as the smoke curve out of it and streak back upward toward the sea.

Orsen has both his hands out in front of him, with water on the inside of his elbows. He curls his fingers and pulls sharply down. Knees come down hard all the way back to the city. The shock goes through the Step and my arm takes my weight on the spearhead. There is a terrible noise from one of the clay sellers, a noise made with her teeth together. The rain keeps wheeling up. Some of it runs right past Orsen's knuckles and he turns his hand to look at the empty palm.

Orsen leaves that palm open and uncurls the fingers of his other hand. The pull on my legs ends. A tray of votive weights goes over and spills down the stone. People get up with a hand on the rail or on their neighbor. The goddess of the Last Light lifts the silver off her mouth. She has to pull at it. There's rain running along the edge of her veil in one long strand.

“Keep us in the dark to come,” she sings. There is a place after that where she waits for the answer. A few of the singers' mouths are wide open as if they might be singing already, and their eyes follow the white water. I've always thought she sounded quite glad to be going into the dark. She draws the veil down as far as it will go. “Give them their knees, Orsen. Give them the weight they know.”

He lowers one open hand toward the stone. I wait for the tug at my shins. I put a foot around the last spearhead and it goes just where I mean to put it. I see a man put his hand up to cover his wife's head as they go down together and then take it off again so she can see. The man and his wife are kneeling in the places worn for them. People are kneeling all the way down the Step, with their faces turned up into the rain. A man slips on a clay weight and the god with the sheaf catches him. Other gods along the rail reach out to steady the people on both sides. I can see straight back to the paving at the city end.

I come away from the spearheads toward that paving. In the place where I knelt for the blessing I turn to see whether the first pair of lights is still there. It has come up nearer the surface. There's a dark shape below it and I think it might be carrying them. I beckon to Orsen with the hand that touched the gold. He looks at the dirt on my palm. He takes my hand, with his thumb beside the little mark, and turns us both toward the paving. I go a step with him.

“Look up, Orsen. There are lights moving under the water. You can see them from here.”

He stops with one foot ahead of mine. I feel his hand move in mine as he leans back to look. His eyes go up with the foam. I wait for him to look down into the water where the lights are. He lets go so fast my fingers go right on trying to hold his. I'm still holding my hand out when he gets his eyes down to the stone and puts his palm flat on it. The weight comes back into my shins. My legs go loose the way they did for him when I was little, and I'm waiting for the stone to meet them gently when my knee hits the back of the hollow and something in the joint goes with a snap I can hear through the bone.

The woman whose husband covered her head gets straight to her feet at the sound. The god with the sheaf looks from my knee to the water. He leans back till the spearhead in his fist begins to bend. When he straightens he has his whole back to the sea. I have both hands down. I can go the whole way to the city if Orsen lifts the weight for long enough to let me up. The paving is clean and dry at the far end, and from where I am there's a split in one of the flags. I'll step over the split. I'll keep right on walking.

“Let me up, Orsen. I'll walk in beside you.”

His hand stays flat. There's time to take several breaths in which nothing hurts any more than it did. I've got most of myself on my hands and there's one good foot. I want to tell him how much my knee hurts. I want to see the shape carrying the lights. If I slide one palm a bit farther back I can tip my face up without moving my knee. I get as far as seeing the foam overhead and the stretch of water where the lights ought to be.

The goddess of the Last Light lifts one hand toward the water. The feast lamps go out all along the Step, as far back as the market stalls. The white of the water goes on moving across her face. She puts her other hand up too. She keeps closing her fingers on the light and opening them again, faster than I can count.

“Last Light,” Orsen says. He looks across me to the god with the sheaf. “Come down here. Both of you. Help me kill her.”

Orsen shuts his eyes. His fingers spread against the stone.

He puts more weight on me and I get my elbow up to shield my face, and the stone catches the side of my mouth even as my arm makes a turn that an arm can't make, and there is a white bit sticking through the cloth of my sleeve. I try to get the sleeve over it. The air goes out in one rough rush, and by the time I hear it come back in a wet patter I've got two teeth loose against my tongue. A bubble of blood sticks to my chin and bursts against the stone.

The god with the sheaf comes over with the broken spearhead in his hand. Even on his knees beside me he keeps his back to the water. The iron knocks twice against the stone before he drives it into my side. I try to get my hand around his. Something warm comes down the side of my body and my wrist slips in it.

The Last Light comes down beside my face and lays the silver of her veil across my mouth. I think she's going to wipe it. The silver pulls at the air left in my chest. I open my mouth wider and the breath goes on going out, even when I try to draw it in. The silver goes dark with my blood where she holds it.

If I could get a finger under me I could leave a little room, just the width of a finger. My lungs are trying to make room for themselves and all they do is push back against the stone. Orsen is still pressing. I know it because I feel the lowest of my ribs give and then the ones above it come apart one after another. The ends go in. My mouth fills again around both loose teeth and I have to try to breathe through it.

There is a strip of the sea I can see without lifting my head. One of the pairs of lights comes all the way across it, with an extra light moving a little way behind. I can't see what's carrying them. There is blood between my hand and the edge of the stone. It swells out, clings, and lifts in long strings into the strip of light. My right hand is lying open with the little dirty mark showing. I want to pull my fingers in. I get one of them to scrape the stone and I am surprised by how much I need to do the same with the others.

Edri's heart stops under Orsen's weight as the light behind the pair reaches clear water.
