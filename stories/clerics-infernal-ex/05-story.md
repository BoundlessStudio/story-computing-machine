# Hello, Lover

Emet Sarn had one hand on the stolen brass when he recognized the file mark.

Three shallow strokes crossed the lower hinge leaf, the middle one wandering crooked because Lacrixa had laughed into his shoulder while he worked. He had once known that scratch by touch in the dark. Now the leaf was bolted into an iron scaffold in a ruined roadside sanctuary, between three black loops and jars of salt-glass cloudy with someone else's stored charge.

“Familiar workmanship?” Udren asked from beneath the cracked arch. Her shield was already angled toward the doorway, the road settlement downhill, and every person who had ignored their warning to stay indoors.

Emet removed his thumb from the scratches. “Familiar principles.”

Not a lie, if one filed truth as badly as brass.

Bosk, the party's bard, crouched beside him, keeping his embroidered sleeves clear of the iron. “Can you quiet it?”

Emet pressed the stamped medallion at his throat, not for help from the inert silver but for the shelter-count cut into it. Breath in. Heat gathered behind his sternum. On the exhale, he fed a thread of his own ward-light through the stolen leaf to feel for the trap's edge.

The scaffold took everything he offered.

Heat drew an upright line in the air. The line opened hand-wide, showing red tile, white glare, and one amber eye.

Emet stopped breathing.

The opening widened. Lacrixa stepped through in a scorched traveling coat, a living demon from the far Cauterized Reach, one horn wrapped in a strip of copper cloth and her boot soles smoking against the sanctuary floor. She looked straight past Bosk and smiled at Emet with all her teeth.

“Hello lover!”

Everyone looked at Bosk.

It was, Emet had to admit, a reasoned response. On three previous jobs, Bosk's stage acquaintances had arrived through a window, a funeral procession, and a magistrate's evidence cupboard. All three had greeted him as if entering to applause.

Bosk put a hand to his chest. For once, he looked genuinely unprepared.

“Actually, my ex is referring to me.” Emet said it because collapse procedure began with correcting dangerous assumptions.

“I had a line,” Bosk said faintly. “It has left the stage.”

No one laughed. Iron slammed against iron behind Lacrixa. The first black loop cinched around the scaffold, brass reddened, and the sanctuary inhaled hard enough to lift ash from the floor.

Udren raised her shield. “Threat.”

Lacrixa's smile vanished. She caught the frame with both hands, the skin along her knuckles glowing the color of banked coals. “Three borrower loops. Freight pattern.” She looked at Emet. “What did you open?”

The pressure pulled at their clothes, toward the red seam.

Emet reached for the severing verse engraved inside his medallion. “I can close it.”

“If you want to cut a street in half.”

Lacrixa did not grab him. She stopped her hand a finger's width from his wrist and pointed instead. A shape wavered in the opening behind her: an apron, an upraised arm, a person backing away through kiln glare.

“Worker on the far side,” Udren said.

Emet lowered his hand.

“Good,” Lacrixa said. “The cleric can still be taught by evidence.”

“The frame is holding your passage open.”

“Our passage.” She tapped the brass with one claw. “This leaf has your keying. Mine signaled a forced expansion, so I supplied heat and breath to inspect it. You supplied ward-work here. The loops stored both contributions and bent them into this.”

The iron and glass had not invited her, chosen her, or made power of their own. They had redirected what two living people deliberately put in. That distinction would have comforted Emet if the redirect were not trying to tear two buildings together.

Ordinarily, their courtesy hinge admitted one traveler or stayed open eighty heartbeats, with fresh assent and living power at both ends. Mounting the stolen leaf in a new frame had not opened a road; the fence's work remained inert until its keyed bearer supplied a fresh living opening. By recognizing the leaf and powering the rig, Emet had knowingly met that condition, and Lacrixa had answered from her side.

Bosk edged toward the wall, out of the suction's straight path. “For the record, I prefer entrances with a rehearsed exit.”

Lacrixa did not glance at him.

His mouth shut. “What beat do you need?”

“Not yet.” She leaned close to the scaffold. Ember-light moved from her palm into the metal; her own, spent by choice. The black loops answered by brightening at three separate joints. “North jamb. Under the floor. Behind the throat. Remove them in that order, bleed each charge into stone, and then we withdraw together after I'm home.”

Emet watched the salt-glass turn milk-white. “Or sever before the next pulse and accept damage to an empty endpoint.”

“It isn't empty.”

“We evacuate it.”

“In the time you took to say that, the scaffold pulled another six feet of my kiln court into alignment.” She pointed to the stolen leaf. “Why is your private half in a freight rig?”

Udren's gaze moved to him. “Compromised?”

“The leaf was stolen with other brasswork. We caught the fence. We followed the components here.”

All true. None sufficient.

“And you recognized it,” Lacrixa said.

Air reversed.

The first pulse struck with a sound below hearing. Emet's teeth rang. The opening flared wider, revealing a crowded court of red tile and copper awnings. Two workers dragged a third away from a rack of cooling vessels. A tool cart rolled toward them without anyone touching it. Here, a crack raced through the sanctuary arch, and hot wind poured downslope through the broken wall.

“Answer,” Udren said.

Emet looked at the arch, the road, the widening seam—every exposure except Lacrixa. “After the first loop.”

Lacrixa's tail struck the floor once, a hard refusal.

“We lose both sites if we debate provenance now,” he said. Formality settled over his voice like ceremonial cloth. “Give me the discharge order, and I will answer after the first loop is safe.”

“One loop,” Lacrixa said. “Then no more careful fractions.”

Udren wedged her shield under the sagging arch. “Deadline accepted. Move.”

* * *

The north-jamb loop hung twelve feet up, its iron collar sunk behind rotten stone. Udren planted her boots and took the arch's weight on her shield while Emet climbed the scaffold. Below, Bosk unhooked the small frame drum from his pack.

“Eight beats,” Lacrixa said. “We feed on one and five. Pull the pin on seven. Stone takes it on eight.”

“A sparse arrangement,” Bosk said. He struck the drum once, listened to the sanctuary answer, and adjusted his grip. “Again.”

The second beat came clean and low.

Emet set his palm against one side of the brass. Lacrixa took the other. They had done this hundreds of times in two years: her heat rising as his breath left, his ward steadying when hers flared. His body found the old timing before his pride could object.

One. He fed the smallest possible thread.

Five. Lacrixa answered. The brass warmed between them.

Seven. Udren drove out the inert pin with the boss of her shield.

Eight. Emet turned their joined charge down through the scaffold's foot. Foundation stone flashed white. The loop dropped, empty and ordinary, and shattered a floor tile.

The sudden relief made his knees loosen. Lacrixa's glow had faded around her knuckles.

“Question,” she said.

“Second loop first.”

She stared until he added, “Please.”

Udren did not look up from the arch. “He bought one loop. Debt's due.”

The floor bucked beneath them. The second collar tightened under a fractured paving slab, dragging its iron staples deeper. Bosk moved his drumbeat faster at Lacrixa's signal while Udren lowered the arch onto a surviving pillar and crossed the room. Emet knelt, hooked numb fingers beneath the slab, and lifted with her.

“Cut?” Udren asked.

“No,” Emet said.

“Not the leaf. The staple.”

“Left one only.” Lacrixa was already bracing the opposite edge. “Right carries the throat.”

Udren struck left. The staple split. Emet and Lacrixa breathed on one and five, and the old coordination returned in humiliating details: the slight turn of her wrist that meant hold; the way his thumb shifted before he sent power; the shared half-breath they had used when a hearthframe caught in wet weather.

He remembered her bare feet on the table in their rented room, polishing hinge pins while he wrote shelter reports. They had been twenty-eight and thirty-one when they began, adults with separate work and keys and the belief that asking clearly would keep them from hurting each other. For a while, it had.

The second loop came free.

The second pulse caught its stored charge before it could discharge. The salt-glass flashed.

The kiln court disappeared. In its place stood their old room: narrow bed, blue washbasin, rain stippling the window. On the table lay two unfinished cups and the little file Lacrixa had jostled when she laughed. The image was no memory Emet had meant to offer. But the charged metal had been shaped to widen whatever route-impressions its living bearers supplied, and his trained intent had reached for safety as it had once looked.

Lacrixa went very still.

Then she saw the repaired corner on his leaf, the bright solder line made after the last winter they shared.

“You kept it keyed.”

A roof slab broke loose.

Udren shoved Bosk clear and caught the worst of it on her shield. Stone struck stone. Dust erased the room, the court, and half the sanctuary. Emet fell into the side alcove with Lacrixa. A tilted pillar blocked them from the others, though Bosk's drum continued through the dust—slower now, a pulse they could follow.

Lacrixa's eyes found him in the red gloom. “You kept it keyed.”

Emet put his shoulder against the pillar. It did not move. “Yes.”

“The letter said your oath required a clean severance.”

His silence had once passed for dignity at council tables. Here it was simply an answer.

“I won't trust final withdrawal to another silence,” she said. No endearment now, no ceremony. “Tell me what I am trusting.”

* * *

Ash blew through the alcove. Each breath scraped. Emet's right hand shook against the frame, and Lacrixa's skin had cooled from ember-red to dull brown at the fingertips. Beyond the pillar, Udren cursed once, concisely, while stone ground over metal.

“I was offered the permanent circuit,” Emet said. “Six shelters. Twenty years.”

“I remember your chronology.”

Of course she did. He had used dates in the letter the way a mason used rubble: to fill the space where a proper stone should go.

“The House of Common Shelter did not forbid us,” he said.

Lacrixa stopped examining the frame.

The third pulse gathered in the salt-glass, a cloudy brightness that grew with every breath they lost.

“They did not ask me to choose the circuit over you,” Emet continued. “You asked whether I wanted a shared home. I was afraid to leave my work. I was afraid to ask you to leave yours. And I was more afraid to say that I didn't know which fear mattered more.”

“So you invented a prohibition.”

“Yes.” The word hurt less than the long explanations had. That was no credit to him. “I made myself obedient instead of cowardly. I let you believe the institution I served had judged you unfit. Then I kept the leaf because calling uncertainty a safety precaution sounded better than admitting I wanted a door I had refused to walk through.”

“And today?”

“I recognized the file marks before I powered the rig. I did not tell them.”

Her face changed by almost nothing. It had always been dangerous to mistake her stillness for absence.

“I spent three years preparing arguments,” she said. “Rules of residence. Kiln-court protections. Precedents from mixed households in the Reach. I had an answer for every objection your House never made.”

The charge brightened behind her. Bosk's beat came through the pillar: steady, asking nothing.

“I am sorry,” Emet said. “For the lie then. For the concealment now. Both were choices. Neither protected anyone.”

He did not add that he had loved her. That fact was visible in the danger around them and useless as a claim.

Lacrixa crouched beside the second loop and worked a claw under its split edge. “I kept my leaf locked away because breaking one half without neutral de-keying can leave a dangerous echo. That was the technical reason.” She pried. Iron screamed. “The other reason was that I wanted you to have to answer me someday. Pride can wear procedure too.”

The admission did not divide fault equally. It was not offered to do so.

“Do you forgive me?” Emet did not ask.

Instead he said, “Will you trust me for one synchronized withdrawal?”

“I will trust the count. I will trust what you do on it.” Her eyes held his. “We are finishing the road, Emet. We are not beginning again.”

“Understood.”

The third pulse broke.

He and Lacrixa threw their remaining power down together. The second loop released its charge into the foundation, and the tilted pillar jumped aside. Udren hauled Emet through the gap by his collar. Bosk caught Lacrixa's forearm, failed to hide his surprise at how cold her skin had become, and adjusted his grip without comment.

No one had time for relief. Behind them, the third loop glowed white.

* * *

“Full account,” Udren said.

Emet forced air into his lungs. “The leaf is mine. I kept it keyed. The last loop sits behind the throat. Once it's removed, Lacrixa crosses, and we both revoke future access on Bosk's count.”

Udren compressed everything she wanted to say into three questions. “Other traps?”

“None I know.”

“Can she close her side?”

“Yes.”

“Will you release on count?”

The honest answer arrived before the respectable one. “I am afraid I won't.”

“Then I stand with you.” She turned to Lacrixa. “Shield before or behind?”

Lacrixa pointed into the widening seam. “Behind me until I clear. The court roof is shedding tile.”

Udren moved without hesitation, placing her shield between Lacrixa and the falling stone of her own home.

Bosk tightened the drum strap around his burned fingers. “Eighty heartbeats until this tries freight width?”

“At most,” Lacrixa said.

“One tempo. On sixty, Udren takes the pin. On seventy, Lacrixa crosses. On seventy-eight, the leaves break. On eighty, nobody is between.” He looked from face to face. “That is the arrangement. No solos.”

Emet nodded.

“Good,” Bosk said, and began.

* * *

At twelve, the opening widened to the height of the arch.

At twenty, kiln wind struck sanctuary air. Heat blasted one way while tools, roof tiles, and loose stones skidded the other. Emet held the near edge with both palms, feeding ward-light through muscles that no longer wanted to close. Lacrixa held the far edge. Her coat snapped in the crossing wind; her horns were dark against the furnace glare.

At thirty-four, Udren advanced behind her shield. The third loop lay behind the throat, visible whenever the opening shuddered: black iron, a hot clasp, one pin driven through the scaffold's rear brace.

At forty, the sanctuary roof began to peel.

Bosk's beat did not change.

Emet's ward-light had once illuminated whole refuge halls. Now it barely silvered his fingers. The scaffold held no strength of its own, but its salt-glass reservoirs returned his earlier contribution in distorted surges, each one demanding that he spend more to keep the person-wide center clear.

At fifty-two, Lacrixa missed her handhold.

Emet shifted as she recovered, matching the old half-breath. Intimacy lived in the body longer than permission. It told him what she would do. It did not entitle him to decide for her.

“Clasp won't lift,” Udren called.

Lacrixa pressed both cooling hands around the hot iron. A last ember-glow moved out of her skin by choice. The clasp softened, then sagged around the pin.

“Foundation,” she ordered.

Emet took one hand from the edge. The throat narrowed, throwing a strip of kiln light across Udren's shield. He drove what remained of his ward down the rear brace. The foundation stone whitened, ready to receive the stored charge.

Sixty.

Udren hit the pin.

It moved the width of a thumbnail and jammed.

She hit it again. The shield slipped in the suction. A tile from the kiln court struck its rim. Lacrixa caught Udren's shoulder before she pitched into the throat, and the three of them lost the count beneath a roar of moving air.

The drum tore free of Bosk's hands. It sailed through the breach and vanished among the copper awnings.

“Sixty-four!” Bosk shouted. His voice cracked, but his tempo did not. “Sixty-five! Sixty-six!”

Udren planted one boot against the scaffold and drove her shield boss into the pin.

At sixty-nine, it came free.

The final loop dropped. White fire ran harmlessly down the prepared line into dead stone, but the throat convulsed around the sudden absence of its brace.

“Cross!” Emet shouted.

Lacrixa backed through first, facing them. Udren followed just far enough to keep the shield over her while remaining on the sanctuary side. At seventy-three, falling tile struck the top edge. At seventy-five, Lacrixa stumbled.

Emet poured more of himself into the leaf.

“Seventy-six!” Bosk called.

Lacrixa rolled, rose on the red tile, and turned. One boot was visible. The other vanished behind the warping edge.

Emet held.

“Seventy-seven!”

The opening drank his breath. Red spread through the brass again. The empty loop sockets began to shine as his continued supply recharged the route.

“Emet.” Lacrixa's voice crossed the narrowing distance. “Both feet clear.”

He could not see them.

He held.

Udren abandoned the pin. She caught his forearm in both hands and planted his palm flat against the stone beside the leaf. She did not tear him away. She made him feel something that was not the open road: grit, weight, the shaking of his own muscles.

“Off,” she said.

Across the breach, Lacrixa reached her hearthframe. “You do not keep me safe by keeping access.”

No ornament. No technical argument. A command he had spent three years earning.

“Seventy-eight!” Bosk shouted.

Emet withdrew.

The loss of power struck like a missed stair. He sagged against Udren, but his right hand found the stolen leaf. He wrenched it from the loosened bolts, set its keyed edge against the foundation, and bent with what little strength remained.

Across the narrowing sight-window, Lacrixa pulled her leaf from its hearthframe. Embercraft lit one fingertip. She melted the keyed edge into a bright, useless curve and let the ruined brass fall onto the red tile beside the frame.

Emet's brass folded under his weight.

“No future invitation,” Lacrixa said.

“None,” he answered.

The opening shrank to hand-width. Behind her, people lifted the fallen kiln worker. Here, the sanctuary settled by inches instead of collapsing. Both places remained themselves.

For one beat, Lacrixa's face filled the small square of light.

Emet did not ask if they would meet again.

“Goodbye, Lacrixa.”

She studied him as if accuracy still mattered, because it did. “Goodbye, Emet.”

The window closed.

* * *

At first light, the road settlement still stood. Smoke marked one scorched field below the sanctuary, but no freight breach had opened above it, and no one had been pulled from the kiln court.

Before dawn, they spent an hour shoring the wounded arch with salvaged beams. Emet could lift only with his left hand. Bosk worked without turning the labor into a performance, and Udren accepted no assurance about a brace that she could not test with her own weight. Each time the wind stirred ash through the scaffold, Emet looked at the warped loops. The iron stayed cold.

Bosk wrapped his burned fingertips in strips torn from the lining of his least embroidered sleeve. Udren bound Emet's strained wrist with considerably less tenderness and considerably better tension.

Emet gave them the fuller account: the two adult years he and Lacrixa had chosen together, the false letter, the retained leaf, the moment he recognized it and said nothing. He kept his sentences plain. Neither oath nor magic received the blame.

Udren tied off the bandage. “We discuss field trust after we deliver the evidence.”

“Yes.”

Bosk glanced at the dead scaffold. There was room for a joke in his expression. He let it pass. “Can you walk?”

Emet tested his legs. “Slowly.”

“A tempo I can manage.”

They left Emet's folded leaf, the three warped loops, the spent salt-glass, and the dead iron scaffold for the local authorities. Udren propped the sanctuary's ordinary door open with a fallen stone. Cool morning moved through it, and Emet followed his companions outside.
