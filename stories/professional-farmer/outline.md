# A Fair Price for Peas

*Provisional direction; the prose may find a better route.*

Seventeen-year-old Romy Talas announces over dinner that she wants to farm professionally. Her family already grows, builds, and makes things for pleasure; her mother Lise is a demanding potter. They happily offer Romy more garden space, then discover she means selling a regular harvest and making an income. Five generations of freedom from earning one's necessities make this sound both unnecessary and faintly alarming. Essentials remain freely supplied by excellent robots; credits still circulate for handmade things, without anyone needing them to survive.

Romy wants her work taken outside the circle of indulgent family praise. She approaches Beni Oru, a neighbor who hosts Thursday suppers, about buying peas through a season: a particular grower and crop he can get to know and influence, without a claim that human peas beat machine peas. He will plan around an agreed regular supply; he will not redesign suppers around whenever she happens to have surplus. A civic adviser can approve selling an existing handmade bowl but cannot say whether paying a seventeen-year-old in advance for promised future work revives something the law abolished. Romy can make a spot sale, but that will not make the continuing arrangement she wants.

The first offer goes badly. Romy has counted the hours and prized the size of her harvest; Beni wants tender peas and rejects the starchy ones her relatives would kindly have eaten. She can go back to giving things away, or learn to offer a smaller, surer quantity and accept correction. The tender harvest window for an agreed Thursday supper falls during the overnight family sail she and Lise have anticipated. Romy can still give notice, refund any advance, and put the first delivery back a week. She chooses this particular beginning over this particular trip, and regrets missing her mother rather than proving her devotion to work. Lise helps with a little practical preparation, then sails as planned and feels Romy's empty place. Her question is reasonable: why make something you love into something you must do? Neither hobbyists nor machines are proved unserious; Romy must discover which obligation she actually wants, and leave room for help, time off, and stopping.

Possible ending: Romy's revised offer states an honest hand-grown/hand-picked claim, a realistic quantity, payment for accepted produce, notice for a skipped week, and returned advances for a missed delivery. The adviser can provisionally allow that concrete continuing sale; the occupational heading "professional farmer" still needs a wider answer. Common provision remains unconditional. The emotional close belongs to mother and daughter: Romy chooses a finished bowl from Lise's real stock, asks the normal price, and Lise manages not to turn the sale into a present. Affection and freely shared food continue around the exchange. The peas need not be perfect or magically better.

Close third person through Romy, warm comedy with room for embarrassment and affection; General target. The supplied green settlement image guides the lived setting. No collapse, evil robots, necessity shortage, or water-infrastructure plot.
