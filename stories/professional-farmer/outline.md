# A Fair Price for Peas

*Provisional direction; the prose may find a better route.*

Seventeen-year-old Romy Talas announces over dinner that she wants to farm professionally. Her family already grows, builds, and makes things for pleasure; her mother Lise is a demanding potter. They happily offer Romy more garden space, then discover she means selling a regular harvest and making an income. Five generations of freedom from earning one's necessities make this sound both unnecessary and faintly alarming. Essentials remain freely supplied by excellent robots; credits still circulate for handmade things, without anyone needing them to survive.

Romy wants her work taken outside the circle of indulgent family praise. She approaches Beni Oru, a neighbor who hosts Thursday suppers, about buying peas through a season: a particular grower and crop he can get to know and influence, without a claim that human peas beat machine peas. A civic adviser can approve selling an existing handmade bowl but cannot say whether paying a seventeen-year-old in advance for promised future work revives something the law abolished. Romy begins with one actual sale while the question is referred onward.

The first offer goes badly. Romy has counted the hours and prized the size of her harvest; Beni wants tender peas and rejects the starchy ones her relatives would kindly have eaten. She can go back to giving things away, or change the crop and terms. Meanwhile her commitment costs her some family pleasure she had assumed she could keep. Lise's objection becomes personal: why make something you love into something you must do? Neither hobbyists nor machines are proved unserious; Romy must discover which obligation she actually wants, and leave room for help and for stopping.

Possible ending: a modest repeat order with a real price, honest rejection terms, and a narrow provisional legal ruling that preserves Romy's unconditional access to the common provision. The emotional close belongs to mother and daughter, not the government: Lise lets Romy's money buy a bowl she would otherwise have given her, taking the exchange seriously without withdrawing the love. Romy uses the bowl at their table; the peas need not be perfect or magically better.

Close third person through Romy, warm comedy with room for embarrassment and affection; General target. The supplied green settlement image guides the lived setting. No collapse, evil robots, necessity shortage, or water-infrastructure plot.
