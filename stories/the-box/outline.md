# The Box — provisional outline

## Promised experience

“Every person has the free opportunity” makes an extraordinary offer personally imaginable. The two explicit outcomes are equally likely and begin when the door is closed. The distinctive pleasure I read in that is having time to want the magic and fear the fire at once, then finding out what a choice made on those terms does to a life. “Every person” suggests a world changed by the offer, even if the story follows a few people at the threshold. Dread and wonder are available in the premise. The prompt leaves tone, scale of action, relationships, and the entrant’s decision and fate open.

## Takes considered

- **The occupied city.** A general boasts that her army could absorb every new magic user, then finds that anybody on either side can call The Box. The central scene is a public wager where two adversaries try it on equal terms and a newly made mage acts beyond the reach of the army.
- **The woman who opens the door.** A successful former entrant meets a stranger who asks her to witness his attempt because he thinks a survivor can recognize a sure thing. The central scene is her trying to make an honest account of her own luck while discovering she wants to see what he'll do with magic.
- **The aerialists.** Two sisters who have made an art out of catching each other want the power to fly. At the open door one asks for the same call that signals her to leave the bar. I choose this take because flight gives the reader something specific to hunger for and the sisters’ real experience of falling gives their encounter with chance a physical grammar.

## Provisional direction

Istrid catches her sister Rissa in a flying trapeze act at Bellwether Yard. Both have seen a woman from the bakehouse call The Box for free and survive. They saw her fly home through an upstairs window. Rissa intends to try and wants Istrid there. Istrid means to talk her out of it, partly to keep their act together and partly because she has wanted real flight for herself and has walked away from the door before. At the threshold Rissa asks for the familiar release call. Istrid declines and stays to witness her choice. Rissa shuts the door and dies in the fire. Weeks later Istrid gets the pleasure of a good catch back with a new partner. She can keep flying on the rig with that partner, or risk her life for the chance to stay up beyond the reach of the bars. She faces the same odds, chooses to close the door and lives, then flies clear of the rig and the roofs beyond it.

The Box comes at the asking to anyone wherever they are, free of charge and without a keeper’s leave. The person inside can walk out until they shut the door. Each closure has the same independent 50/50 odds. The Box focuses living magic and a survivor can work that magic in their own body. Leave its makers and the source of the odds offstage.

## Craft plan

**Teller.** Try an immediate first person with Istrid’s practical ear and a close third past that follows her bodily calculations before admitting the desires behind them. Give her exact attention to what the air can do to another person, and let that competence fray when she can see only a closed door.

**Cast.** Istrid wants one more season together and wants to know what’s above the far roofs. She thinks Rissa takes the catch for granted and that she can get her sister to stay if she gives her another chance to do the trick right. She speaks directly, particularly about what went wrong and when, and under pressure may explain every decision up to the one she’s afraid to name. Rissa wants to go above the roofs under her own power. She thinks Istrid calls her brave when she means careless, and hopes she can get the call without asking for approval outright. She speaks in generous, diverting runs of talk, makes a joke by giving it ludicrously exact detail, and can land a plain request after a long approach. Both are adults. There are no supplied or established characters.

**Conversation owed.** At the door the sisters have to talk about why Rissa wants to risk her life for flight, what Istrid has wanted from The Box herself, and what it would mean for Istrid to give the call. Rissa can tell Istrid about the moment the bakehouse woman heard someone at the upstairs window and changed direction in midair. Istrid watched the same flight. The instant the woman stopped swinging back to earth is the one she wanted for herself.

**Place.** Bellwether Yard takes this over Shot Tower Square and Kiln End Music Hall. The square would make the attempt principally a public spectacle. The hall would cap the sightline that tempts both sisters. Bellwether is an old sheep market turned open-air circus ground, where the rig is wedged between rows of tall workshops. Early autumn, late afternoon, chiefly at the end of practice as the bread shop shutters. Hemp scrapes a wrist, a pulley ticks as the weight shifts, anise from the bakery reaches the board, chalk hangs on black catch cuffs, and the copper roof beyond the yard goes pink before the yard darkens. A loose shutter slaps in a crosswind that can steal a catcher's cue. Beyond the rig a person could pass over an entire street, if she could get up to the height of that roof. A separate swinging catch bar and the net make the sisters’ practiced danger tangible. [The Hebrew Flying Trapeze](https://www.fly.org.il/en/about)

## Scene briefs

1. **The practice, about a sixth.** At the top of her swing Istrid steals one sight of the seams in the copper roof. She has to turn back to make the catch. A slapping shutter steals part of the release call, but years of practice bring the two pairs of wrists together. Istrid wants Rissa to repeat it on the right count. Rissa wants her down on the ground and tells her she means to call The Box here. She has already chosen where and when. Istrid has kept her own visits to the door to herself.
2. **The threshold, a little under half and the long scene.** Same dusk, under the idle bars, with workers in the shops across the yard. The Box appears with its door open. Rissa asks for the rig’s release call. Istrid hears a request to help her die, then has to hear what the bakehouse woman's flight meant to Rissa and admit how close she herself came to trying. Istrid wants Rissa to step back and sees her actually do it and consider leaving. Rissa wants a witness through either outcome. Istrid will be that witness and tells her why the call has to stay on the rig. Rissa steps in again and closes the door herself. Stay with Istrid hearing the fire and calling Rissa's name as the door opens. Someone from the bakehouse shuts the banging shutter and crosses with a cloth to cover Rissa. Let Istrid remain beside her sister through this first response. The death is unequivocal and non-graphic.
3. **The second closure, a little over a third.** Several weeks on, same yard. Istrid opens the scene by catching a new partner cleanly for the first time. She enjoys it and makes plans to work the return together tomorrow. The partner packs up, invites her to supper, and leaves with the expectation that Istrid might follow. Istrid wants to. She calls The Box when she is alone and stands inside with the door open. She can still walk across the yard and be at that supper. What she has wanted since before Rissa ever asked her to wait is still real too. Take time before Istrid reaches for the door. The silence inside the closed Box leaves her listening for the sound she now knows. Her odds are unchanged. When she can bear her own weight in the air, a few physical beats establish that she’s working magic. She passes the point where she should swing back and keeps climbing.

Summarize the days between the two attempts. Leave ceremonial funerals, the history of The Box, and any new career offstage.

**Ending.** In the first scene Istrid steals a glance at the copper roof at the top of her swing and has to turn back to make the catch. Rissa speaks about that loss of view when she tells why she wants magic. In the last scene Istrid can see the shadow of the empty rig getting small on Bellwether Yard, and streets beyond the roofs as long as she wants to look. She carries the absence of the person she most wants to turn and tell.
