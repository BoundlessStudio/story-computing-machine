# The Copper Roof

I see the copper roof when I swing back with my arms empty. It’s my only look at it on a catch, so I’ve learned it in pieces. There are three seams near the chimney that swell up off the roof in a hard frost. Once a man in a red shirt put down his hammer and slid the length of it on his backside. Today a bird is balancing on the spoon they bent into a rain cap. I’d like to stay up here until it flies away. I’d like to see where it goes.

The smell of anise comes up from the bakehouse. The catch bar works against the raw place behind my right knee. I turn my head. Across Bellwether Yard my sister Rissa is off the board and headed toward me. The loose shutter bangs just as I call “Hep!” She lets go right where she ought to, with the end of the word only just coming back at me off the bakehouse wall. Then she puts her hands out. The slap when we take hold runs straight up into my teeth.

She grins at me from the other end of our arms. There’s flour on her shoulder. She swore she’d wait to eat till we got home, and she was all of half an hour keeping her word. I can see the print where she brushed at it with her fingers. On the way up the far side she gets very light, and there it is, the little squeeze of her hands to say she wants the return. We’ve done it together since we could lie side by side in the net and hardly dent it. When I send her back she finds the bar with both hands at once. The weight comes off my arms and I can feel exactly where her thumbs were.

We drop into the net before the light goes. Rissa gets both knees up and rolls into the middle where I’ll have to climb over her to get out.

“You’re lying on my foot,” I say.

“It’s nice to feel the net come up and meet you. I don’t think I’ve ever finished one of these and got straight up. Have you?”

“I’ve got one more in me if you want to try it again on the call.”

“What were you looking at before you caught me?”

I pull my foot clear and lie down beside her. She must have done the whole first swing waiting to see if I’d turn my head back in time. It’s getting cold through the meshes and she comes a little closer.

“A bird,” I say. “It was on the copper roof.”

“There was a man up there last week eating an apple. What a place to have your lunch. With your feet in the gutter so you don’t go sliding off and kill yourself.”

Above us, her bar and mine are still moving. Soon the shutter will be louder than anything else in the yard. I start to get up. She lays her hand across my knee.

“Stay a minute, Istrid. I’m going to ask for The Box.”

There’s a flap at the side of the net where we get out. I go to it and climb down. By the time Rissa follows I’ve got the water bottle in my hand and nowhere to put it. I set it down by the foot of the ladder. Across the yard the joiner from the last shop in the row is piling up the boards he means to bring in for the night.

“You mean here in the yard,” I say. “Today.”

“I thought I’d ask when I could still get your attention. You start walking home and by the time we get to our door I’ve got you talking about supper, and I’ll listen to you all the way through supper, which these days can be an hour if you have a catch you want to get at from both ends.”

“I’d be very pleased to walk home now.”

Rissa puts her hand out at her side, crooked a little so she might be reaching for a handle. I’ve done that. Anybody can call it, even out on a road with no house for miles, and it won’t cost a penny. Rissa could have waited for me to go home. Before she takes her next breath The Box is standing beside her, its door open.

Someone at the bakehouse runs a tray in hard on its runners. I cross under the net to get a better look. It could be one of the joiner’s boxes. His own sits out by his step with a lid that’s lifted at the corners since spring. This door’s edges are as straight as they were when I first saw The Box standing in the rain years ago. Rissa has her knuckles against the dark edge where she can pull it to from the inside.

“Give me the call when I get in,” she says, her fingers slipping round the edge.

“Rissa. Take your hand off the door.”

She takes her hand away. “When you call for me to let go of the bar, I can feel it all the way down my arms. I might stand in there till we both get hungry and go away if you leave me to it.”

“Come over to the bakehouse with me,” I say. “We can leave it standing here while we eat.”

One side of her mouth goes up. In the air if I give the call at the right moment Rissa comes to me. If she shuts herself in The Box, she has one chance in two of coming out able to do magic and one in two of burning to death. I’d never call for her to let go on odds like those. I take one step into the cold that’s coming through between the shops, thinking if I go all the way to the gate she might call me back. I get as far as the ladder and stay there.

“Do you remember when the woman from the bakehouse first went up?” Rissa says. “You were over there rolling up one side of the net with me. I put the whole thing down in the dirt and then you did too. The woman had come straight out of The Box with her apron on. She pulled it in against herself as she went up and I thought, she’s scared she’ll go up out of it like a flag out of its ties. Then her girl called from the window above the shop, and the woman turned and went straight in at the window and a minute later I could see her passing the girl a hairbrush. I’ve been thinking about it ever since. If you called me from down here when I was up over the roof, I could turn and come down right where you were.”

“Come where I am now, please.”

She does. There’s a thumb’s width of flour left on her shoulder and I’m silly enough to brush at it for her. The shutter bangs and we both look at it. I wish we had something familiar to do, an eyelet to sew back on, anything I could take up where she can come and be beside me.

“I remember her stopping,” I say. “Before the girl called. I thought I could have a whole minute up at that height. Maybe more. I’d want to find out how long I could stand it before I had to look down. I brought The Box up on our landing last week. It came so close to the top stair that I could have got into it with one foot still on the step. I stood there holding on to the rail to see how far I could lean in. Then I heard you putting a cup in the sink down below, and I took the last step up and went past The Box to my room.”

“I could have come up and run straight into you,” she says. “You might just as well have called down to me.”

“I didn’t want you to be there till I’d decided. Then I could have done it and come and told you, the way you tell somebody you’ve been somewhere and you wish they’d come with you. Yesterday I waited till you’d gone home and I stood in it here. I took two steps backward and kept on walking. I was afraid I’d turn right round and get in again.”

Rissa looks at the ground on either side of The Box. “I’d put two bowls out yesterday and sat so long waiting that I started to think I’d call it while you were gone. I had this lovely picture of flying up to tap at your window once you’d gone upstairs to bed and seeing your face when you got to the glass. Then there was a bang out back and I was out of my chair. I thought you’d shut yourself in The Box. I got to the latch and tried to think whose door I could knock on to ask somebody to come with me and look for you. Then I heard the man next door swearing about the coal shovel he’d dropped and I went back and put your bowl on the stove.”

I remember picking up that bowl with my sleeves pulled over my hands because she’d kept it so hot. Rissa looks straight at me now. “Afterwards I kept putting you in my place,” she says. “Going round all the houses asking if they’d seen me.”

“When I call from the bar I can see your hands. I can see where they’re going to be. I’m so sure of what’ll happen to them I call before they’re ready. It’s taken me years to get that word into your feet. I can’t give you the call here. I’ll stay here with you and you can tell me what you like when you come out.”

Rissa gets in and stands with her arms at her sides. Her left foot is a little ahead of her right, the way she stands with the bar in hand at the edge of the board. I try to think of something I can look at in the yard that will give me the time till she reaches for the door. The man with the boards has made a stack twice as high as his doorstep. He bends to get an arm under the whole load. By the time he’s drawn himself up Rissa has stepped down again. She stands next to me and we watch him take the boards in. He gets the longest ones through by going sideways. I turn a little toward the gate so she can walk next to me. Rissa looks back at The Box.

“I wish I’d been on the stairs with you that night,” she says. “One step up and I could have put my head on your belly just as I used to when you got big enough to go up the ladder before me. I’d have stayed till you told me to move.”

She takes my hand. Her fingers are cold on the inside where they’ve been in the wind. I can tell she’s holding lightly so she can let it go.

“Come wait at the door,” she says. “Right up where I can see you till it shuts.”

She leads me back by the hand. I stop a little short of the jamb and she brings me the rest of the way with a small pull. She steps inside with her hand still in mine and turns to see my face. Then she lets go and pulls the door shut.

The door trembles with a low noise coming from inside. I stand to the side of the door so that when it opens she’ll have all the room there is. A line of fire shows under it. The noise rises and my hands come halfway up to my ears, and I stop them there. When I catch Rissa on a bad day, she takes a little dragging breath that I can hear all the way through the swing before she says so much as my name. I listen for that much of a sound from her. I keep my feet where they are so I won’t have so much as the scrape of a boot to hear through.

The line goes out and I hear the shutter bang across the yard. I’m holding my own wrists very hard. I have to uncross my arms to reach for her as the door opens. Heat runs up under my cuffs. Rissa is on the floor with her right hand open, palm up. It’s the hand she squeezes my wrist with when she wants me to throw her back. I put my wrist alongside it where she could take hold.

“Rissa,” I say, in the voice I’d use if I came up behind her in the yard when she thought she had it to herself. I want her to hear who it is and have that one bit of good news before she tries to get to her feet. I’ve gone down on one knee beside her. I keep my own hand open while I wait.

When I look out the woman from the bakehouse has caught hold of her shutter. She pulls it in and walks across the yard with a cloth folded four ways. The wind tucks the skirt of her apron between her knees on the way. She kneels down at the doorway to unfold the cloth. I reach for an end and get it wrong and pull where she means to let it open, and we’re both working against each other.

“Give me this corner,” the baker says, putting her hand above mine. I let her have it and she gets the cloth open. “Istrid. Shall I stay here with you?”

I give her some room beside me and watch her cover the hand I’ve been waiting for. My sister is dead. I look across the yard anyway to see if the sound has brought Rissa running out of one of the shops. In a little while the joiner comes back out to get the two boards he left by the step. He puts his hands up on the jambs of his door and looks across at us.

* * *

Five weeks later, in the last of the light, I make a catch that pleases me so much I laugh just as the weight comes on my arms. The woman I’ve been working with since I came back to the yard two weeks ago has the broadest wrists of anybody I’ve ever taken hold of. This time I have all that weight just where I want it. The knots in the net are hard from the cold when we get down, and I lie still a while with my hands open. I can feel the ropes rise under my back as she gets out.

“We could start the return tomorrow,” I tell her.

“You’ll tell me when you mean to send me. You can say my name if you can’t think of anything else, but I’d like a word of some kind before I see the bar coming at me.”

I get as far as my knees to look at her. “I’ll say your name twice. Once when you’re in my hands and once when I let you go.”

“Come up the hill and eat with us. My sister made so many dumplings she’s put a plate out on the step to cool.”

The invitation puts me in mind of the bench by her stove, where they give you a cushion to tuck between your shoulders and the hot pipe along the wall. I can sit against it till I’m warm. I want to tell her sister how near we were to getting that catch wrong last week and how well we did it today.

“I’ll be a little while,” I say. “Go on ahead.”

She puts on her great loose coat and goes. I get my own coat from the hook by the ladder. There are still the outlines of houses on the hill beyond the gate and there’s the first pale light in the upper bakehouse window. I want tomorrow at the rig with the two of us finding out if I can put her where she needs to go to get hold of her own bar, and her wanting me to try again if I get it wrong. I thought one good catch together would put everything I want right here in the yard for a while. I lay in the net just now with the notion that I could go straight up out of it.

I walk out from under the net and ask for The Box. It stands in front of me with its door open as if I’ve walked up to it. When I step in I have a view of the yard gate and a little of the hill. The two flat boards on the gate lean away from each other above the latch and the light on the hill shows between them. A boy runs past the gate. I hear his steps slow as he takes the hill. He’s going the way I would go.

I put my hand on the inside of the door. There’s a hollow there the right size for four fingers, the sort of place the joiner would rub back with sandpaper till it felt good to reach for in the dark. Rissa had put her fingers here. The last thing I felt of her hand was the cold inside her fingers as she took them away from mine.

If I let my hand fall, I can still get to supper in time to eat with everybody. In my head they’ve got the lid off the pot already and the steam smells of onion. My shoulders loosen in advance of the heat of the stove. I’d walk into the yard again tomorrow and there would come that one look at the copper roof on the way back to the catch.

In another five weeks a man might be up there shovelling off the snow. He’d stop every once in a while to look out and decide if it was going to snow again, and I’d want to be up there long enough to see what he was looking at. I’d want to stay as long as I could stand it and then go higher. I still want supper up the hill. If the door opens and I can walk out, I want to go and tell them everything I’ve seen.

It takes hardly any strength to pull it shut. I keep my hand on it for a little while in the dark, and then I know I’ve left it on there so I can feel the heat come. I take two breaths and I can hear that the second one is longer. I think the sound could start so softly I’d draw another breath without knowing it. I do draw another one, just so it can be over.

The door swings open and there are the two boards that lean away from each other on the gate. I look down at my feet. I can feel the dark board of the floor so plainly under both soles, and that means I can step out. I let myself stand there a little while. I’m going to say something as soon as I have somebody in front of me to say it to.

When I step down my left foot stays up. It carries so little weight at the ankle that I can do what I want with it. I bring the right one up beside it. I know I’ve got my balance from the feeling in my hips, and then I look down and there’s a space underneath both my boots with the yard showing through it. I take my hand away from the jamb. I can feel in my thighs where to get more height, and I rise until the ends of the ladders go past me on either side.

The copper roof is level with my eyes. I can see right along a seam to where a flat piece of wire sticks out in the gutter, and there’s a pot upside down behind the chimney that could only have got there because somebody had a use for it and left it when they came down. I have time to look at those things and at the rain cap and the dark behind the rain cap. I go on upward far enough that the roofs behind it open out, one beyond another, and there’s a whole street I’ve never been able to see, running down the other side of the hill.

When I turn to tell Rissa, the sash above the bakehouse is full of light and there is nobody at my height. I have my hands out already, the way they go when I want to show her a distance. I look at what I’ve measured between my two hands and then look below them, to where the bars and net of Bellwether Yard are small enough to see all at once. The shutter has come loose again and I can see it move. I listen for it, but all I hear is myself breathing. I stay at that height till the lights come on all the way up the street.
