# The World at One Step

Six weeks after the Boxes opened, there were enough buildings above Lipstone to put the freight hall in shadow before the end of a shift. The hotel went up that afternoon with its washing still out. A sheet came free as it crossed the apron, and Temra Vosen watched it fall the length of the building and go on falling until a driver got up on his truck to catch it. The doorman called down to thank him. He had to call twice.

The owners had offered Temra the highest office in Lipstone at a time when you could put a number on what that meant. They had sent her a plan of the hall at its proposed new height, with her desk marked in the corner and the people waiting at ground level drawn in pale ink. She’d unfolded it so often that the paper had gone soft at the crease. By Thursday the people at the bottom of the building had rubbed right off.

“Have you got a minute before you go up?” said the woman with the blue repair on her glove.

Temra had been waiting all week for a chance to say how much she could do for the crew from that office. She got through the first two things before she saw that Sefra had set her locker key on the back of her bare hand.

“My boy comes to the side door on Thursdays,” Sefra said. “He brings enough dinner for both of us if I’ve had to stay. I told him which pane to knock on because it’s got a wire loose in the glass. Will there be a door he can get to when the hall goes up?”

“They’re going to run a lift from the street.”

“Will you come to the door yourself every Thursday and tell the pilot to put my boy on with his dinner? How many times can you do that once the lift’s full of people who’ve paid?”

Sefra turned her hand over. The green key stayed in the air where she’d left it. She put her glove back on, pulling it tight at the wrist with her teeth.

Beyond them, the old freight radio was selling places by the seat. A pilot in the islands had four left to the market above her own capital. At Rendar it was already dark and the last ride to the hanging terraces cost twice what they’d asked in daylight. The managing owner was sitting in the booth he’d had built for the first ticket seller. He was eating one of the small hard apples Temra used to take up with her on a long run. His was the signature on her pay and on the deed to the hall. His partners had left him to raise the building or sell it before the end of the shift.

Past his booth stood the Box. The booth with a man in it came up only as high as the door cut into the graphite steel. One of the two wide copper bands had gone into shadow. The higher one ran round the casing three storeys up and was still in the sun. The bands had come in on their sides, with a man standing upright in the middle of each to undo the ties. As fast as the factories made the parts, the planes and ships had sent them out. Temra had packed the same numbered housings for capitals and islands she knew only as the other end of a route.

A man at the top of the low ramp changed his mind and made his way back down. With the door open he could take all the time he needed. The woman behind him held the rail until he had cleared it, then went up herself. She stopped inside with her right glove off and the left still on. She reached for the door. As she drew it shut, Temra heard the first low note of the motor go through the casing.

She’d been able to see a square of Olek’s red shirt until the last inch. Afterward there had been heat at the foot of the ramp where the air should have been cool. She’d packed the notices with every shipment. Anyone could try for free. Every closing, at every Box, an even chance of coming out with magic and an even chance of burning to death, whatever had happened to the person before you. She’d known the notice by heart while she stood there waiting for Olek. He’d burned at this one. The first thing she’d done with her changed hands after her own turn was pick up the back of her truck, which had a loose bottle in it she could hear rolling away from her up the bed.

The steel drew back. The woman came down holding the bare hand out in front of her. Halfway to the bottom she stopped. The glove she’d been carrying rose out of her palm, caught the air by one finger and slowly straightened. She walked the rest of the ramp with her palm under it, laughing in short bursts as if she were trying to get somebody a long way off to look before they missed it.

On the freight radio somebody said, “Kelm Quay. Hello to any town with its feet off the ground. We’ve got room off the headland and we can see you coming a long way out. Call for Mevrit once you can see the island.”

Temra knew Kelm by the road Olek had planned to take from the airstrip to the first place you could smell the sea. He’d made her sit down once and trace it with him. Sefra and two drivers were looking at her. Inside the hall the blue paint still smelled where they’d marked off lanes for the owners’ passengers. The foreman had put a line of chalk round the foundation for the lift. At the corners the chalk was already scuffed away by boots.

“Take the hall to Kelm,” Sefra said. “You’ve got all of us to move it. Let’s have a look at where it could go before somebody puts a ticket booth in front of it.”

The traffic office would have been Temra’s at seven the next morning. She could go over and eat an apple and ask the man in the booth what the pay was. She’d had the chance to ask him all afternoon. She asked him what he’d take for the hall.

He put the apple down. “Sign your name for the office and I’ll tell you exactly what you’ve got. A proper wage for everybody who takes a job up there. Your crew can take a look at the place we have ready before you move anything.”

Sefra came up behind Temra and asked how her boy would get up to bring her dinner. The owner offered to pass him through on Thursdays. Sefra moved back from the booth so he could see the people who’d come round behind her.

The line of chalk went underneath the booth. Temra could see the white there where the boards kept boots off it. “If I sell you back my shares in the carrier, will you sign the hall over to the crew?” She put the little blue share book on the ledge between them. She’d started filling it back when you could buy a share for two nights’ pay. She could still remember working round a hobbled plane for three nights to buy the last four.

He picked up the book and went slowly through it. “I could use those. I’ve still got people drawing wages on the ground.” He came back to the page with all the stamps on it. “The hall goes. The apron stays with the carrier. The company wages stop for anybody who goes with you.”

One of the drivers asked how long he could think about it. The owner said he had until the end of the shift, and swung the booth door open so anybody outside could hear him say it. Sefra caught the key she’d left hanging and put it in her pocket. The driver went to look for the people on his shift. Temra stayed there while the owner wrote the receipt, and held the blue book steady while he drew his pen through her name.

Three people left the hall while the rollers were still running. Sefra and the others were standing inside by the steel columns. At the doorway Temra rolled back the striped cuffs of her jacket and put both palms flat on the steel.

The first thing she felt was the others taking hold. The wall took a little time to follow. A light cable pulled loose at the side door, and the rollers’ bell sounded with its last poor breath and went quiet. Still pressing her palms to the steel, Temra looked down through the wired glass. The driver on the apron was small enough now that it took him both hands to hold the sheet up to where she could see it. The Box stood clear on the ground beyond him, its second copper band coming back into full light as the shadow of the freight hall moved away.

* * *

The frost in Shale Crown had whitened Davra’s steps, and cold came through Ivari’s boot soles as she stood on them. They had run straight to the door till the neighbor up the lane lifted the house forty feet into the air. He was down beside Ivari now, getting his flying board clear of the bottom step so people could come by. Overhead the wind teased up a ruffle of snow from the roof and let it settle again.

“You’ve still got your place if you want it,” the quarry buyer called to Davra from the turn. He’d brought the keys to the new houses, with a picture to show people what each would open. “I’ll show you the pictures again, if you like. The bedrooms are all the same size.”

“Let me show you ours,” Davra said. She put her head back and pointed up at her kitchen window. “We got up there yesterday and that was the first time I saw all the way to the birches while I was putting out plates.”

“You can take your house wherever you can get it to. My men come up from the quarry at noon. I need the lane empty when they get here.”

Davra’s husband had come up behind her with his coat open. He was looking at his end of the house, where the window was small and the snow had caught in the gutter.

“We have our ride to the headland,” he said. “I want to go into the Box first.”

Davra lowered her arm and looked at the neighbor’s board. He and his brother had got their own houses up first. That was his board to get from one house to the other, and he’d agreed to bring Davra and her husband along with their house when he went. Ivari had seen him take hold on the first lift and ask them to move the plates toward the middle. He could feel the weight go over.

“Where are you after going?” Davra asked her husband. “What’s wrong with the ride we’ve got?”

“I want to bring you up and down to the house myself,” he said. “I spent forty minutes down here last night waiting to get to my own bed. If I come out with it you can tell me what you want done with the house and have it done then and there. You could ask me to put it all the way out over the birches.” He looked up again. “I’d like to see them from the kitchen.”

The buyer went on down the lane and stood a little apart from them, putting the keys back in his pocket one at a time. Across the forecourt the Box stood where they used to keep the plow. The whole plow shed would have come inside the span of its upper band. There was hoarfrost in the shallow cuts of its factory number. The copper was wet where the current warmed the casing and dry again a little way below. In the light at the door you could see marks along the wall where people had gone in far enough to put a hand on it and come out to think.

“I’ll stay at the bottom,” Davra told her husband.

While he went up the ramp, Ivari crouched to get her laces tight. Cold came in where the leather folded. Her coat dragged on the frost. When she looked up again Davra’s husband was sitting down under the white light with his arms loose at his sides. He waited for Davra to look straight at him before he drew the door shut.

There was scarcely a sound. A line of orange appeared at the sill, grew bright enough for Ivari to see it on the ramp, and went out. In the next breath she smelled hot wool.

Two people were coming across the forecourt with a litter. The motor started up and the door slipped back from a darkness that shook with heat. Davra’s husband lay dead within the lip of the door. The people who’d come for him had to wait. Davra went up past them to the edge of the heat and bent down to speak her husband’s name as if she were the first one up that morning.

Ivari stood with her laces done up and nothing to do with her hands. She watched her own breath go away. The people with the litter carried him down to Davra’s sister’s house. Davra followed them. When she came back she had his cap. From the lane Ivari could see her working it open where it had been folded to fit in his pocket, and brushing it off with the back of her hand. The neighbor let his board come to rest by the empty steps and asked if she wanted to go up to the house on it.

“I can carry you with it when I go,” he said. “I’ll set you down wherever you ask.”

“And then you’ll go back for your brother?”

“He’s still got the hens below.”

Davra looked up at the kitchen door. “Set it on its steps. I want to walk up to it. I’d like to go in the back without anybody seeing I’ve gone in.”

Ivari could see the narrow part of the lane, the way between the empty steps and Davra’s sister’s back window. In winter they shoveled that part first because a person had to be able to get from their door to somebody else’s. Ivari had been thinking in houses ever since the first one went up. She’d had a whole list of them under her coat and room on the back of each sheet for how many people would need a ride.

She asked the neighbor to put Davra’s house back on its steps. He stooped to put a hand on his board. Up he went on it, his collar blowing flat against his cheek, until he was level with the kitchen. She watched him take hold at the corner. As the house came down Davra reached behind herself for the handrail and set one foot on the bottom step.

“If you brought your place back and your brother brought his,” Ivari called up to him, “we could take this much of the ridge together. I could take my end where the rock shows past the last house. I’ve worked on that face and it’ll go a long way down before you get a split. What I want to know is whether you’ll take hold with me at the other end. Get everybody who put something up to come and do this while we’ve got the people.”

“If I’ve understood you, you want the lane,” said the neighbor.

“I want enough of it for her to get up to her own door.”

Below the forecourt the buyer took a look at the side of the ridge. “As far down as the outcrop? I borrowed on that stone. My men have to be paid even if they come up and find the ridge gone.”

“Tell them before they start up,” Ivari said. “Take the low path down to the quarry and you can get to them now.”

He kept looking awhile longer, measuring with a hand held flat against the outline of the hill. Then he took the house keys out of his pocket and went down by the low path.

The neighbors spread down the long face of the hill. More people came up from the airstrip, and as each found a place to lay a hand there was another wet glove put down in the frost. A man from the last house on the turn came out with his scarf in one hand. He wanted to stay in that house. It was just below the part Ivari had asked them to take.

“Will there be a way up if I change my mind?” he said.

“You’ll have to send for one of us. The lane goes at your top gate.”

He folded the scarf across his arm and stood with her long enough to see the last houses go back into place in their old order. Then he took the low path with his boots sliding at the steep part, and waved when he reached the quarry road. Ivari could see where he’d set each foot in the frost.

She put both hands to the rock. A lorry would have fit easily in the first cleft that opened. From where she stood she could see the roots of the birches come out of the ground in their full length, carrying little bells of frozen soil that rang against one another. The lane came with them. She heard someone shout from above and had time to look and see a whole washing line between two houses draw tight in the wind.

They took the ridge clear with the occupied streets on it and the great copper bands of the Box shining in the forecourt. The quarry stairs stopped in air. High up on the house side of them, Davra had both feet on the first of her own steps.

For eight days it took all the people they had to steer the ridge across the ocean. At night they let it stay where it was, and the weather moved beneath it. Snow had gone from the roofs by the time they reached the shipping lanes off Lirven. They could smell kelp from the same height where they’d once been able to smell snow.

Ivari went round the curve of the lane to see why her end of the ridge had stopped. Over the coast of Lirven there was a white square a long way off the ground, with a double row of government buildings standing along its edge. The whole square was coming up toward them. Ivari remembered looking down on it out of a plane. She’d counted four streets coming together at the white columns. Now she could see all four streets end in the air.

The name of the court came across the freight radio. People with petitions should wait below for further notice. The court would sit above the coast from noon. One voice was giving the new height of the court while a man on the ground asked what to do with the people who’d come to have their papers heard. Ivari could see them only as a few dashes at the foot of a wide flight of steps. One person set a table there, in the last of the shade. Behind the table a line began to form. Beyond it the upper band of Lirven’s Box came out into the sun. The Box was staying on the ground.

The ridge waited till the court had gone by. Down below, on the open sea, there was a ship with white lengths on its deck. Ivari took them for canvas over ordinary freight until the captain called from the Rendar packet. It had been to three countries for people who’d died at the new Boxes. Some of the families had come along to take them home. The captain named the dead over the radio so people on shore could tell who was coming. The name read out last took a long time. Somebody on deck had said it once for her and then had to say it again.

Before they were out of range, the captain asked where Rendar’s landing was now. Ivari had heard that the moving part of the town was bound for Kelm, and told her as much. Then a house on Ivari’s road shut its door. She could hear the latch go in clear air, a clean click a whole sea’s width from where it had been put on.

* * *

Above the clouds off Kelm there was sunshine on the pears. Sali had put her crates down by the first of the empty stalls and was shaking the rain out of her sleeves. Below her feet the freight exchange ran to a chipped white edge. A month ago it had stood at the head of a set of steps on another island. You could still see the ends of the iron banisters where it had come away.

Mevrit had been up once already. She knew the surprise of walking into sunlight with a load that had gone on under rain. The smell of the sea fell away at this height. Standing by the stall she could smell a pear that had split at the corner of Sali’s crate. She meant to take Sali to a place by the outer rail where you could see a string of six separate streets hanging over the water. On her way there Sali stopped with one hand on the warm stone and stood quite still.

“Is that our island?” she said.

Through an opening in the cloud Mevrit could see the landing and the square top of the Box. Sun caught a corner of one copper band and there was a scatter of light on the water beyond it. On the island itself the market awnings were the red they went when they’d had all the rain they could take. She pointed out the green one they had got from their aunt.

“I can see that,” Sali said. She went straight past Mevrit to the end of the rail. “Look where the shadow of the old market ends. It comes right up to the boats.”

Mevrit hadn’t known that was what Sali would want to see. She could have shown her Shale Crown, which had come in by dark and was waiting out to sea. The freight hall from Lipstone hung lower on its own, its broad doors level with the cliff opposite. Sali stayed at the rail, following the shadow of the old market until it touched the first boat at its mooring.

Sefra called them onto the platform. She was holding the rail with her bare hand and had the glove with the blue repair still on the other. Sali hurried back to draw a crate of pears a little farther into the sun. For an instant she had it on one knee with just the tips of her fingers under it. Then she had to put it down hard. She walked onto the platform and went straight to the rim Sefra had told them to stay a foot back from. Sefra pulled her glove off with her teeth and gripped the rail with both hands. The deck moved out until every part of it was over the cloud. Sali backed up then, laughing, and brought her whole weight against Mevrit’s shoulder.

At the foot of the old quay steps, the pilot brought the deck to rest above the wet stones and took her hands off the rail. For a little while it was queer to feel the ground give so little under each foot. Mevrit went up the steps to help Sali get their aunt’s awning. They unhooked the weighted ends together and walked the folded thing down between them. Already they could hear the pilot counting the people on her next load.

Temra was under a lean-to keeping a list of names dry with her jacket. Sali asked if she could take the awning up to her pears. Sefra’s lift was full already. Temra put a finger on the list and said she could call it straight back down for Sali.

“The girls will still be in lessons,” said a woman waiting under the lean-to. Mevrit had seen her see them off that morning, the two of them going up with one schoolbag between them. “It’s their lift home.” Temra moved her finger back to the top of the sheet.

“Leave me the awning,” Mevrit said. “I’ll bring it up myself when I’ve spoken to Shale Crown.”

Sali picked up the other end of the awning and brought it round so the two of them stood on opposite sides of it. Rain was coming through the canvas onto Mevrit’s boots.

“Come as far as the steps with me,” Sali said. “You’ve got things to do.”

There was more light at the top of the steps. They set the awning on the fixed landing by the Box. The long street went uphill into town. The red shelf of the market ran along the side of the headland to their left, and to their right there was deep water. The eaves of the first houses were scarcely level with the lowest copper band. Mevrit could reach that band from the top of the ramp. It had been warm under her hand on the day she went in.

Sali took hold of the rail at the start of the ramp. Mevrit remembered how the shore had come into sight in parts as she walked down after her own trial, and how she’d wanted to lift every part of it.

“Have you been thinking about going in?” she asked.

“Since I tried that crate up there.” Sali took her hand off the rail and put it back. A woman coming down the slope saw where they were standing and gave them room. “I had it in my head where I wanted to put it, and it seemed for a second that my hand could do that. Are you going to tell me I can’t?”

“If you want to, you can.” Mevrit tried to say the rest looking straight at her. “Which part would you want to do if you came out with it?”

“Bring the pears up at four,” Sali said. “Carry the empty crates home afterward even when you’re gone. I went round to get you last week when you said you’d take me over to the other island. Your shoes were outside and I thought you’d left them there for me to see. I waited on the step till it started to rain and found out you’d flown a chair straight out of the window without putting shoes on.”

“That woman needed the chair.”

“I know. I had somebody waiting for me, too.” Sali glanced over at the awning. “You do a lot for me. You say you will, and then there’s a great deal of waiting.”

A line of drops fell off the outer lip of the door. From inside came the slight metallic hum Mevrit knew in her hands. She wanted to say if Sali just waited now, she would take every last thing there was to take up to the exchange herself. Under the running rain the four steps between them and the old market were black, the same steps she’d been going up and down with crates since she was big enough to turn one over on her own. Her aunt had needed them to sit on near the end, to get her breath when she came out to inspect the stall.

“Suppose we moved the market,” she said.

“We’ve got one up there.”

“Out to sea. Right out from this landing, so you’d come down the street the way you do in the dark. Where it bends by the stairs you could keep going. You could wait here while I ask the others.”

Sali gave her a look. “That’ll be five minutes, will it?”

“I saw you from my window when I went out with that chair. I could have called down.” There was a little warm air on Mevrit’s cheek even at the foot of the ramp. She stepped back from it. “I’m going to ask them about the market. If there’s somewhere on it you want to put up the cloth, let me know where to bring my end.”

Sali looked in through the door. The light was good enough to show the rain on her shoes. “I can clear the part of the shelf I’d use,” she said, and drew back from the rail. She had to crouch to get both ends of the awning off the ground. Mevrit put out a hand. “Leave it,” Sali said. “Come and get my table when I’ve emptied it.”

Mevrit found the people on the headland who’d already lifted things, drinking from the end of the tea stall. She pointed out the shelf and showed them how she’d bring its end round to the landing. One of them used to sell plums on the outer edge and went back to take down his stall. Others came with him. Sali had emptied her family’s table by then. She showed Mevrit where to get a grip and took the lighter end herself.

At the foot of the steps she took up the radio. Ivari had brought the ridge near enough to see the fixed landing. Mevrit asked her to put the ridge road against the end of the market, where it would be open to anybody coming down from town.

“I can still take these houses so high the quarry man will never put a foot on his old stone again,” Ivari said. “If we put it down by your market, he can walk right up to our doors and tell us what the ground is worth. We’ve taken our houses this far. I want to hear what your people think they’ll have a right to if I bring them in.”

“Let us walk as far as the birches,” Mevrit said. “Further, if there’s a public lane. Your houses belong to the people who live in them.”

The radio carried wind for a time. “I’ve left a man at the bottom of that road who can’t come up through his own top gate,” Ivari said at last. “If he comes all this way, I want him to be able to walk in. Leave me the wide end of the market.” Mevrit heard her tell somebody away from the receiver to take hold at the far end of the ridge.

It took the vendors time to clear the weighted stalls. Some took the fruit out, moving one box at a time under the awnings, and some merely stood by until a person with a pair of strong hands could come over and float a whole stall clear of the shelf. In the gap where the fruit tables had been, Mevrit put her hands to the stone with enough room to straighten her elbows. She could feel it give as the others reached the ends of the shelf and took hold. Her first impulse was to look behind her and call for the people on the stairs to stand back. Then she saw how far all of them had gone from the stairs already.

They brought the shelf round until the old lane that used to take you from the step to the first stall pointed straight out to sea. On land there was an open cut where the market had been. Water was still running off a red awning that someone had brought up flat across both arms. Mevrit could see, past each of the pairs of hands on the stone, a little more water where just before there’d been the shoulder of the headland. She had to move slowly to match her end to the landing below the Box. It felt the way a wet board feels when you lower it in the dark, with the width of the load in both arms and all your attention on the last place where it might catch. The two edges met on a level. When she let go there was a little water running from the market stone across onto the landing.

The lift from the upper exchange came down for the Lipstone crew. A man in a white coat took his hands off the rail and said the berth was ready for the hall. Through a break in the weather Mevrit could see the bright flags overhead where the hall would go. The streets on either side were full of people going to market in the sun. There was a moment, before any of the crew moved, when she saw the things the man in the white coat had come down to offer them.

“We can go up now,” Temra said to the people on the steps. “We all get paid from the first day, as I told you. I want the hall here, with both doors on the market. Anybody who can still afford the turn, come and take hold with me.”

“I could have stayed in Lipstone at a wage,” said a man on the lower steps. “I came because you had work for me up there. I’ve been putting away what I have to send back for my boys. If we put it down here, what do I tell them when it’s time to send the next lot?”

Temra turned the list of names over in her hands. “I haven’t got money to promise you. I sold my shares for the hall. I can put in a shift on the door here myself. If you want your place on that lift, take it. You can still come in our side door whenever you’re down.”

The man took a step out of the way of the people who’d stopped behind him. The man in the white coat said there was work for anyone who could help raise the next stands for the exchange market. Two members of the hall crew went aboard with him, their jackets still rolled up at the wrists for taking hold. The father stayed on the step until Sefra moved her own bag clear. Then he climbed on with them and took the place beside the pilot.

Sefra came over far enough to see the new shelf past the Box. “Set the wired door here,” she told Temra. “Let whoever needs to knock get to the pane.” One of the women from the hall had set her bag in a place on the lift. She reached over the rail to get it back and laid it beside Sefra’s on the step.

They had lost the people who knew how to turn the far end of the hall. Mevrit heard Temra count the places they’d left. She brought two of the market lifters onto Sefra’s platform with her. High out over the sea she stepped from its rail onto the freight floor and took hold where Temra put her. Through both open doors she could see the lines the boats made behind them on the water. Once she had the corner under her hands, she felt what everybody at the other end had been up against. It took Temra starting again before the whole floor moved. The jacket stripes ran up to her elbows when she widened her feet against the pull.

Mevrit could step straight off onto the new shelf once they had the hall low enough. She turned on the edge to look back through both freight doors at the sea.

From beyond the far end of the market came the sound of stone moving in the air. A dark line was coming toward them out of the rain. It was a road, with birches growing along one side, and on its far side the whole long ridge with lights on in the houses. Below the road the rock went a long way down to roots that hung over the sea. Mevrit looked up from where she stood and saw someone from Shale Crown taking hold beside the first tree with Ivari, who’d opened her heavy coat to the wind. All the small changes of height the ridge made while they brought it in went through the line of roofs and into the trees. The woman beside Ivari kept calling out the distance from the road to the edge of the red stone. Mevrit went out past the last stall and called back as the first birch came level with her feet.

The road came flush with the market. Temra and her people turned the freight hall until the largest doors faced the new street and the small one with the wired glass was at the corner of the steps. Then the whole line of shade from stone and roofs lay out past the farthest waves Mevrit could see breaking. Boats that had been crossing open water under full light passed into the shadow of a coast.

A woman waited for Ivari to take her hands off the rock. She’d come up along the road from one of the houses, wearing boots with little white grains of Shale still pressed into the seams. Ivari called her Davra and offered her a hand. She smiled at Ivari and walked across. Mevrit watched her pause by a stall that was just going back on its feet. She put one thumb to the gold side of a pear.

Sali had the awning back over her shoulders. She took the steps two at a time. By the foot of the Box ramp she stopped to see whether Mevrit was looking. Then she went over too. The path was wide enough for her to carry the awning without turning sideways. She unfolded it on her family’s table in the place she’d cleared for it and went back for the weights herself. “I’ll get my crates down on the last lift tonight,” she called. “They’ve got sun in the meantime.”

Somebody’s radio in the open hall was still taking bookings. Eight to the upper exchange at the evening fare. Off Kelm, a row of lights came close enough through the rain that Mevrit could make them out as lamps down the middle of a street. The people from Rendar were bringing an inhabited ledge longer than any of the pieces here. Beyond the two copper bands of their Box the roofline ran down to the old harbor steps. Below there was a ship riding at rest. Its deck was full of people who had been on their feet long enough to know how to stand in the swell without a hand on the rail.

It was the harbormaster of Rendar who called to ask if the berth at the exchange was still clear. Mevrit could see a person on the steps with one hand up to her mouth. The bottom steps were dark where they used to meet the tide. Now they were well above the tips of the ship’s masts.

“We’ve still got space for your street,” came the answer from overhead. “Dry berths and the full rate for your market sheds. Come up now and I can leave the lights on for you. A ship that size can’t come with you.”

Another voice cut in. “Take the berth,” he said to the harbormaster. “The sheds can start paying in the morning. If we turn it down, I haven’t got a day’s wage for any of the people who brought the quay out here.”

The captain of the packet came on the radio. “The little quay at Kelm can’t take this ship. If your steps stay in the air I’ll go back to Lirven at dark. It’s the next place I can get in. I’ve got people who came all that way to get their dead home.”

Mevrit went to the open doors of the hall to see how much sea there was beyond them. One moment the old steps at Rendar were outlined against empty air, and the next you could see the edge of the whole shore behind them. She picked up the radio. “If you bring your road down level with the foot of the Box ramp, there’s sea to spare here past the doors of the hall. Your steps could reach the ship.”

The harbormaster asked for the people holding both ends of Rendar to speak. There was a woman at the far end who said she could reach the market if they started to turn now. The man with the sheds asked if his people could bring what they had to sell onto the new street. Mevrit went to see where the hall’s shadow ended. She told him there was room for them in the open over the sea, beside the door. A voice at the water end said they had three hands on the quay. The harbormaster told them to bring the steps down first.

Mevrit put down the receiver. For a little while the lamps stayed at the same height. Then they began to turn toward Kelm. She could see a pair of people taking hold between every two of them. The sea came up to the dark line on the steps. The ship changed course with just room to spare for its stern and somebody on the deck threw a line. A woman with a light at the foot of Rendar’s steps leaned out to catch it. She held her light low for the first people bringing a white case up the stone.

At the top of the quay steps a woman was going up the ramp of the Box. She put her hand to the casing for a while before she went inside. She had the whole breadth of the doorway to turn in. All the air from the new coast came past her as she reached for the edge of the door and pulled it shut.
