# Provisional direction: A Fire for a Stranger

## Promised experience

The explicit sequence is a traveller seeking storm shelter, an old manor, a pale woman in a silver gown who offers her hand and leads him upstairs to a freshly lit fire, then breakfast in the village and the discovery that the house has stood empty since its mistress was murdered. The pleasure I infer from that sequence is Gothic hospitality becoming uncanny in retrospect: the reader must feel the offered hand and the fire's welcome before learning what should have made both impossible. The request specifies the encounter between host and stranger, but no romance, revenge, culprit, supernatural rules, or explanation. It sets no required scale or ending beyond the breakfast revelation. The supplied original supports a luminous pale-haired adult woman reaching toward the viewer within a dark Gothic room, with an ornate mirror and tall window; I take its inviting, unsettling contrast as visual guidance, not a demand for erotic treatment.

## Writer's provisional choices

A compact, close-third-person tale follows an adult traveller with an ordinary reason to be on the road, without a hidden connection to the murder. His immediate conflict is whether to trust an unfamiliar house; the later conflict is what to make of a kindness that cannot have occurred. The mistress remains an individual host whose intentions cannot be completely known. No rule requires him to solve her murder or release her. The violence stays in the reported past.

- In failing evening light, the storm makes the road impassable on foot. A light appears at the manor. The woman opens before his second knock. He hesitates at her offered hand, removes his wet glove, and chooses to take it. Her hand is cold; her grip is firm. The silver gown catches light that seems absent elsewhere in the hall. She pauses on the upper landing before taking him into the guest room.
- A freshly kindled fire welcomes him. The room has an ornate mirror and a high window through which he can see the storm. In the mirror he sees her watching the landing. Asked if she is expecting someone, she says that people sometimes arrive in this weather. He offers a particular split-edged coin; she closes his fingers over it and says there is no charge. The brief second touch seeds the coin through the prompt's offered hand. She leaves him with genuine warmth and a dry bed. No other voice or footfall sounds in the house.
- Before dawn he wakes rested, with his coat dry and the fire low. His host does not answer his quiet thanks from the landing. He takes the same coin out of his purse and leaves it on the warm hearth for fuel and bed. Its placement is visible and unambiguous. He closes and pockets his purse, leaving the coin behind, then departs for breakfast in the village.
- At the inn he asks the mistress's name so that he can send thanks. The landlady interrupts the ordinary breakfast: the manor has stood empty since its mistress was murdered many years before. His description identifies the pale-haired woman in her silver gown. The landlady's mother was the housemaid; she saw the mistress admit a visitor out of a storm and later found her murdered on the upper landing. These witnessed events are distinguished from inference about the killer. The traveller first feels the remembered landing and her cold grip before attempting an explanation. The landlady's disbelief about the fresh upstairs fire returns his attention to its impossible warmth. The account stays brief; the visit becomes neither an investigation nor an explanation of the ghost's purpose.
- When he opens his purse to pay for breakfast, the same coin falls into his palm, too warm to hold. He drops it onto the saucer. Its heat recalls the manor hearth on which he left it; he remembers her cold fingers closing his hand over it. The returned coin brings the hand and fire into daylight. Her hospitality remains real, and her agency continues beyond his departure. The story does not settle her intentions or explain a kindness-after-betrayal moral.

The actual dramatic center is the traveller's bodily trust in a stranger, reinterpreted by the discovery of her murder and prolonged by her impossible return of the coin. The largest risk is that the payment could displace the welcome or make its meaning reassuringly tidy. Keep payment brief, let the murder change his memory of her particular route and touch, and preserve the disturbing persistence of her agency without inventing a threat merely to force a darker answer.
