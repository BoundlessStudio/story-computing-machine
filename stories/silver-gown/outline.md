# Provisional direction: A Fire for a Stranger

## Promised experience

The explicit sequence is a traveller seeking storm shelter, an old manor, a pale woman in a silver gown who offers her hand and leads him upstairs to a freshly lit fire, then breakfast in the village and the discovery that the house has stood empty since its mistress was murdered. The pleasure I infer from that sequence is Gothic hospitality becoming uncanny in retrospect: the reader must feel the offered hand and the fire's welcome before learning what should have made both impossible. The request specifies the encounter between host and stranger, but no romance, revenge, culprit, supernatural rules, or explanation. It sets no required scale or ending beyond the breakfast revelation. The supplied original supports a luminous pale-haired adult woman reaching toward the viewer within a dark Gothic room, with an ornate mirror and tall window; I take its inviting, unsettling contrast as visual guidance, not a demand for erotic treatment.

## Writer's provisional choices

A compact, close-third-person tale follows an adult traveller with an ordinary reason to be on the road, without a hidden connection to the murder. His immediate conflict is whether to trust an unfamiliar house; the later conflict is what to make of a kindness that cannot have occurred. The mistress remains an individual host whose intentions cannot be completely known. No rule requires him to solve her murder or release her. The violence stays in the reported past.

- In failing evening light, the storm makes the road impassable on foot. A light appears at the manor. The woman opens before his second knock. He hesitates at her offered hand, then takes it because his fingers are too numb to manage the stair rail. Her hand is cold; her grip is firm. The silver gown catches light that seems absent elsewhere in the hall.
- She takes him upstairs herself. The guest room has a freshly kindled fire, an ornate mirror, and a high window through which he can see the storm. He first sees her watching the stairs in the mirror rather than watching him. When he asks if she is expecting someone, she says only that people sometimes arrive in this weather. She declines payment and leaves him with genuine warmth and a dry bed. Small absences, such as no other voice or footfall in the house, accumulate without turning the night into an overt attack.
- Before dawn he wakes rested, with his coat dry and the fire low. His host does not answer his quiet thanks from the landing. Reluctant to leave without acknowledging the fuel and bed, he puts a distinctive damaged coin on the mantel and leaves for breakfast in the village.
- At the inn he asks the mistress's name so that he can send thanks. The landlady's matter-of-fact response interrupts the ordinary breakfast: the manor has stood empty since its mistress was murdered many years before. His description of the woman, her silver gown, and the room identifies her independently of his assumption. The murder is reported briefly, without solving it or making his visit an investigation. His dry clothes and rested body make a dream explanation inadequate.
- When he opens his purse to pay for breakfast, the damaged coin falls into his palm. It is warm enough that he drops it onto the saucer. The story ends with the breakfast fire unlit, the coin still warming the china, and the traveller remembering that his hostess had refused payment. Her welcome is real; whether returning the coin is courtesy, insistence, or a continuing claim remains unanswered.

The actual dramatic center is the traveller's bodily trust in a stranger, overturned by the discovery of her death and prolonged by an impossible return of her refused payment. The largest risk is that the returned coin could make the ending feel reassuringly tidy. The prose should preserve the woman's unresolved attention to the stairs and the disturbing persistence of her agency into daylight, without inventing a threat merely to force a darker answer.
