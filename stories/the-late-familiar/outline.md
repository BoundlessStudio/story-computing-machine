# The Late Familiar — provisional direction

Hesta, an adult woman with brown skin, dark hair dyed blue at the ends, and a very small rented room, summons her first familiar at nineteen. Three years later she is a scenery painter for a traveling theatre company, self-taught at a few unreliable illusions, and finally due to leave town with them. Then twenty pounds of long-haired white-and-gray cat arrives. He sleeps across her face, eats her travel provisions, and gives no useful help. She has the return basket ready. The lost years include concrete occasions when she waited for magic to qualify her to begin; she has only recently stopped doing that.

The kingdom's most feared sorcerer visits her because the company has discreetly recommended her for a modest disguise. His daughter is making her stage debut with them. He wants to sit in the audience as an ordinary father, but the terrifying glamour he has worn for decades now comes on whenever strangers look at him. On seeing Hesta's cat, he asks whether his old master is accepting apprentices again. His deference makes the animal no less obstructive. Hesta has paid for a familiar and needs to leave; the visitor wants his former teacher back. The cat has not arranged their setbacks or secretly improved Hesta by withholding himself. He mislaid her summons and, once he found it, treated arriving late as an inconvenience she would naturally absorb.

Hesta's first attempt to cover the sorcerer's glamour with a grander disguise fails. The cat taught him to frighten enemies when he was young and frightened himself; knowing how to cast that protection is different from knowing how to stop reaching for it. He asks the cat for a stronger counterspell. Hesta recognizes a performer trying to see the whole audience at once, something her small stage illusions also fail under. She offers a rehearsal instead: one spectator, one simple, chosen thing to show them. The cat may understand immense magic, but Hesta has spent three years learning how to be seen while imperfect. The sorcerer has to try her instruction without a guarantee or his master's endorsement.

Possible central scene: at the company's empty little theatre, Hesta uses the unglamorous painted scenery she helped make and a tiny moving light she can sustain without a familiar. The sorcerer is asked to hold a plain, harmless illusion for her rather than overpower his old image. It goes badly before it goes better; she stops treating his fear of failure as a command to flatter or rescue him. The achievement is a brief glimpse of an ordinary, tired man, enough to begin rehearsal for his daughter's opening night. His whole past is not repaired. The cat does not supply a last-minute answer.

Hesta keeps her departure date. Whether she keeps the cat depends on his accepting that this is the life he has arrived in: she will not stay home to become his disciple, and his former pupil cannot commandeer her new companion. She asks for an account of the delay and practical help now, rather than accepting his pedigree as repayment. He has to acknowledge being late and do something useful on her terms, perhaps hold her difficult moving illusion while she packs scenery. Their eventual partnership should include affection and a real remaining nuisance. Possible ending: on the first night on the road, she makes him a place near her pillow. He tests the route toward her face; she shifts him, and he finally stays where she puts him. The old sorcerer is coming to the opening performance without his ceremonial escort.

The prose will probably use Hesta's dry, close first-person voice, with the cat's grandeur revealed through the visitor's behavior before the cat speaks. Protect the ridiculous face-sleeping, the novice's justified grievance, the sorcerer's dignity inside his embarrassment, and the value of the life she built while waiting. Open risks for readers: whether the glamour's mechanism makes the rehearsal genuinely causal; whether the daughter occasion earns its place without turning into a second family story; whether keeping the cat costs or means enough; and whether the ending should leave more feline resistance than the pillow compromise suggests.
