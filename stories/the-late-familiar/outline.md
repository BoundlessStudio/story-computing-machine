# The Late Familiar — provisional direction

Hesta, an adult woman with brown skin, dark hair dyed blue at the ends, and a very small rented room, summons her first familiar at nineteen. Three years later she is a scenery painter for a traveling theatre company, self-taught at a few unreliable illusions, and finally due to leave town with them. Then twenty pounds of long-haired white-and-gray cat arrives. He sleeps across her face, eats her travel provisions, and gives no useful help. She has the return basket ready. The lost years include concrete occasions when she waited for magic to qualify her to begin; she has only recently stopped doing that.

The kingdom's most feared sorcerer visits her because the company has discreetly recommended her for a modest disguise. His daughter is making her stage debut with them. He wants to sit in the audience as an ordinary father, but the terrifying glamour he has worn for decades now comes on whenever strangers look at him. On seeing Hesta's cat, he asks whether his old master is accepting apprentices again. His deference makes the animal no less obstructive. Hesta has paid for a familiar and needs to leave; the visitor wants his former teacher back. The cat has not arranged their setbacks or secretly improved Hesta by withholding himself. He mislaid her summons and, once he found it, treated arriving late as an inconvenience she would naturally absorb.

Hesta's attempt to cover the sorcerer's glamour with a grander disguise fails. The cat taught him to frighten enemies when he was young and frightened himself. The old protective spell is tied to a gesture, thumb folded inside fist; under watching eyes he closes his hand before he notices. He knows how to open it alone, but stronger counterspells do not interrupt the action sustaining the original. Hesta notices the fist. She offers a theatre rehearsal: sustain a small moving illusion of a moth above his open palm, attend to its landing point, and let one spectator look at him. Failure has a visible cause when his fingers close. The exercise gives him another physical action under that pressure, rather than a wise analogy which dissolves his difficulty.

At the company's little theatre, the cat makes the sorcerer's glamour visible to its own caster through his live sight. It is a spell made for other eyes; an ordinary looking glass has always shown the sorcerer his own plain face. Now his teacher can show him the thing other people see and exactly when it changes. That is formidable, useful mastery, but the cat initially instructs him to stand still and withstand the gaze, which tightens his fist. Hesta asks him to stop and let the man move. The cat has to follow her rehearsal direction; she needs his magical perception, and he needs her practical knowledge. A stagehand's unexpected entrance tests the small success with a real stranger. They achieve a brief glimpse of an ordinary, tired man, enough to begin rehearsal for his daughter's opening night. No one supplies a permanent cure, and neither Hesta nor the cat can have made the scene work alone. The cat need not have abandoned his old pupil or caused every later mistake for his teaching to have limits.

Hesta keeps her departure date. Whether she keeps the cat depends on his accepting that this is the life he has arrived in: she will not stay home to become his disciple, and his former pupil cannot commandeer her new companion. She asks for an account of the delay rather than accepting his pedigree as repayment. He acknowledges being late and follows her direction when her work requires it. She has also seen what it would mean to learn from him: brilliant instruction she can question, immense ability which does not make him right about everything. Keeping him begins in the rehearsal, not as a reward for a chore afterward. On the first night on the road, he still wants her face and she still needs to breathe. The exact negotiated nuisance of their sleeping arrangement remains for the prose; a docile cat would close the joke and the grievance too neatly. The old sorcerer is coming to the opening performance without his ceremonial escort.

The prose will probably use Hesta's dry, close first-person voice, with the cat's grandeur revealed through the visitor's behavior before the cat speaks. Protect the ridiculous face-sleeping, the novice's justified grievance, the sorcerer's dignity inside his embarrassment, and the value of the life she built while waiting. Open risks for readers: whether the gesture and shared-sight mechanism remain easy to follow; whether the daughter occasion earns its place without turning into a second family story; and whether keeping this particular cat feels like an appetizing, consequential choice rather than mere tolerance.
