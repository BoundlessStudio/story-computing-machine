# Provisional direction: Where the Gods Left Their Doors

The vanished pantheons are ordinary documented history to Talia, whose family kept one of their temple doors. After a fire displaces her mother and several neighbors, she pulls down a damaged temple panel and exposes the outer face of a warm, ribbed living gate. In the gods' time, mortal attendants crossed only by invitation from its hidden inner face. Her mother tries the familiar outward rite on the exposed gate; it gives no answer. Talia presses her palm to the living tissue, and it draws apart into a human-sized passage. That response grants access, not permission. Her mother refuses to lead anyone into what she still calls the gods' home, but follows Talia to see what lies within. Outside, the neighbors wait with blankets and bread. Talia wants a place where they can sleep tonight; her mother has spent a life preserving the door for owners who never returned.

The Hearth Nine's great table is laid with stone plates, but no meal or host remains, and the fires are cold. Their own bread will have to feed them. A bounded old guest passage reaches the storm pantheon's court, where glassy clouds cast moving shadows across empty thrones and sharp hail makes the floor unsafe. These are distinct, documented divine homes left vacant, not a general absence inferred from a few doors. Looking for a room they can use, Talia meets three signs of what may follow divine abandonment. Living red, blue, and green currents feed a separate black frame. She touches green, seeking a kinder place, and it opens high onto a forest so large it curves beneath the horizon; a moss-covered stair on a vast bough bears an old pantheon's documented mark, but there is no safe step down. A different threshold shows red grass and a sleeping carrier under copper awnings; an abandoned shrine bearing another known mark has been built into its inhabited shell. At a hot arch, a horned worker comes into the guest passage carrying glazed bowls from a populated brick kiln court; an old divine relief is visible behind her, put to ordinary use. The views are brief, with different consequences: the green place is real but inaccessible to these refugees, the carrier's back is occupied, and the worker has a claim on the passage. These observed places can be former divine domains without their distinct access workings having one origin.

Talia finds a plain service annex once used by mortal guests. It has daylight, breathable air, intact walls, warmth drifting from the nearby working kilns, and enough floor for their blankets, but the kiln workers use its central aisle to carry ware between their court and another old route. Talia's mother recognizes the guest markings and remembers that the aisle was always kept clear, even when gods occupied the great hall. Their work does not give the kiln workers ownership of the Hearth Nine, yet Talia cannot turn their passage into her private shelter. She asks the worker whether her neighbors may sleep against the walls. Her mother offers to sit beside the annex's doorway to the guest passage and keep the center aisle and doors usable through the night. The worker accepts those terms. Talia takes the exposed, shared refuge instead of the secure empty room she wanted; she returns for the waiting people and places the first bedroll beside the wall, clear of the path. Her mother brings the old temple door-keeper's stool to the agreed doorway and sits down. The divine courts remain desolate; in their former service space, living people begin a particular afterlife for one abandoned domain. The realms themselves originated as those deities' domains and have outlasted them.
