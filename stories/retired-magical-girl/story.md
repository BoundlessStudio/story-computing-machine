# The Last Loop

I got my permit at eleven in the morning. By four, Mom had made me drive past the same red mailbox so many times that its owner came out to see what we wanted. I waved. Mom said, “Two hands, Nola,” and gave him the little apologetic wave she usually reserved for people who'd caught us in the wrong seats at the movies.

The next time round she told me to take a left at the box and keep going up. I'd never been up that road. We passed a sign for a lookout with the face of it peeled white by the sun. I wanted to take the curves at a speed that felt like I was getting somewhere, but every time the car nosed downhill even a little, Mom slid her right heel out and took hold of the door handle. For all the good it did her she could have stayed home and pulled on a cupboard.

“Does that help?” I said on the last bend.

She looked down at her hand. “No,” she said, and kept it there until we pulled into the lookout.

There was a low wall on the town side of the lot, with a gap where the footpath went down the hill. I parked well back from it. Mom got out and took her old red glasses case with her. It had one hinge and a pattern of little dents where I'd chewed it as a baby. I was relieved when she went to the wall. I thought she needed a minute without me.

“Come see,” she said.

The lot was hot enough to smell of tar. Down in town, roofs caught the light in uneven rows, and there was a tram coming across the high span. I tried to guess which of the red roofs belonged to our house. Mom opened the case. Inside, where I'd expected to see her reading glasses, there was a length of pink ribbon rolled around itself so neatly I could see its white edge going all the way into the middle.

She thumbed the end loose and drew it through her hand. It went on coming long after there should have been any left. She flicked her wrist. The ribbon cleared the wall, rose on its edge, and made a bend high over the tram. It kept going, through a drop and up into a huge last loop, before it came around and pointed its nose back toward us. The very far bits looked thin enough to have been drawn on a window.

Mom put her hand through a little loop at the end she was still holding and stepped into the gap in the wall. Light ran up her arm. The wind snapped the loose edge of her grey jacket. I saw pink satin on the next snap, and when she pushed her cuffs back there were stars all along the lining, so bright they showed through the cloth. On her feet were pointed white boots with pink wings at the ankles, the kind a kid would draw if you'd asked her to design boots for running on the sky.

I knew those boots. Everybody in our sixth-grade class had seen the film of the tram that came off the high span. It was old enough to be blurry, but you could see Comet Lace draw a great U of pink under the falling tram. When the tram hit it, the ribbon bowed nearly to the street before she pulled it level. For a second just before the catch you could make out the girl herself, one hand drawn close to her hip and one heel out in front of her. I'd just watched Mom do that on every bend up the hill.

“Comet Lace?” I said.

“I was, yes.” She looked down at herself. “I forgot about the wings.” She pulled at one with her free hand and it sprang back against her boot.

“You were a magical girl.” I had to say the whole thing out loud to see if it sounded as strange as it felt. “You. How long?”

“Seven years. I retired two summers before you were born.”

Behind her, the ribbon gave a little shiver all the way out over town. I put a hand on the wall. It was as solid and unremarkable as it had been when we got there.

“Does Gran know?”

“Gran cut out the wings.” Mom crooked her ankle to get a better look at them. “She said I should have made them bigger. I told her I had to get through doors.”

There was so much I could have asked that for a minute all I could think was that I had a picture of Comet Lace in a shoebox somewhere. I'd drawn it for school. Mom had helped me make the wings the same size.

“This was my first route.” Mom held up the little hand loop. “I used to come up here with a pocketful of ribbons and fly until my legs shook. The way I've laid it, you can hold on and coast all the way around to here. It carries you like a slide.” She indicated the start at the gap and the place where the far end dipped into the lot. “Once you ride a ribbon, it wears away behind you. I've been keeping this one coiled up.”

“You want me to go on it?”

“At the permit office you said you couldn't wait to be able to go somewhere. I thought you'd like to see the first place I could.” She was looking at the far end now. “We can just look at it from here, if you like. Then I can wind it back up.”

The enormous loop was the only bit of the ribbon that didn't look made for getting anywhere. It went up out of the course and doubled back on itself like the swirl on a soft ice cream. “What's that bit for?”

“That's where you see the roofs upside down. At the very top you get a second where it looks like the whole town might fall off the hill.” She drew her chin back, the way she did when she wanted to keep a smile in. “I added that when I got better.”

“Why'd you stop?”

Mom ran her thumb back and forth over the edge of the loop in her hand. “Those three notes at the start of the tram film? That was the sound the dispatch box made. By the end I heard it when the microwave went off.” She picked a speck off the satin at her wrist. “I handed in my box. On the way home I came up here thinking I'd take one last ride. I wound it up again without stepping on. Then I went out for a pizza.”

“By yourself?”

“I got mushrooms. The whole thing.”

She had her face turned into the wind. Her free hand made a little turning motion. I could practically see her going through it.

“Come with me,” I said.

She looked at the single hand loop. “This is built for one.”

“I want to see you do it. I mean from up there.” In the tram film I could never make out her face.

Mom looked out at the big loop. Then she took her hand out of the little one and worked her thumb under a knot just above it. She stood that way a long time with her thumb still and the whole pink course shining. When she tugged, the far loop came open. A curve I could have fit half the town inside slipped out of the sky and came running through her hands. She caught the spare length against her hip. It was only cloth again. She doubled it, made a second hand loop, and tied it beside the first.

“Now we can both hold on,” she said. “I'll have to make the turn home while we're coming down. I can show you how I did the tram.”

I looked over the wall. “How far did that drop?”

“Far enough. Ready?”

I slid my hand in. For a second the ribbon felt like any piece of ribbon pulled tight across my palm. Then Mom leaned forward and there was weight in it. We stepped off together.

The ribbon took us hard around the first bend. I felt it pull at my shoulder, then felt my feet go light. The car and the wall swung out of sight. Below us, a man in a white shirt had stopped halfway across the road to look up. He had a square bag of groceries in each hand. In the time it took me to see what he was looking at, we'd gone over him and I was looking into back gardens I'd never seen, a green bicycle on its side, red sheets on a line. Mom laughed into the wind. I could hear it in little pieces.

Behind us the ribbon had begun to break into threads. They hung in the air for a second and went dark. Ahead, at the bottom of the big drop, the strip narrowed to a pink crease. We were going down so fast I couldn't get a breath to call to her. Mom stuck a white boot out. She brought her fist into her hip and I thought, *She's going to catch us there.* I got my feet out in front of me too.

She hauled up on the ribbon. The crease spread into a broad, deep U. I felt the pull in my knees as we went through the bottom of it. Two streets that had been coming right at us swept underneath instead. Mom turned her wrist, drawing the rise around toward the hill. Our speed carried us up until I could see the car again, over the wall and a little to the left.

“Straight in from here,” she called. She had to catch her breath. “Or do you want to turn over once? Give me your loop for that.”

It was warm where the loop went around my hand. I could have kept it. The wall was right there. Mom put her free arm around my waist and brought the hand with her loop next to mine. I leaned my weight against her arm and let go on her count. For an instant I was holding on to nothing. Then she had both loops in one fist. She rolled her wrist and the ribbon turned with it like the flap of a page.

All the roofs hung above my head with the late sun in the spaces between them. A tram came out onto the span, its windows lit even at that hour. It looked like it could let go of the hill and sail away. I held my breath and waited for it to go. Mom gave me just that long. Then she leaned and put the hill back under us.

My shoes came down on the tar and took three big running steps. Mom landed beside me. Her boots had gone back to the sneakers she wore for driving, and a short strip of pink came away in her open hand. It lost its color from one end to the other and was gone. Mom kept her hand out a moment after. Then she looked at me, panting, and grinned so hard I could see the silver in the back of her mouth.

I took out the keys. I was going to offer them to her, but she'd gone around to the passenger door already. I got us out of the lot and onto the first bend. Mom put both hands in her lap.

“Did you laugh when you caught the tram, too?” I asked.

“Oh, no,” she said. “I was the one screaming.”
