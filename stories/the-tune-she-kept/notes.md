# Editorial notes

## 2026-10-11 — Starting direction

The writer chose a fiddler and mother whose joyous Fae night costs two hundred mortal years. An old recording lets her hear the little daughter she remembers as a fully adult woman, with musical choices and a life she never witnessed. The coordinator asked what the living singer wants independently, what staying costs beyond a symbolic refusal of the Fae, and how the ending can remain an encounter rather than make archival work cure grief. The writer owns the provisional direction and prose. No supplied originals; no shared-universe additions proposed.

## Outline first readings

Codex: "The strongest possibility to protect is the recording as an encounter with Agnes’s independent artistry." Its central risk was that "her transformation [becomes] something the story supplies to her"; it asked what Dinah actively pursues and what that pursuit threatens with the living musician. It also warned that "an old Agnes laughing, having children, and exercising preferences may settle Dinah’s fear too comfortably" and that the final exchange needs a consequential adjustment. The reader preferred unsettled guilt but distinguished that preference from the causal need.

Claude: "Readers come for the vertigo of the return, but the stories that stay with them make the night worth something." It asked why Dinah leaves Faerie, how she finds Agnes’s married name and recording, and whether the invitation remains demonstrably tempting. "If Faerie is just a memory by the climax, refusing it costs nothing." It warned that food, bed, and archive logistics could crowd out the recording in 3,000 words. These observations preceded exchange and do not assert that unwritten scenes have already failed.

## Outline exchange and judgment

Both readers received each other’s words and the writer’s response. Codex challenged Claude’s claim of an already demonstrated agency failure: "The risk is cumulative passivity, not an absence of agency by definition." Claude accepted that correction. The writer proposed a voluntary departure to keep a promise to Agnes, an instrument shop reached through recognizable fiddles, Dinah pressing for the search and repeated listening, a still-usable Fae invitation, and one specific arrangement for tomorrow rather than a replacement family. The writer rejected a fiddle sale as compulsory penance and an invented sunset deadline; both readers accepted testing the revised causal direction in prose. Codex warned against staging Penny as another version of Agnes to teach the same lesson. Claude asked what only tomorrow can offer, and what Penny wants beyond appraising an antique. The coordinator treats those as live scene questions rather than prescribing a grand obligation: lost time need not become morally deserved to make guilt and grief real. The early outline is preserved in Git; its update leaves the concrete exchange to drafting.

## Cold draft first readings

The 3,725-word first draft was committed before review. Codex experienced "discovering that a lost child became a person her mother cannot recover or fully know." It protected the unavailable adult voice, the interrupted harvest song, Penny’s "I heard," the viable temptation, and the final missed leap. Its most consequential hesitation was the "speed and completeness of the historical discovery": at "I’ve heard this" it noticed "the story arranging its means of consolation." It suggested resistance or a clearer bridge without a research adventure. The Fae man’s question about telling Agnes read as possible incomprehension or cruelty, an ambiguity that did not break the scene.

Claude called the recording "the story’s best and most important passage" and found the final learner’s role earned. It disliked the return’s familiar inventory of a horseless vehicle, a flat object, and a request for a phone number: "Everywhere else, strangeness comes from precise, slightly wrong details." Its largest discussion question was whether keeping the bright string softened commitment; it read the open door as intentional and wanted to protect it unless another reader experienced only postponement. Minor hesitations were the referent of "Both of you?" and the earlier lower-phrase echo. Both complete first observations were preserved before sharing.

## Draft exchange and local repair

The writer accepted the cumulative search convenience and proposed failed name hits followed by Penny recognizing Dinah’s music and directing the local-history search. Both readers tested the proposal. Claude advised recognition of only the opening, preserving the later full performance as a defensive attempt to make Agnes’s version wrong. Codex likewise distinguished playing to occupy the hands from playing to invalidate the recording. Both asked that false leads remain brief. The writer tightened the arrival around a passenger inside the moving bus and retained the bread man’s attempted contact. Codex defended Dinah’s recitation of relatives as "a specific loss"; Claude accepted that correction, also correcting its bus paraphrase and overconfident interpretation of Agnes’s refusal. The coordinator protected the relative names, the incomplete biography, and the lower phrase without added explanation. "Both of you?" becomes "You and the fiddler?"

The writer and Codex rejected making the string’s survival a defect. Codex: "Cutting the string would introduce an absolute renunciation the story has not earned." Claude agreed after exchange. The coordinator accepts the limited "Not now": Dinah can choose a difficult mortal evening while retaining the pleasure that tempted her. Neither striking the Fae man nor explaining Agnes’s refusal would repair a demonstrated problem. The Fae man’s incomprehension remains uncomfortable by design. Penny’s finite, generous help remains; her work, irritation, and independent enjoyment of the dancers give that kindness a human context.

## Returning whole-story reading

Both returning readers reread the complete revision. Codex found the causal chain repaired: "Initially Dinah plays to occupy her hands; afterward she plays to establish authority over what she has heard." Claude agreed that the search "now causes its own discovery" and that the partial opening preserves the later difference. Both singled out the new waiting sentence, "When the answer came, I was at the place where Agnes used to stick her foot out for the second stocking." Claude found a local aftereffect: the retained old title reveal and explanatory dialogue announce the discovery repeatedly. Codex read "You’ve heard her?" as an earned emotional recognition; the coordinator accepts that reading but judges the adjacent announcements redundant, and asked the writer to tighten this exchange before fresh final reads. The lower phrase stays unchanged because its immediate musical function is legible; no added explanation was agreed.

Claude reread the tightened reveal in complete-story context and found each line follows the participants’ knowledge; the counter grip carries the emotional recognition. No new uptake problem appeared.

## Fresh final first readings

New Codex and Claude readers received only prompt and current whole prose, with no outline or earlier discussion. Codex cared about "whether Dinah could hear Agnes without forcing the old woman back into the seven-year-old she remembers." It found the search persuasive, the time scale coherent, and the final missed leap particularly effective. Its narrow material hesitation was the Fae man’s "Did you tell her?": "I stopped here to decide whether he was knowingly tormenting Dinah, incapable of understanding mortal lifetimes, or still failing to grasp what she had said." Those possibilities changed the offered hand’s emotional meaning for this reader.

Claude experienced the missing centuries as "revision, not as graves." It protected the recording and final apprenticeship, but found "Not now" initially read as stalling because all immediate interiority at the doorway pulled toward Faerie. "A reader can reconstruct the logic, but they reconstruct it after the fact." It proposed Agnes’s added phrase intruding at the doorway. It found the man’s question excellent uptake of the promised story, a direct difference from Codex’s effect. Minor concerns were the known/never-heard tune wording, changing designations for the Fae bride, and "my world"; the observer framed several other reservations as taste. These first effects are preserved before exchange.

## Final round-robin, repair, and readiness

The writer, fresh Codex reader and same fresh Claude session all received and answered one another’s actual findings. Codex distinguished preserving mortal life from already knowing how to inhabit it: "The Crown then discovers an affirmative possibility rather than supplying a missing explanation retrospectively." Claude accepted this correction to its initial diagnosis. The writer first considered recalling Agnes’s three notes, then changed the proposal after Codex’s challenge: remembering the interrupted elderly breath would preserve the Crown’s discovery. The resulting addition after "I finished the phrase. She let it end" is "Agnes’s breath was still in my ears. The song had been about to start." Both fresh readers checked their effects in whole-story context. Codex found the groom’s intent no longer obstructed Dinah’s choice; Claude found a visible counter-pull, and accepted the second sentence because it identifies the harvest song. The Fae phrase ends by consent; Agnes’s song is cut off by the cylinder. Neither is the later added leap Dinah can learn.

The coordinator declines a ruling on the groom’s cruelty or ignorance. His warm invitation and damaging incomprehension can remain unsettling once Dinah’s own attention is legible. The writer declined explaining the first known/never-heard tune, trimming the strained further search, or standardizing bride/fiddler/wife: immediate musical action, repeated listening, and explicit marriage identification make those choices functional. "In my world" became "Here," with an explicit Dinah dialogue tag after Claude caught a possible attribution ambiguity. The coordinator reread this mechanical clarification. These are reasoned decisions rather than a vote on readiness.

The current prose is ready because the Fae night has a felt attraction, Dinah causes the discovery through her own playing, the old Agnes retains an unknowable adult life, and the choice of mortal time now has immediate resistance as well as an enacted future. The prompt’s single night and two hundred years are explicit. Dates hold (1819 birth, seven in 1826, eighty-five in June 1904, 1907 death, 2026 return); the May 17, 2026 weekday is correct. The string, cake, fiddle, pathway and repeated recording remain continuous. Agnes Wold is a different established identity; Agnes Bell/Rusk’s family, dates and locality distinguish her. No shared-universe fact or new canon is asserted.
