# The Tune She Kept

Provisional direction: a standalone story in close first person, about 3,000 words, beginning with General as the audience target. In 1826, Dinah Bell, a widowed fiddler with a seven-year-old daughter, Agnes, follows music through an opening in the woods. She means to be back before her daughter wakes. The Fae Realm gives her a night of astonishing music, food, awkwardness, laughter, and the pleasure of being wanted for her playing. She does not knowingly choose two hundred years over Agnes. The joy must remain real after its cost is known.

Dinah returns in 2026 with her fiddle and a means of finding the Fae musicians again. The village has become a town. Trying to find her house and establish what happened, she comes into contact with a present-day musician, initially through a practical exchange involving her old instrument rather than an unexplained willingness to believe a time traveller. This musician has her own appetite for the music and her own limits on time and help; she is not a descendant appointed to forgive Dinah. Their friction and particular arrangement will be discovered in the scenes.

An early sound recording survives of Agnes Bell under her married name, collected when she was in her eighties around 1904. It is a performance, not a message intended for her mother: a difficult tune Agnes has changed, an interruption by the collector, laughter, and an adult choice about what she will and will not sing. Dinah knows a child's voice and expects either accusation or proof of endless love. What she hears is an old stranger who has had children, preferences, resentments, and pleasure. The original little tune Dinah taught her has survived inside music Agnes made her own. This encounter should unsettle the comforting legend of a daughter waiting all her life, without conveniently proving that she was unharmed.

The invitation to resume the Fae night offers Dinah somewhere immediately recognizable and a pleasure the mortal world can no longer give her. Remaining entails actual arrangements for food and a bed, asking something specific of a living person, and letting that person refuse or bargain. A possible decisive cost is parting with the valuable fiddle instead of carrying it back to the dance, but the prose should reject this if it makes her choice a purchased moral cure. The ending will occur within a live musical exchange, where Dinah cannot command the familiar tune or the other player's response. She can neither cancel her wonderful night nor borrow Agnes's whole life as absolution. She can enter the next mortal day and risk an attachment she will have to keep in time.
