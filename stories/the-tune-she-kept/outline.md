# The Tune She Kept

Provisional direction: a standalone story in close first person, roughly 3,000 words or the room the scenes need, beginning with General as the audience target. In 1826, Dinah Bell, a widowed fiddler with a seven-year-old daughter, Agnes, follows music through an opening in the woods. Agnes is safe with a known caregiver while Dinah is out; Dinah means to keep an ordinary promise to her when she returns. The Fae Realm gives her a night of astonishing music, food, awkwardness, laughter, and the pleasure of being wanted for her playing. Dinah voluntarily leaves because she wants to keep that promise. She does not knowingly choose two hundred years over Agnes. The joy must remain real after its cost is known.

Dinah returns in 2026 with her fiddle and a usable invitation to find the Fae musicians again. The village has become a town. She enters an instrument shop because its hanging fiddles are something she recognizes. The present-day musician initially treats her instrument as an antique appraisal. Dinah presses for information about Agnes Bell; the musician's familiarity with local tune holdings and a search under the maiden name lead to Agnes's married name and an early recording. The musician has her own appetite for the music and limits on time and help; she is not a descendant appointed to forgive Dinah.

The recording was collected when Agnes was in her eighties around 1904. It is a performance, not a message intended for her mother: a difficult tune Agnes has changed, an interruption by the collector, laughter, and an adult choice about what she will and will not sing. Dinah knows a child's voice and expects either accusation or proof of endless love. What she hears is an old stranger who has had children, preferences, resentments, and pleasure. The original little tune Dinah taught her has survived inside music Agnes made her own. Dinah drives the listening, asking for repetitions, wanting her childhood version endorsed, and pressing for another recording that does not exist. The sound stops before it can answer what her absence did or whether Agnes forgave. This encounter should unsettle the comforting legend of a daughter waiting all her life, without conveniently proving that she was unharmed.

The invitation remains viable and tempting after the return, with the Fae musicians still at the same point in their night. They cannot restore the morning she meant. There is no arbitrary deadline and no fiddle sale as penance. Remaining entails actual arrangements for food and a bed, asking something specific of a living person, and letting that person refuse or bargain. The ending will occur within a live musical exchange with real friction, and earn only a specific next-day arrangement rather than a replacement family. Dinah can neither cancel her wonderful night nor borrow Agnes's whole life as absolution. She can enter the next mortal day and risk an attachment she will have to keep in time. The precise exchange and choice remain for the prose to discover.
