# Provisional direction: The Wrong Sort

The explicit promise is a royal heir who rejects the life her inherited wing markings prescribe, tattoos them, wears cold iron, joins magical outcasts, and inadvertently changes the ancient laws themselves. My interpretation is an exuberant, dangerous fantasy of personal self-expression acquiring public force: punk pleasure, chosen company, consequential enchantment, and the exhilaration of breaking a system that has treated bodies as instructions. The scale begins with a band and reaches the whole realm. The central bond is the heir's friendship with the outcast who tattooed her. The anticipated payoff is both a visible release from hereditary compulsion and her continued life with the band; she does not become a kinder version of the monarch she refused to be.

Close third person through **Veska**, with a sharp, amused voice that can admit fear. The supplied original informs her punk confidence, dark hair with violet streaks, large purple-and-gold moth-like wings, iron jewelry, and a moonlit harvest gathering. **Nim**, a mixed-lineage fairy tattooist and fiddler, and **Hox**, a copper-winged drummer, are lovers forbidden to marry. Their band, the Wrong Sort, has desires, work, jokes, and an audience before Veska joins. **Regent Orset** controls the court and its guards while preparing the marked heir's public accession. No existing named people or places are reused.

- Begin in the pumpkin-lit field outside the city where the Wrong Sort are playing a forbidden show. Veska's black briar tattoos cross the elaborate gold lattice on her wings. The tattooing and iron piercings were her chosen refusal, undertaken before anyone knew alteration could change law. Her cold iron prevents the court's summoning glamour from reaching her; it does not insulate her from her friends or cancel every kind of magic. Show the band enjoying their new member, not merely sheltering a princess.
- The royal lattice includes a closed double loop that every fairy recognizes as the restriction on marriage between wing-lines. A briar thorn has crossed and opened it. During a raid, Nim and Hox expect the ancient marriage ward to tear their joined hands apart, as it always has; instead the ward opens for them. Distant marriage bells answer. The apparent picture of the law is a living source of it. Neither the court nor the outcasts expected this.
- Test that discovery with one deliberate, legible alteration rather than a string of random wishes: crossing the low-born border in another small pattern lets Hox enter a place his copper wings previously barred. Veska knows the designs because the court taught her to read them as immutable descriptions. Tattoos can join, redirect, or interrupt that existing grammar; they cannot grant any convenient unrelated wish. The knowledge transforms her possibilities from personal flight to a way to free other people.
- Orset uses the surviving royal law to counter them. The crown's summons can still compel ordinary subjects, including Hox, into custody; Veska's rings protect only their wearer. He offers real safety and recognized status to the whole band as Veska's royal household if she submits to the sacred restoration before her accession. Taking that bargain could protect her friends immediately, but would place them under her household's permanent obligations. Nim refuses to exchange exile for being the princess's property. Veska must stop assuming that what she can give is what someone else wants.
- The band accepts entry to the public accession as apparent royal musicians, with a plan they have made together. Orset expects Veska's displayed wings to renew the old order. She intends to redirect its central pattern, where every bloodline's path converges on the one sovereign. Establish the grammar and the possibility beforehand: a line turned back to its own circle ceases to reach the crown. The band's noise, Nim's needle, Hox's action under the summons, and Veska's deliberate resistance must each matter when Orset tries to stop them.
- The final tattoo returns each bloodline's line to its own bearer and severs the central crown's exclusive authority. Make its immediate consequence large and physical: compelled knees straighten, forbidden partners reach one another, and guards must decide whether to act without the ancient force behind their orders. The royal convergence fades from Veska's wings, so she cannot reverse the release or bind the realm with another lone edit. Ordinary conflict and prejudice survive, but the inherited compulsion and her monopoly do not. End in the band's continuing company and sound, with the new freedom already being used by other people, rather than a speech that settles the realm.

This is provisional. The greatest risks to test are convenient glyph magic, a regent who yields too easily, treating outcasts as an audience for royal generosity, and a final benevolent decree that leaves solitary sovereignty intact. The prose may find a better route while preserving the request's force.
