# The Other Hand

Provisional direction, open to challenge. First-person prose, probably about 3,500 words, centred on Oona, a working puppeteer whose younger brother Anton is missing. An unexplained worldwide event happened at one instant, before sunrise where she lives. Within its first twenty-four hours 1.3 billion people were dead or missing; eventually forty per cent of the population are identified as having powers. The prose omits the calendar date. The setting is a modern-looking undated city, without a proposed link to an existing universe system.

Oona wakes to a puppet walking without its strings. She also sees the scale of the disaster: a neighbour goes through a stair tread, fire comes from someone trying to help, flight is easier to start than stop. These consequences stay non-graphic. Her apparent telekinesis seems small and absurd beside them, but it is troublesome rather than obedient. It keeps dragging things toward her and catching them with the wrong grip. She can oppose its pull, which only makes it pull harder. Anton's flatmate reports him missing from a room whose door was still locked. Oona cannot find him through ordinary calls and searches. The theatre becomes a place for displaced people to sleep, so the puppet stage and the catastrophe remain physically together.

She initially treats the power as a new limb she must master. It uses a left-handed grip despite her being right-handed. When it lifts the damaged fox puppet, it first braces the loose jaw and then rolls its head in the precise ungainly way Anton used after an injury to his left thumb. This is a clue, not proof. It could be a memory of him inside her. They grew up performing together; he later left the family show, and their last argument concerned her treating his adult work and choices as a temporary detour. The fox mattered to them both, but it cannot supply the whole man he has become.

Oona tries a conscious exchange instead of another feat of control. At first she makes a revealing mistake: asking yes/no questions about their childhood gets reassuring answers she herself knows. A sceptical displaced woman, Renske, points out that grief could conduct both sides of that performance. Oona lets the unseen force move a pencil, then asks for information she does not possess. The reply asks after Basil. Oona assumes this is a person; Anton's flatmate, reached without first being told the reply, names the dog Anton adopted after Oona last visited. A mundane photograph and a specific present-day habit corroborate the unfamiliar name. Further replies are fragmentary but responsive, including a correction Oona dislikes. They can test a conscious connection; they cannot yet establish how it exists, what brought it about, or whether it will hold.

The live danger is another displaced person's violent, recurring power surge, with an apparent pattern of recoil that Oona has been treating as a tantrum. Her discovery changes what she tries: offer a way to answer and negotiate with whatever is making that movement, rather than overpower it. The other case must remain distinct from Anton and must not conveniently solve itself as another loved relative. An intelligible pause or answer supports a second agent somewhere, while leaving the person's identity and the channel's mechanism uncertain. Her new approach averts a local injury; it does not control every power or make her a disaster saviour.

The proportion of empowered people exceeds the dead and missing by far. Oona explicitly resists concluding that each gift is one missing person, or that everybody missing survived. Known deaths remain deaths. At most her evidence suggests that some seemingly individual powers have another participant, that at least one missing person is alive enough to answer, and that violent resistance may make contact dangerous. She shares the unedited exchange and its limits so others can test it, despite the risk of false hope and the temptation to keep Anton private.

A possible ending returns to the fox, now functioning as an expression rather than a recovered childhood prop. Anton asks whether it is light where she is. He reports darkness, other voices, and no location he can give her. Oona answers honestly. Their contact has not brought him home or settled their last argument. He asks her to keep the light on. She leaves space for his next movement. The ending's physical action and exact reply may change in drafting; the intended arrival is a person making a request, not a theory explaining him away or a promise that rescues the billion.
