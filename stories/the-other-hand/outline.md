# The Sky We Broke

New provisional ensemble direction, superseding the single-narrator interception. Close third person moves between five independent regions under one elapsed clock. All scenes, including the ending, occur inside the first twenty-four hours. A retrospective narrator may name the day's totals without adding later action: seven billion people at the start, 2.8 billion developing powers, 1.3 billion dead or missing. No date. The worldwide birth is the generating force; nobody becomes its sole decoder.

The airborne strand begins before dawn over a western sea. A mother teleports onto a falling wing while her daughter involuntarily gathers the burning aircraft around herself. An invulnerable passenger falls intact and cannot fly. A projected figure beckons people toward somewhere its owner cannot physically reach; invisible passengers still need saving; someone comes back after being declared dead. Mother discovers release-before-passage by failing to jump while daughter holds her with force. She wants her daughter and as many passengers as she can reach on ground. Daughter discovers the exhilaration of suspending her own weight and wants to carry survivors herself. Their rescue opens onto a place that is not below their aircraft. This strand contributes the lived release/passage clue and people who can cross without treating a surface as an obstacle.

In a southern night city, an ordinary train driver has a moving train full of people when its track reappears above a different city. She wants to get those passengers out, not become a leader. Shapeshifting changes who fits through a gap; luck gets one person through a lethal accident while somebody nearby is not spared; a stranger's spoken words become solid objects that block the carriage. A child can make falling run sideways. These discoveries alter her actual route. The driver initially tries to keep the old destinations and sends a returning mass toward the ocean strand. She learns that an apparently successful local escape can displace its danger elsewhere. Her eventual irreducible action is to move a large ordinary population onto changed geography without pretending everyone has a gift.

At midday on a northern ocean, a technopath can feel her ship as an extension of her body. She wants to launch it herself, dismissing the ordinary captain's warnings, and enjoys being able to wake dead engines. Her new control is not a world network: physical contact and particular machines matter. The falling mass from the southern city breaks the departure she thought she commanded. Her choice to hold position instead of flee becomes a loss of her own first voyage, but the ship can provide a moving receiving surface for people elsewhere. Other gifts are independently reshaping the port: water moves upward around a levitating worker; a person leaves a slow-time wake; transmutation turns a seized cargo into a weapon before anybody can classify it. This strand discovers that motion and received mass survive a crossing, and supplies a receiver which another strand cannot conjure.

In an eastern megacity, a young man dies in the first stampede and rises again. The resurrection is his, not a uniform answer to everyone's deaths. Around him, bodies change shape, projections walk through crowds, minds are exposed, people fly, and others remain ordinary. He is seduced by becoming someone nobody can permanently punish. He gathers followers and claims a newly appeared spatial edge as the border of a territory he can own. His appropriation, not a rescue attempt, creates the harmful decision other strands must confront: he blocks incoming people and tries to make the edge reflect force outward. The apparent local defence returns an ordinary explosion into another region. He has to discover that his own survival doesn't protect a city, and choose between maintaining his new authority and releasing the constriction. He remains a consequential viewpoint through that choice, not a villain glimpsed on a screen.

Above a western desert, a woman who can fly pursues the first pure joy of leaving the ground. A clairvoyant beside her glimpses conflicting outcomes but cannot supply a certain itinerary; an astral projection travels where its body cannot survive; an invisible raider exploits abandoned vehicles. The flyer wants the horizon more than duty. Her exploration brings her into the returning explosion from the eastern edge and makes her abandon a private ascent. She discovers through physical approach that two remote edges share a returning path, not merely matching images. Her contribution is to find and carry a route between them under conditions nobody elsewhere can observe. Neither clairvoyance nor luck quietly solves the climax.

The middle hours interleave consequences rather than summarise worldwide news. Material, messages and people cross with delay, error and disagreement. Each region changes another's situation before they understand the connection. Powers unrelated to the spatial edges continue to disrupt, delight, enrich, exploit and rescue; 2.8 billion gifts do not share a universal mechanism. A limited common inference emerges from several experiences: these particular edges are linked, force can return, and defending one side can crush another. That is not an explanation of why powers arrived.

The late-hours convergence is a dangerous decision to inhabit changed geography rather than restore every threatened place. The mother's release/passage, the driver's irreversible route choice, the technopath's moving receiver, the resurrected claimant's relinquishment, and the flyer's dangerous observation each make a different part possible. One must give up a locally safe route to spare another; one takes an unwanted moving mass; one stops an apparently successful defence. This is not a meeting, a single leader's theory speech, or five volunteers doing the same rescue. The connected crisis can include direct confrontation and large power collisions, with decisions forced while people are still pursuing private wants.

The final set piece and exact outcome remain open to prose discovery. Its scale must be physical: ships and districts at impossible heights, bodies flying and falling through linked skies, ordinary people moving alongside gifts they cannot possess, and several independent actions needed within the same narrowing window. By hour twenty-four, the new powered world is real and unfinished. Some people have returned from death; innumerable others have not, and the first-day loss remains. No later epilogue, tidy origin revelation, universal cost rule, recruitment ceremony or reassurance that humanity has solved itself.
