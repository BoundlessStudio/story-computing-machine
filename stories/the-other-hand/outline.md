# The Other Hand

Provisional direction, open to challenge. First-person prose, probably about 3,500 words, centred on Oona, a working puppeteer whose younger brother Anton is missing. An unexplained worldwide event happened at one instant, before sunrise where she lives. Within its first twenty-four hours 1.3 billion people were dead or missing; eventually forty per cent of the population are identified as having powers. The prose omits the calendar date. The setting is a modern-looking undated city, without a proposed link to an existing universe system.

Oona wakes to a puppet walking without its strings. She also sees the scale of the disaster through one closely observed neighbour's power and the damaged city around it, rather than an extended catalogue. The consequences stay non-graphic. Her apparent telekinesis seems small and absurd beside them, but it is troublesome rather than obedient. It keeps dragging things toward her and catching them with the wrong grip. She can oppose its pull, which only makes it pull harder. Darkness worsens its agitation. Anton's flatmate Silvie reports him missing from a room whose door was still locked. Oona cannot find him through ordinary calls and searches. The theatre becomes a place for displaced people to sleep, so the puppet stage and the catastrophe remain physically together. Introduce Harriet here: flame repeatedly appears in her hands, particularly when she shuts her eyes, and she is exhausted. Her body has needs before it becomes another case for Oona.

She initially treats the power as a new limb she must master. It uses a left-handed grip despite her being right-handed. When it lifts the damaged fox puppet, it first braces the loose jaw and then rolls its head in the precise ungainly way Anton used after an injury to his left thumb. This is a clue, not proof. It could be a memory of him inside her. They grew up performing together; he later left the family show, and their last argument concerned her treating his adult work and choices as a temporary detour. The fox mattered to them both, but it cannot supply the whole man he has become.

Oona tries a conscious exchange instead of another feat of control. At first she makes a revealing mistake: asking yes/no questions about their childhood gets reassuring answers she herself knows. A sceptical displaced woman, Renske, points out that grief could conduct both sides of that performance. Oona lets the unseen force move a pencil, then asks for information she does not possess. The reply asks after Basil. Oona assumes this is a person; Silvie, reached without first being told the reply, names the dog Anton adopted after Oona last visited. A mundane photograph and a specific present-day habit corroborate the unfamiliar name. Testing also shows that the participant can see what Oona sees and hear through her ears; it cannot read a card when she shuts her eyes. They can test a conscious connection; they cannot yet establish how it exists, what brought it about, or whether it will hold.

Once his identity becomes credible, Anton asks for Silvie. Oona wants her own reunion first and tries to draw him into the fox's familiar performance; he repeats the request instead. She puts Silvie on speaker and becomes the conduit for a conversation she cannot lead. The readers should see her lose her prized first reunion as a private return of the family-show brother. This does not settle the old argument. She necessarily overhears the conversation: she cannot stop being the conduit, but can keep what she carries out of circulation. Before showing any contact record beyond the room she asks Anton what may be shared. He permits the hand demonstration and verification, keeping his conversation with Silvie private. A willing participant is not automatically public evidence. This call is the intended emotional peak; the action complication afterward should be shorter, giving her discovery consequences without eclipsing the people.

A power failure makes Harriet's flames a live danger among the sleepers. Binding her hands has already hurt her without ending the fire. Oona's discovery changes what she tries, but politeness cannot resolve the incompatible needs: an unknown participant may be trying to see through Harriet's eyes, while Harriet needs to shut them and sleep. Oona uses a battery lamp so Harriet can look at simple written choices, and asks the participant to answer with a brief flame or its deliberate absence. She cannot borrow sibling trust or pretend to know its dark. The negotiation attempts a warned ten-second loss of sight with Harriet's agreement. A failed trial creates heat or a small burn, not an unearned clean success. Longer closures require agreement, practice, and someone beside Harriet ready to wake her if the fire returns. The bounded repair is safer rest, not guaranteed sleep or indefinite compulsory wakefulness. Oona cannot attend Anton's pencil and puppet while giving Harriet this sustained attention; she loses some of the reunion. The second case adds a bodily conflict and a tentative shared-sense limit, not a second convenient relative or another identity confirmation.

The proportion of empowered people exceeds the dead and missing by far. The limited contact record and witnessed deaths should carry most of that uncertainty rather than a paragraph of narrated caveats. At most Oona's evidence suggests that some seemingly individual powers have another participant, that at least one missing person can answer, and that closing a host's senses can distress a participant. It does not explain every power or save everyone missing. She shares what Anton authorised, with gaps and limits still visible.

A possible ending returns to the fox, now functioning as an expression rather than a recovered childhood prop. Anton can give no location for a search. His ordinary adult request is to see Basil, whom Silvie can show through a phone picture or call. The fox turns away from its scripted wave to look at the sleeping dog. Oona follows its attention instead of supplying a performance. Basil returns as a beloved animal rather than more evidence. Announced eye closures have become tolerable to Anton, though Harriet's unknown participant remains more frightened; the lamp and shared sight cannot impose indefinite wakefulness on either host. Following his earlier unwanted wish has already changed Oona's behaviour; the final kindness need not demand another imposed sacrifice. The exact gesture may change in drafting. The intended arrival is a person making a request, not a theory explaining him away or a promise that rescues the billion.
