# Opus review: Where No Unicorn Stands

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the physical and flight exchanges work; the argument and decision exchanges run on verdicts and word-corrections, and the climax's plan depends on knowledge the prince is never given.
- Prose: PASS — concrete prose that stays close to the body; only minor narrator-verdict fragments, one inverted "as if", and one irrigation-texture phrase.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is delivered within the length band; a few minor staging slips (tether, cup/bowl, captain's presence, message route).
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree — it passed an unsupported knowledge claim at the decisive offer, plus the verdict-line house voice in every argument scene.

This story's dialogue is better than the collection norm wherever a body is being handled: harness checks, flight commands, the triage at the platform, and the hut are grounded, uneven, and specific to each speaker. It breaks down whenever people argue or decide. The regent, the captain, and at times the harness-keeper fall into the house register: short verdicts, redefinitions of the other speaker's word ("That is not an order", "It is evidence", "I said what the mark means"), and parallel rebuttals that close exchanges on a shaped last word ("I carried you here because ours was already falling"). The moral hinges of the story (interrogation, planning, custody change, final exchange) are the places most affected. The edit pass should make those scenes sound like tired, frightened people on a dissolving floor. Where the story already shows an action, let that action answer instead of a line. Cut the verdicts. Add the one perception beat the climax needs. Leave the harness-keeper's material precision and the flight fragments alone.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Interrogation closes on a chain of verdicts

- Where: L89–L107, regent questions the captain beside the plunged prince
- Text:
> "That is not an order."
> "The council debated while the nursery floor thinned."
> "So you have brought me a crime and attached our survival to it."
> "I brought the one horn that can loosen a fresh royal claim."
> "You brought a prince who cannot stand in the room where you intend to bargain with him."
- Problem: The regent's two questions (order? consent?) are her own rhetoric and are fine. Everything around them is a negotiation/procedure routine. She corrects his answer ("That is not an order."), then issues a courtroom verdict, and the captain and regent trade a parallel "brought / brought / brought" chain that ends on a title-echo epigram. L105 is also Exposition: the regent already knows what the horn is for, since her council debated it. That line exists only to set up her rebuttal (Punchline Patch). Both speakers sound identical here, and the scene's real action (the regent turning away to order poles and food) gets upstaged by the speeches.
- Fix: Keep "Under whose order did you seize him?", the captain's need-answer at L93, "Did he consent to come?" and his "No." Cut L95, L103 and L105. If the captain answers again at L97, let it be less shaped: fatigue, a number, a place. Keep the concrete fact behind L107 (he cannot stand on their floor) but say it plainly, as practical exasperation, then go straight to the food order. Illustrative only: "He cannot stand on our floor, Captain." She turned to the Pegasi wrestling dismantled poles…

### D2 · Major · Regent's correction misstates what she asked

- Where: L111–L117, sling questioning
- Text:
> The regent asked questions as workers tightened the sling: when my mother had taken the crown, where the storm had been when she claimed it, whether her horn still touched the coronation stone.
> "My mother did not know you lived here."
> "I asked what she knew, not what you hope."
- Problem: Ground/uptake plus Punchline Patch. The narrated questions are when, where and whether. She never asked what the queen knew, so her reply misdescribes the conversation the prince just had, and he would notice. The prince's unprompted defense works as a character beat, but her answer exists to land a neat "knew / hope" correction.
- Fix: Replace L117 with something that follows from his actual claim in her question-asking mode, or cut it so "Four days… the root is still dividing" flows into the captain's plan. Illustrative only: "Who at your court opens letters that come down from the sky?" This also seeds the message-rack scene.

### D3 · Major · Planning ends in thesis and echo-reversal

- Where: L119–L131, sling, the captain's plan
- Text:
> He pointed with one wing toward the vanishing lower city. "Anywhere but fixed."
> "And drowns the valley under us." I had become very still in the sling. "You carried me here to choose which kingdom falls."
> The captain's answer came after one wingbeat too many. "I carried you here because ours was already falling."
- Problem: Three speakers pass one sentence along ("drops the stored rain" / "also saves us" / "And drowns the valley"), and then comes the Philosopher move. The prince states the story's dilemma as an abstract formula ("choose which kingdom falls"), and the captain turns his verb into a polished comeback (Punchline Patch). This is the moral crux, and both lines sound authored. The frightened prince talks like the captain, and the captain, who per the story cannot own guilt fluently, wins the exchange with a neat line. "Anywhere but fixed" is a smaller epigram of the same kind. His gesture at the vanishing city already says it.
- Fix: Keep the regent's "One cut drops the stored rain." (information the prince needs) and the captain's blunt "It also saves us." Let the prince react to the flood with what he has seen (the river lamps at L13, the orchards), not with a thesis about kingdoms. Keep the narration beat "after one wingbeat too many", but attach it to a plain, unshaped answer or to silence. Let the gesture at L123 carry "Anywhere but fixed", or replace the line with a place or direction. Illustrative only: "I know what is under us."

### D4 · Major · Message rack: distinction without difference and a lost "You said—"

- Where: L157, L173–L185, returned messages at the outer hatch
- Text:
> She tested the new knot with her full weight. "Some."
> "So she never saw them."
> "It means they did not reach your border riders. Keep your right hoof inside."
> "You said—"
> "I said what the mark means." She ducked under a beam. "Right hoof."
- Problem: Correction ping-pong at a key knowledge beat (did the queen know?). For these six bundles, "did not reach your border riders" and "she never saw them" amount to the same thing, so the harness-keeper's correction corrects nothing and exists to set up "I said what the mark means." The prince's "You said—" has no recoverable referent (Ground/referent). He may mean her earlier "Some", which implies other messages did not come back and might have reached the queen. That is the only reading that makes the exchange matter, but the reader cannot tell.
- Fix: Decide what the prince is reaching for and put it in his words. Let the harness-keeper's refusal come from her trade limit, not from scoring a precision point. Keep "Right hoof." as the interruption, since it is her true register. Illustrative only: "Some came back. Then the others—" / "I sort what comes back. Right hoof."

### D5 · Major · "It is evidence" contradicts the act it accompanies

- Where: L249–L251, court, the regent cuts the tether
- Text:
> "Regent," the captain said, "that tether is our claim."
> "It is evidence." She cut the braided rope tying my cradle to the court.
- Problem: Ground/figurative plus correction ping-pong at the decisive custody change. She redefines the captain's word, but "evidence" (of the crime she named at L103) is exactly what one keeps, and she immediately sends it into the whiteness. The reader has to argue with the line to recover what she means. The cut already answers "that tether is our claim". The line adds nothing and muddies it.
- Fix: Cut "It is evidence." Let her cut the rope without answering him, then "Carry him home." If she must answer, give her an order, not a redefinition.

### D6 · Blocking · The prince names the climax plan with no on-page access

- Where: L227 (storm shears) and L255–L261, court, the prince's offer
- Text:
> The storm began to shear after sunset. I knew it first through the cradle: every third wingbeat landed at a different angle.
> "There are three moving seams," I said louder. Fear made each word a separate object. "I can loosen them. Lightly. Not the root. Three releases, while we descend."
- Problem: Ground/knowledge at the decisive exchange. Nothing on the page gives the prince this. He has felt one crease (L189) and heard two seams named by Pegasi ("western", "upper"), and his only experience is one hard cut. Yet he states the count, that the seams move, and a technique the Pegasi who live in the storm never proposed. The listeners never register the gap: nobody asks how he knows or confirms it. The resolution therefore reads as author-supplied. It also breaks the story's own information limit, which lets him know ceremony, not cloud engineering.
- Fix: Add a brief perception beat before L261, best at L227 when the storm shears: as at L189, his horn picks up three separate pulls sliding through the storm. Then let a Pegasus with the knowledge take it up at L263, for example the captain naming or confirming the seams from his route knowledge before he objects. This turns the plan into something the prince senses and the Pegasi verify. Keep "Not the root." and the fragmented delivery.

### D7 · Major · Offer terms include a condition nobody can meet or answer

- Where: L263–L273, court, the prince's bounded offer
- Text:
> "You tried lightly once."
> I offer aid bounded by my consent: three seams, on a homeward course, if my mother breaks contact with the coronation stone before the last.
> "Homeward course," he said. "Rig him to me."
- Problem: The ceremonial fallback is earned. It is the prince's established pressure behavior and the narration marks it. The content is the problem. The condition about his mother is addressed to people who cannot fulfil it, gives no reason (the reader only guesses later that the last seam runs into the root), and gets no uptake. The captain accepts only "homeward course", so the clause that sets up the queen's decisive act hangs unanswered (Ground/uptake). "Aid bounded by my consent" also reads as the theme word the regent planted at L99, handed back as a formula. The captain's sarcastic "You tried lightly once." is real distrust and can stay.
- Fix: Keep the ceremonial cadence and the tether clause. Give the condition its reason in the prince's own terms (he will not cut his mother's claim, and the last seam meets it). Let a listener register that they cannot promise it, so the queen's unprompted release at L345 visibly matters. Optionally turn "bounded by my consent" into the concrete terms. Illustrative only: "We cannot make her lift it." / "Then I stop at two."

### D8 · Major · Final exchange: the queen explains the act the prince watched

- Where: L337–L345 and L381–L383, ridge, then shepherd's hut
- Text:
> She saw the captain carrying me.
> Only then did she turn away from me.
> "The stone?" I asked.
> "Cold," she said. "I released it before I knew you would reach us."
- Problem: Philosopher at the final meaningful exchange, with a timing clash. The prince watched her see him and then wrench her horn free, so "before I knew you would reach us" is either news to no one or, read plainly, contradicts what both of them saw. The strained reading is "before I knew you would land alive." The line is her only substantive speech, and it justifies her motive, which the story's own plan for her voice ("decisions already visible") rules out. "Cold" answers his question fully.
- Fix: Cut the second sentence, keeping "Cold," she said. If the edit pass wants the unconditional release to be unmistakable, do it in the staging at L337–L345 (she lifts her horn while his survival is still visibly in doubt), not in her mouth. Any replacement should be present-tense care, not motive. Illustrative only: "Cold," she said. "Hold still."

### D9 · Minor · Lore shorthand the reader cannot unpack

- Where: L69, L89, L113, L119, landing platform and sling
- Text:
> "The western seam is taking everyone," somebody answered.
> We need his horn at the binding.
> "Four days," she repeated. "Then the root is still dividing."
> A direct descendant can slacken the claim until it travels again.
- Problem: Ground/referent. "Taking everyone" could mean people are falling or that every worker is busy there, and "Then strip the canopy poles" only fits the second reading. "The binding" never recurs. "Still dividing" is never paid off. In "until it travels again", "it" could be the claim or the storm.
- Fix: Use the terms the story already teaches (root, claim, seam) and make "taking everyone" clearly about hands. Cut "dividing" or give it one concrete meaning the prince can see. Illustrative only: "Every pair of wings is on the western seam."

### D10 · Minor · Correction last words in small exchanges

- Where: L219–L221 (after the tear), L277–L283 (bearer before launch), L327–L331 (second seam)
- Text:
> "It is," the harness-keeper said. "For water."
> "That was not my question."
> "You weren't."
- Problem: Each small exchange closes on a precision correction, the collection's default rhythm. "It is. For water." also turns the aftermath of a near-death and a flooded field into a quip. The other two are "gotcha" last words.
- Fix: L221: give the fact without the twist, or let the captain's order cut across it. L281: cut it and let her hand over the emergency line as her answer to "If he slips?" L329–L331: cut "I am." / "You weren't."; "Keep that" plus the prince pressing harder is enough.

### D11 · Minor · "Don't cut!" is never resolved; said-bookism

- Where: L309–L317, second seam, snagged emergency line
- Text:
> "Don't cut!" the captain barked.
> I went rigid. Cut nothing. Move nothing. Be literal or kill a kingdom.
> "He said don't—"
- Problem: For "Hold" the story tells us what the captain meant (L305). Here it never says whether "Don't cut!" meant the line or the seam, so the literal-listening thread loses its clarity at its sharpest point. "Barked" is a said-bookism.
- Fix: Make the captain's object clear (in his words or in one narrated clause, as at L305). Use "shouted" or an action beat. See P1 for the triplet at L311.

### D12 · Minor · "Down" makes horns rise; unicorn bowstrings

- Where: L339–L345, exchange ridge
- Text:
> "Bows down," she called.
> My mother stepped between the armed line and the cliff. "All of them. Down."
> Horn tips rose from their aim. Bowstrings slackened.
- Problem: The verb does not fit the action. She orders "Down" and the horns go up. Bowstrings on a hoofed line (the prince undoes buckles with his teeth) also make the reader stop and work out how they draw.
- Fix: Use an order that fits both weapons ("Stand down" or "Horns up"), and either cut the bows or make them something hooves can work.

### D13 · Minor · "Route held?" gets a non-answer

- Where: L375–L377, hut
- Text:
> He swallowed. "Route held?"
> "Eat later," the harness-keeper told him. "Talk less now."
- Problem: Nobody has offered him food, so "Eat later" answers nothing, and the neat later/now pairing sounds composed. His question, the one thing he still owns, goes unanswered, even though the prince who rode the route is right there.
- Fix: Let someone answer briefly (the prince or the regent), or keep only "Talk less."

### D14 · Minor · Narration glosses lines the reader already understood

- Where: L133, L225, L297, L353, L395
- Text:
> The regent ordered him out before he could turn that into a defense.
> No praise. The route was already beneath his next instruction.
> It was not a command anymore. It was all the question he could carry.
> She meant the whole account. The six sealed bundles. The torn terrace. The cut tether and everything after.
- Problem: Each gloss explains the subtext right after the line ("Three?", "When you can") and leaves the reader nothing to infer. L225 ("For that much, at least, I was grateful") is the mildest and can stay if the others go.
- Fix: Trim to the action: "The regent ordered him out." Cut "No praise." and its explanation. Keep at most one of the two sentences after "Three?" Shorten L395 to a single clause, or drop the recap list.

- Text:

### P2 · Minor · Inverted "as if"

### P3 · Minor · Irrigation texture (policy)

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C2 · Minor · Message provenance and the route that got through
