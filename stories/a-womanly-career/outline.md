# A Womanly Career

*Provisional direction; the draft can find a better route.*

Lady Cressida Varn has finished an expensive officers' education and is expected to take the commission arranged for her. She wants instead to become a nursery teacher. She loves children's absorbed, peculiar attention and the work of helping them do things for themselves. In this culture that is patient men's work; a highborn woman should have the steadiness for heavy weapons. Her mother, an accomplished .90-calibre sniper, hears both a social disgrace and a daughter giving up reliable pay, regimental quarters, and a pension for a poorly paid apprenticeship. Grandmother, who served as a tank gunner and radio operator, is no automatic ally: she remembers the power of earning her own living. Their pride is affectionate, specific, and capable of hurting.

Close third person with Cressida, probably three scenes. At the family table the prompt's accusation lands among the ordinary details of this household. Cressida has investigated the school, made an application, and saved enough to live through the paid trial term; she cannot fairly claim that every practical objection is mere prejudice. The argument makes her choice concrete: turn down the commission and take the apprenticeship without the family's military connections smoothing her way. She may be good at shooting. Her vocation does not require that she be bad at it.

An ordinary trial morning at the nursery makes the calling desirable and difficult on its own terms. A male senior teacher has professional authority; Cressida is an apprentice who must take correction, rather than a gifted amateur instantly fixing the profession. Children will have particular wants and disagreeable edges, not arrive as a chorus validating her. A parent's difficult departure and an apparently insignificant activity can carry the scene; no emergency, no classroom run as a platoon, and no discovery that teaching is another weapon. Her family has not become irrelevant simply because the work is good.

A final encounter brings the family pressure back after she has made a real start. Mother need not announce approval of nursery teaching or concede the whole culture's error. The possible ending is her awkward, particular participation in Cressida's new life: she shows up to see where her daughter works and has to address the experienced male teacher as the authority, or makes space to listen to an unheroic account she cannot turn into a campaign anecdote. Cressida must also stop presenting every hour as proof that she chose correctly. Aim for a changed relation, a remaining embarrassment, and a comic physical image; avoid a family portrait or chore that neatly explains the theme. The exact closing action remains open until the scenes earn it.
