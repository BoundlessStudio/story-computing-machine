# A Crown of Quiet Hours

Water counted is fire denied.

That maxim was cut above every door in the municipal water office, stamped on
every ledger, and spoken by senior auditors whenever they wished to make a
junior clerk feel that arithmetic was a branch of heroism. I had always
believed it anyway.

On Founding Day, with forty thousand oil lamps waiting to be lit and a dry wind
combing the capital's roofs, the western cistern was short by nine hundred
thousand gallons.

I added the columns again.

Outside the inspection room, worshippers streamed toward the palace square in
white festival coats. Each carried a little clay lamp for the Crowned
Ancestors, the dead monarchs who had ended the border wars, built the
granaries, and required landlords to keep buckets on every stair. My mother
had survived a tenement fire because one of their cisterns had worked. She
still lit two lamps every year: one for the monarch who had ordered the works
and one for the inspector who had refused the builder's bribe.

I intended to become that sort of inspector. A permanent appointment waited on
my certification of the Founding Vigil.

The ledger offered me a simpler future. A strip of crimson wax crossed the
missing-water entry, impressed with the royal seal. Below it, in an immaculate
hand, someone had written: *Adjustment authorized. Correct total and certify.*

The chief auditor had already placed his pen beside the amended figure.

"The seal settles custody," he said. "Sign, Istra."

"The seal doesn't fill a hydrant."

His glance moved toward the square, where Vicar Estrel's assistants were
arranging lamps in forty-seven golden circles. "An assistant auditor does not
postpone the Founding Vigil because she dislikes a subtraction."

I dipped the sounding rod again. It struck stone far too soon.

Then I heard water moving.

Not in the municipal main. Beneath it.

The palace conduit ran behind a locked iron grille, humming with a strong,
steady current. An obsolete plaque beside it identified the pipe as part of the
royal emergency works. Above the grille hung the rope of the Flood Bell, gray
with dust. No clerk had authority to ring it. The bell belonged to siege,
earthquake, and royal command, in that order.

I put the unsigned certificate in my coat, took the ledger, and followed the
sound underground.

* * *

The palace sluice descended through three gates. The first admitted auditors.
The second admitted only auditors carrying royal business, which the wax seal
on the ledger served well enough. At the third, the air turned cold and wet,
and I began to suspect embezzlement on a scale requiring architecture.

I emerged above a cavern full of dragons.

There were eleven.

I counted because that was my profession, and because counting postponed
screaming.

One lay coiled in a nest of white sand, pale as old ivory, with a damp cloth
folded over its eyes. A green dragon was turning three times upon a bed of
river stones with the hopeless concentration of a dog that had been promised
comfort by a liar. Two copper-colored dragons argued in whispers about whether
the drip from the northern vault was soothing or intolerable. A narrow blue
one had pushed its entire head beneath the water. The largest, black-scaled and
scarred along one shoulder, was packing wool into its ears.

All around them, the stolen water gleamed in a new basin.

The dragons had rugs. They had pillows. They had shelves of ledgers. One had a
porcelain cup balanced between two claws.

Queen Orvessa IV stood on the ledge below me in her Founding Day robes.

"Assistant Auditor Noll," she said. "You are early."

I held up the municipal ledger. "The city is nine hundred thousand gallons
late."

Every draconic head turned. The black dragon removed one plug of wool.

Queen Orvessa closed her eyes. "I asked for a less conspicuous adjustment."

One copper dragon murmured, "You asked a water auditor not to notice water."

"I was tired."

"We are all tired."

The queen looked up at me. She had the face stamped on coins: mild eyes,
silver-threaded hair, the small rueful smile that painters had called maternal
for nineteen years. "Istra, I can explain."

"Start with the reptiles."

The mild eyes narrowed. Then the queen's outline shivered.

No crown or hidden engine performed the change. Flesh moved because she moved
it. Her robes drew tight, split at seams arranged for the purpose, and fell
away as her body lengthened down the steps. Silver hair flattened into a ridge
of dark red spines. The famous hands became claws; the maternal smile acquired
enough teeth to govern by itself. A red-gold dragon settled where the queen had
stood, one foreclaw still wearing the royal signet like a ridiculous ring.

"I am Kharost," the dragon said in Queen Orvessa's voice. "Queen Orvessa IV is
my present office."

I gripped the rail.

"You worship neither," Kharost added. "The Vigil is for the Crowned Ancestors,
who are deceased legal fictions. We were quite careful about that."

"How considerate."

"We thought so."

Behind Kharost, the ivory dragon lifted the cloth from one eye. "Is the bell
going to ring?"

"No," Kharost said.

"There are forty thousand lamps above us," I said, "and the hydrants have no
water."

The blue dragon surfaced. "That sounds like a reason to cancel the lamps."

"They worship your dynasty with them."

Kharost sighed, and warm smoke curled between her teeth. "Yes. That got away
from us."

She led me past the basin to a wall of portraits. Forty-seven rulers gazed down
in oils and mosaics: conquerors who had signed peace, reformers who had built
sewers, solemn children who had grown into solemn adults and died at
politically convenient ages.

"Masks," I said.

"Offices," Kharost corrected. "Rotated among twelve of us. Monarch, heir,
adviser, remote cousin, occasionally disagreeable foreign envoy. It is useful
to lose an argument to oneself in public."

"Two hundred and eighteen years of royal history."

"Two hundred and eighteen years without a city burned in war." Kharost looked
longingly toward the basin. "We require deep sleep. Cool stone, no vibration,
stable water, and—this has proved the difficult part—several centuries in which
no one sets the neighborhood on fire."

The black dragon pushed the wool back into its ear. "Meaningful sleep," it
said, "has become a theoretical condition."

* * *

Kharost did not ask me to trust her. She showed me records.

That was almost worse.

The royal archive filled the cavern's eastern wall. Original treaties bore
corrections in the same twelve hands across generations. Grain tallies showed
which reserves had been opened during lean winters. In a narrow volume from
the thirty-second reign, a proposed border campaign ended after the monarch
invited both generals to dinner and quietly melted their swords into the
table.

"A theatrical period," Kharost said.

I found the appropriation for my mother's cistern. The contractor's first
design had cut the reservoir in half. A royal examiner had rejected it and
sent two officials to prison.

The paper shook in my hands.

"The peace was real," Kharost said. "We did not fake full granaries or unburned
homes. We stopped six wars, three succession crises, and an astonishing number
of men who believed torches improved negotiations."

"You also taught the city that obedience was gratitude."

"We taught it not to burn."

"You let people kneel to people who never existed."

"The Crowned Ancestors existed as public work. They do not hear prayer. They
hold no present office. Orvessa does, and while she lives your doctrine
expressly forbids worshipping her." Kharost tapped the archive with one claw.
"We separated the functions."

"You designed a religion as a filing system?"

"It was supposed to be an annual speech."

I laughed then, once, because otherwise I might have thrown the appropriation
ledger into the basin.

Kharost unrolled a thick manuscript on a stone table. *The Book of Quiet* was
written across its cover.

"At dawn," she said, "our last measures would have taken effect. Tax bands,
reservoir rules, appointments, grain purchases, fire inspections. All
specified for the coming centuries. Human officers would administer them while
we slept."

I turned pages. The work was meticulous. It anticipated drought, population
growth, crop failure, corruption, and seven varieties of dispute concerning
goats. Corrections had been layered over corrections until some margins looked
furred with ink.

"Where do we change a rule that's wrong?" I asked.

"The rule will not be wrong."

The green dragon stopped circling its stones. Several of the others looked
away.

I laid my ledger open to the missing total. "This one was."

"For one night. The basin must settle to sleeping temperature. Once the Vigil
ends, the lamps are extinguished, the municipal cisterns refill, and we
withdraw. The palace crews have contingency carts."

"For a square packed with forty thousand people?"

"The calculated likelihood of a major ignition is less than one in eight
hundred."

"Is tonight's child the first or the eight hundredth?"

Kharost's pupils thinned. "Do not mistake a hard choice for indifference."

"Do not average people until they disappear."

For the first time, anger overtook her patience. Heat flashed through the room,
and the edge of the Book of Quiet browned beneath her claw.

Then she lifted it quickly. "That," she said, "was poorly illustrative."

The other dragons were silent.

Kharost folded the municipal ledger closed. "I cannot allow this out of the
palace before dawn."

"Then cancel the Vigil."

"A royal cancellation without visible cause would cause panic, investigation,
and noise. The whole point of tonight is that it is the last night."

"You stole fire water for pillows."

"Cooling beds."

"Pillows with plumbing."

She set the ledger on the table beside the Book of Quiet. "You may remain here
comfortably until morning."

Comfort was represented by a straight-backed chair between two sleeping
dragons.

While Kharost argued with the copper pair about who would watch me, I slipped
my original sounding sheets from beneath the royal archive copy and reclaimed
the municipal ledger from beside the Book of Quiet. The dragons had spent two
centuries studying human government. None had learned to count what an auditor
carried.

I climbed back toward the lamps.

* * *

By dusk, the square burned gold.

Thousands of worshippers stood shoulder to shoulder within the circles of
light. At the palace steps, Vicar Estrel wore the plain gray of a living
officiant; all the gold had been reserved for the Crowned Ancestors. Behind
him, Queen Orvessa's empty chair waited outside the ritual, because the living
monarch held authority but received no prayer.

I found Estrel beside the ceremonial fountain and showed him the sounding
sheets.

"The west cistern cannot serve the square," I said. "End the Vigil. Now."

He read the figures twice. His face changed, but his voice stayed level. "The
palace certified the adjustment."

"The palace diverted the water."

"To what?"

The honest answer would expose Kharost. The cautious answer would leave forty
thousand lamps burning.

"An unlawful private basin."

Estrel looked toward the crowd. People were already reciting the names of
forty-seven reigns, each response rolling across the square like surf.

"On an assistant auditor's accusation," he said, "I can delay one lamp circle.
If I halt the Founding Vigil against the royal seal, the crowd may press the
gates. Give me proof people can see."

"I am telling you the hydrants are empty."

"And I believe that you believe your figures. They believe this ceremony
remembers the peace that kept their grandparents alive."

A gust struck the square.

The lamps guttered. A row of canvas prayer streamers snapped hard enough to
break its upper line. The loose end dropped, swept across six flames, and rose
burning into the awning over the western arcade.

For one long breath, everyone watched.

Then the awning fell onto a vendor's roof.

Someone opened the ceremonial fountain. It coughed twice and spat mud.

I ran to the nearest hydrant, wrenched the cap loose, and heard the hollow rush
of air.

Above the first screams, Estrel shouted for calm.

The fire crossed to the next awning.

* * *

The Flood Bell rope hung behind the palace grille.

I reached it with smoke at my back and a palace guard in front of me. I held up
the royal-sealed ledger as if it still belonged to me and said, "Emergency
water order."

The guard hesitated just long enough to remember that people who forged royal
seals rarely wore municipal boots.

I ducked through.

The bell rope was stiff with age. Beside it stood the emergency sluice wheel,
painted red and chained in place. The chain yielded to the guard's axe on the
third blow. I set both hands on the wheel.

If I opened it, the new basin would drain into the civic mains. The prepared
stone would warm. The current would churn the sand nests and soak every pillow.
Two centuries of deception would rise after it in twelve furious bodies.

Behind me, burning cloth sailed over the crowd.

I turned the wheel.

Deep below, a gate groaned open. Water struck the old pipes so hard the floor
jumped.

Then I pulled the rope.

The loudest bell in the capital woke the dragons.

Its first note hit like a bronze wall. The second rolled over the square and
shook ash from the arcade. By the third, water hammered through the hydrant
mains.

People were running in every direction. Estrel climbed onto the dry fountain,
seized one of the tall ceremonial lamps, and held it above his head.

"Who remembers the first deliverance?" he called.

The old response came raggedly from the nearest worshippers. "We remember
together."

"Then together—clear the west lane! Pass empty buckets inward! Full buckets
out!"

He turned worship into instructions one line at a time.

"What do the faithful preserve?"

"The living!"

"Then pull down those streamers!"

The lamp circles broke apart. Their little lights, meant for dead royal masks,
marked bucket stations through the smoke. Hands passed water from the reopened
hydrants. Others dragged carts and canvas away before the fire could take
them. A row of bakers soaked the roofs with trough pans. Two worshippers used a
festival pole to tear down a blazing awning and nearly brought it onto
themselves; ten more seized the pole and guided it into the flooded gutter.

The water had returned, but pressure built slowly. Flame climbed the western
arcade and found the old beams beneath its tiles.

Queen Orvessa came through the palace doors barefoot, her silver hair loose.

"Noll," she said over the bell's fading roar, "you have destroyed conditions
that took nineteen months to establish."

"Your city is on fire."

"I can see that."

"Then ask for help."

She looked at the crowd, at Estrel commanding lines beneath the golden circles,
at the hydrants finally spilling water. "The queen can order them."

"That is the problem."

A burning roof beam cracked. People scattered below it.

I caught Queen Orvessa's hand before she could move. It was already too warm.
"Ask them in your own face."

Fear changed her more completely than flesh had. Not fear of the fire. Fear of
being seen.

Then Kharost tore through Queen Orvessa.

Red-gold scales swept across the palace steps. Her wings opened wide enough to
knock two banners down, and the crowd's panic struck as a physical force.
Buckets fell. People screamed. Estrel stared up at the living power that had
worn the throne, then at the golden circles dedicated to rulers who had never
been people at all.

Kharost lowered her head.

"We took your water," she said. Her voice carried without the bell. "We built
this peace because we wished to sleep in it. We lied about who ruled you. If
you will help save the square, we will answer for that when it is safe."

The cracked beam fell.

Kharost caught it across one shoulder. Fire washed over her scales, harmless
for an instant, then bright enough to make her recoil. Human hands pulled
trapped buckets and people clear while she heaved the beam into the fountain
basin. A scale split across her shoulder.

The palace doors burst outward again.

Eleven dragons emerged into the wreckage of public worship.

The ivory one came blinking, its damp eye cloth still snagged on a horn. The
green one wore half its river-stone bed clattering between its belly scales.
The narrow blue dragon streamed basin water across the steps. The black dragon
had forgotten to remove one wad of wool and kept turning its wrong ear toward
Estrel's orders. Copper, gray, dun, and mottled bodies filled the square until
every royal proportion seemed absurd.

They did not end the fire.

They bought us the time to do it.

Two braced the arcade while citizens stripped burning tiles from the roof.
One shielded a bucket line with its wings until the thin membranes smoked and
curled at the edges. The blue dragon carried water in its mouth, discovered
that this made shouted coordination impossible, and submitted with visible
offense to being used as a cistern. The black dragon dragged three blazing
carts into the open square, where human crews drowned them.

Kharost tried to climb toward the hottest roof.

"No," I shouted. "Hold the eastern beam."

She glanced at me, royal reflex warring with necessity.

"That is not a suggestion from a subject," I said. "It is an instruction from
the water line."

Kharost held the eastern beam.

Estrel raised his lamp. "What do we preserve?"

This time the answer came from the whole square, frightened and furious and
working.

"The living!"

By midnight, the fire had nowhere left to go.

* * *

The western arcade lost its roof. Thirty-one people were treated for smoke and
burns. No one died.

That last number was entered in my ledger without adjustment.

At the public inquiry, the square filled again, though nobody brought a votive
lamp. Kharost and the other dragons lay in the old parade ground under guard,
too large for the hearing chamber and too tired to object to weather.

Some citizens demanded the return of Queen Orvessa IV. Her reign had been
gentle, they said, and gentleness was rare enough without interrogating its
anatomy. Others wanted twelve execution platforms and had given no thought to
the engineering.

I testified between them.

I entered the uncensored water figures. I entered the royal archives, the war
records, the granary accounts, the prison orders, my mother's cistern, the
secret basin, and the Book of Quiet. The lie did not empty the granaries after
the fact. The saved lives did not make the lie consent.

"You kept the peace," I told Kharost. "You do not therefore own it."

She lowered her head until one gold eye met mine. "What will keep you from
burning it when we sleep?"

"Nothing."

The hearing erupted.

I waited.

"Nothing kept you from draining our cistern except your own judgment," I said
when the room quieted. "That failed. So ours must be allowed to fail in public,
where other people can correct it before the roof catches."

The Charter of Waking Hours took six weeks, nine hundred amendments, and one
incident in which the black dragon fell asleep during a tax debate and crushed
a decorative hedge.

It placed law, water, taxation, records, and appointments under elected human
councils. It opened royal archives and accounts to public audit. It ended state
worship of the Crowned Ancestors without forbidding private remembrance. It
retired the forty-seven masks, including Queen Orvessa IV, and recognized
Kharost and the other eleven as residents: protected by law, bound by law,
entitled to no throne, sacrifice, or uncounted gallon.

Kharost argued for a provision requiring all bells to be padded.

She lost.

We compromised on walls.

Seven months later, I inspected the cooling works beyond the inhabited wards.
Its reservoir had its own watershed, its own public ledger, twelve enormous
stone beds, and soundproofing thick enough that the dragons could not hear the
debate over its cost. Human engineers had designed shutoff gates accessible
from outside. Human inspectors had tested them. My permanent appointment came
with instructions to distrust every seal, including my own.

The twelve dragons settled one by one.

The ivory dragon placed the damp cloth over its eyes. The green dragon turned
three times upon its stones, reconsidered, and turned once more. The copper
pair resumed their argument about dripping water until Kharost opened one eye
and said, "Two centuries. Please."

At last only Kharost remained awake.

"Will it hold?" she asked me.

I looked at the gauges, the open ledger, the gate controls, and the human crew
waiting for my answer.

"Not by itself," I said.

Kharost considered that. Then she smiled with her own alarming teeth, tucked
her head beneath one red-gold wing, and slept.

For the first time in two hundred and eighteen years, nobody had been ordered
to be quiet.

We were anyway.
