The day I meant to ask Rilla to marry me again, I carried a printer's proof home on the tram as if the printer had buttered it. There was a crescent of blue on my thumb where I'd taken the sheet from him. A man in front of me asked if I'd mind lowering it so he could read out the dates to his wife. I had to put my chin right into the wet fringe of her umbrella to oblige him. I'd had Rilla's name made bigger this year. *The Lark house orchestra plays the arrangements of Rilla Toller.* My own name was at the foot of the bill, under *Lark Assembly Rooms, Fellcross*. I held it there a little longer to give the wife a chance at Rilla's name.

Last November Rilla signed our marriage contract lying down, with me holding it flat against a book, and we put *Band Room* on the line for where we could still see each other after she died. When she came back in December she sat in the good chair by the radiator as if I'd got up to make her tea five minutes earlier. Miss Hurst brought up two cups and Rilla took one out of her hand.

Our year was up at midnight. I was going to ask for the ballroom this time.

That Saturday, the first of November, Freda Searle came to the till at eleven with her dance shoes in one hand. The draught from the street went straight up my sleeve when I put out a hand to make change. Every week since Rilla died, Freda had found a way to offer me a dance. This time she set her shoes down and waited, her weight on the knee she could still turn on. Rilla had written two extra beats into “First Night on the Hill” for that knee.

“Will you feel up to it tonight, Ellis?” Freda asked.

“I'll let you know in time for the late set.” Come back at twelve, Freda, and ask my wife for yourself whether I ever could dance.

Miss Hurst came over from the coat rail. She'd worked the till last winter when I was bringing Rilla her meals in bed. I told her I'd come back for the late tickets at twelve. A man at the rail held up his cloakroom check. She put two fingers up to him. “Take your time, Mr. Galt,” she said, already reaching down for the second tray of change. I took the proof and the renewal upstairs.

On the landing I put my thumb against the brass knob in the place where the polish had worn away. I'd stood there the first time Rilla left the band room after she died. She pulled the door open from inside and stepped through. I watched the landing for her shoe, which ought to have followed the hand, and there was nothing on the boards but the light from downstairs. I put my own foot where hers would have fallen and felt the loose board give. When she was alive I could follow her into the next room, even if by the time I got there I'd forgotten what I meant to say.

Rilla was in the good chair tonight with her coat folded over her lap. The room smelled of dust off the radiator. The brass grille in the skirting rattled on the low notes from downstairs. I put the proof down and she took hold of my thumb, turned it over and rubbed at the printer's blue.

“Did you come all the way on the tram with that up in the air?” she asked.

“Most of the way.”

“I thought so. You've got rain in the neck of your shirt.” She reached behind my hip for the knot of the apron and put her other hand on the hem. I felt her find the penny that had worked into the seam. She always tried to get it out when she felt it. This time she gave it a rub between finger and thumb and left the apron on me.

The pianist downstairs got to the turn in “First Night on the Hill.” Rilla held up a finger. “He's going to cheat Freda of a beat. Listen.” The next chord came up through the grille a beat early. “If she makes it round, she'll see up somebody's nose.”

I'd heard Rilla's own tunes with her through that grille all year. I wanted to see her face with a whole room of people playing and dancing where she could put out a hand and reach them. I slid the proof over to her. She set her cup on the middle of her printed name and drew the contract free from under it.

“Where did you want us?” she asked.

“The ballroom. You can have the big table by the door where you get a draught. I'm always being asked how long it's going to be before I feel up to going out on my own floor. I'd like to take you over to Freda at the start of the late set and ask her to make room for my wife.”

Rilla lifted her cup far enough to read the name. “You've given me the good size.” She set it down again.

“I was already paying for blue. I could have put your name right across.” I ran my thumbnail down one of the edges. “I think they'll want to ask you for a few tunes. I'll tell the band to give you the whole evening off if you want it.”

“I'd like a ballroom,” she said.

I got the pencil from my apron and put the point to the room line. Rilla took a folded programme out of her coat and opened it right across the line, under my hand. *The Plover Rooms, Old Market. Saturday Late Set. Rilla Toller and her orchestra.* The folds were already going white.

“The Plover's in the town where I live now,” she said. “I want you to come across to me this year, Ellis. Put that room down.”

I put the pencil down and brought the programme closer to the lamp. Seven players' names followed hers. I read her name again. I would have reached for my spectacles if it had been any smaller, and I wished it had been. “I thought you played one or two numbers when people asked you.”

“At first I did. I went in for tea in February and there was a piano with two keys that wouldn't come back up. I played round them for a few weeks till Ned Spald fetched the man who used to tune it. When we got the two notes back I said I'd play a whole Saturday if he'd find me a drummer. By August we were taking money at the door, with Ned at the piano for the early set and me for the late one. There's a woman who saves me the last of her lemons every week because I played for her and her brother the first time he came out dancing. Her name's May. I've told her about you, and she'll give you her chair if you like, although she wants it back for the polka.”

She'd told me about the two keys one night when I was doing the books. I'd asked her if the man had charged for tuning the whole piano. I remembered waiting for her to tell me how much so I could tell her what I'd paid the last time ours needed doing.

“If we put the Plover down, that's our one room for the year,” I said. “I'd hoped you'd be able to meet people here. I'd have liked you to come down and see what I've done with the floor.” There was a long board I'd had changed by the cashbox because she'd always fussed about the sound it made under a heel. I'd already imagined her coming off the floor that way, and how long I'd be able to wait before I asked if she'd noticed.

“I thought if you came to the Plover you could see what I'd done, too.” Rilla ran the backs of her fingers along the old fold. “These people know I've got a husband. I want them to meet him.”

I had my hand out for the renewal. “Well, there's still the band room. You've got your Saturdays and I've got mine. I could close the till early on Fridays and come up before the last dance. We'd have that much more of the evening to ourselves.”

“I can tell how busy you've been by how far up your sleeve your arm is cold when you get here. I've got very good at warming it up. I want to take you over to May and get two chairs at her table. I'd rather let our year run out than sign up for another one of hearing you take the stairs when you think you've finished.”

She turned the contract face down and left a hand on it. I reached for the corner and caught her cuff. I put two fingers under it to the bare skin, and she let me keep them there. My watch said twenty minutes to twelve. I could put the good chair by the radiator tomorrow and set the cup without a crack within reach of it, and at the end of the night I'd have a full cup to carry back down the stairs. I tried to put her in the chair beside the one May was saving for me. I still had her at the piano.

“That chair you want me to have at the Plover,” I said. “Where will you be when the music starts?”

Rilla drew a breath with her eyes on my hand. The bass cut out and the grille gave one last knock against the skirting. A man's voice came up through it. “Can we have 'The Red Fan' the way she played it?”

Rilla used to make the drummer roll so softly before the first chord of “The Red Fan” that I could hear the cashbox shut across the floor. The piano came in before the drummer tonight.

“We were going to dance 'The Red Fan' at eleven,” Rilla said. “Our anniversary, when you wore the shoes with the patent toes. I'd left the music out for the band so I could get down and dance with you. When eleven came somebody wanted to dance with his daughter before they caught the tram. I played for them and then I saw you fetch your chair up close. A boy came to the piano with three more requests on the back of a programme. I took it out of his hand and stood it in front of the music I'd put out for us. I could see your face from the bench. I was quite pleased with myself because I could play the whole list and still have you sitting there when I'd finished.”

“I sat there till half past twelve,” I said. “Then I took my shoes up to the landing outside this door. There was one of those screws that won't quite go down in the edge of the step, and I had a foot on it all through the last dance. I heard you play 'The Red Fan' without anybody asking what I'd done with my shoes. When you covered the keys I got them back on so you'd think I'd just come up. You asked whether I'd had a good night and I told you I thought you'd played very well.”

Rilla turned her wrist till I could feel the soft inside of it against my thumb. “I'd still have played for the man and his daughter,” she said. “They were going to miss their tram. I could have left the boy to take his list home.”

I let go of her and brought my cold hand round the side of her cup. She'd told me afterwards that night that my new shoes looked as if they'd hardly touched the floor. I'd taken it for a compliment about the polish.

“I'd have come down in my stockings if you'd come up for me while the music was still on,” I said. “Would you let somebody else play a late set at the Plover? I'd come for it if you could give me the whole set. I'd like to be able to ask you to dance while everybody else is getting up.”

“I could have you there after it's over. There are two lamps by the piano I could leave on for us.”

“And I'd hear you do the last one from the side of the floor. You could still come up here after my late set and we'd have about the same thing.”

Rilla took my wrist where the cuff had ridden up. “I've played every late set since August,” she said. “There'll be somebody who asks when I'm coming back up.” She let go, turned her programme to the row of dates and picked up the pencil. She put a short line through her name above the first of November and wrote Ned's beside it. “I'll give him tonight. He can have what I'd make for it. I want to be there when you come in.”

The pencil was still across the programme. “Would you do that next month, too?” I asked.

“Give me the first Saturday,” she said. “Ned's good at the fast things. If I tell him to bring his own book, I'll have to listen to what he does with it. I can do that once a month. Can you get away on the first Saturday?”

“That's the one night I have to keep two trays of change at the till,” I said. “Miss Hurst knows how to run it. I can pay her to take the late set.”

Rilla lifted her cup clear of my proof and set it beside the Plover programme. “We ring the old market bell a little after your twelve to start the late set. I'll be on the floor when it goes.”

There was a quarter of an hour left. I looked at the door. “If I needed to get back to Miss Hurst, could I?”

She got up and took hold of the brass knob. “Open this one after twelve and think of the Plover. If you want to come home, step back through. You've seen me do it.” She took her hand away and I put mine in its place. Where her thumb had been was warm.

“I'd better go and offer Miss Hurst the key,” I said.

“Write it down before you go.” Rilla came back to the table with me. She turned the contract over and took the fountain pen from behind the upright of the radiator where she'd put it to get warm. I wrote *The Plover Rooms, Old Market* on the line where we'd put *Band Room* last year. Under it I put the late set on the first Saturday of each month, when I'd be off the till. She put down Ned Spald and the fee, then set the nib to the paper in the space we had left and wrote *together* in a hand I could have recognized across the room. She signed while I kept the paper steady with my thumb. When she passed me the pen I lifted my hand and she put hers down where it had been. I let her hold the paper for me while I wrote my name.

* * *

I heard a tram squeal at the turnaround as I reached the till. Miss Hurst had the yellow tickets ready, and kept one hand on them while I took off the apron and gave her the cashbox key. I counted out the sum I'd paid her for a night last winter and asked if she'd take the late set. She put the money in her pocket and the key on its hook under the till.

“You'll want to put that apron somewhere,” she said. “If you leave it here I'll be wearing it by the time you get back.”

I left it there. I found Freda just inside the door with a shoe on each side of her bag. “Rilla's expecting me for the first dance,” I said. “Can I ask you for yours another week, Freda?”

“I'll keep this pair a little while longer, then,” she said, and pushed her bag aside with her foot. When I went back up the stairs I could hear her husband asking her if he had to wait for me to get out of sight.

Rilla had gone. She'd taken her programme and left the signed contract flat on the table. I pulled the door shut on the good chair and waited out on the landing. When the clock struck twelve I put my thumb on the worn place on the knob and thought of the Plover Rooms. I had to pay attention to the name. I could still hear the floor under our late crowd until I turned the handle.

The first thing I got from the Plover was the smell of hot lemon peel. There was a square of ridged matting under the door, and I had to lift a foot to get it over the edge. I kept my hand on the handle and put the foot back once. My own landing board gave under it, in exactly the place it always did. Then I took the other step and let the door close.

The Plover still had the old iron columns from when it was a market, and lamps hung over the tables where the stalls must have been. The piano was up three steps, close enough to one of the columns that people put a hand on the iron to come round it. Rilla was at the bench with her book shut. She left it on the piano and started down. At the middle step she met a man coming up with a book of his own and put two folded banknotes into his hand. He got them into his waistcoat without having to set his music down. Rilla got as far as the last step before she looked back to see where he put his fingers.

A woman pushed a chair round the column towards me. “Ellis?” She had one hand around a glass with a curl of lemon in it. “I'm May. You can have this till the polka.”

She set the chair next to hers and pulled it a little forward so I could see past the column. I sat down in it. I heard Ned try a note twice, with a little click when the key came up each time. A man at the foot of the steps put a folded request into Rilla's hand. She had her thumbnail under the fold before she looked over and saw me sitting there. She gave the request up to Ned still folded and came round the column as the market bell sounded.

“Will you dance with me, Ellis?”

Ned gave the band a chord to start from. I offered my left hand the way I did when we danced round our kitchen table. Rilla took it till we'd reached the floor. Then she let it go and took my right. A couple ahead of us had got up close to the iron and were trying to turn. Rilla put her whole hand flat on my back and gave them their two beats. Then she took me round in the space they'd made, with the piano at our backs and the late set barely begun.
