Downstairs they are playing “First Night on the Hill” again, and the new piano player is taking the turn too fast. There's a place where the dancers have to face the direction they've come from. Rilla wrote in two beats there on account of Freda Searle's knee. He cheats her of one of them. If Mrs. Searle makes it round at all tonight, she'll come face to face with somebody's ear.

Rilla sets her bare foot against the brass grille. It has a little movement in it when the bass comes up, enough to make it rattle against the skirting. When the band room first gets warm on a Saturday it smells of dust off the radiator. She can tell how cold it is downstairs by putting a hand on one of Alis's arms when she gets here. The one she carries the books with is always cold farther up under the sleeve.

Last November Alis held the marriage papers sideways for Rilla to sign, where she could reach them without having to sit up. There's a box for the room the dead partner can come to. She wrote *Band Room* in it herself. A month later, when she was dead and came back, she tried to get a hand around the doorjamb and lost the feel of the wood exactly where it gave onto the landing. The door saddle was brass too. She knows how far along it she can set a toe. They've got until midnight to sign for another year.

There are two chairs upstairs and only one of them has a bottom rung to brace a heel on when you pull your stockings on. Rilla has dragged that one over to the radiator. Alis sometimes brings cakes up from the buffet when there's been a wedding. Rilla has set out a plate in case Alis thinks a renewal ought to come with cake. A marshmallow has welded itself to the plate since Easter. She had the knife out before she thought how it might make Alis laugh.

The band stops and Rilla hears the drag of a microphone coming out of a clasp. “'The Orange Tree' next, for our own Rilla Toller, with love from the Lark.” Somebody gives three sharp claps ahead of the rest. Rilla keeps her foot on the grille until the last of them. There are people down there who would raise their hands just for the title. She hopes one or two have noticed what the pianist's done to the time.

Alis comes up with damp pin curls and the papers braced against her hip. She's still got her cashbox apron on over her green Saturday dress. Rilla goes to unfasten it for her, lifts the apron at the hem where a penny's worked into the seam, and feels Alis put her warm hand over both of hers.

“Freda Searle has just offered to dance with me,” Alis says. “She'd changed back into her street shoes. She had the soft ones laid out on her bag in case I said yes.”

“Her husband minds the coat while she changes. That's what men used to be for down there.”

“He offered her to me all winter. Tonight Freda came up to the till and asked for herself. I stood there with my hand out for the ticket, the way you do when somebody's only just come in. She got this out from under the shoes.” Alis brings a little yellow wad out of the apron pocket, held with a red rubber band. “'I'd like Rilla to have them,' she said. She's kept the lot since December. I told her I'd put them somewhere good. And I thanked her for asking me to dance and said I'd be upstairs for the last one.”

Rilla slides the oldest ticket out. Its corners have gone soft, and someone has written the date on the back in a hand she recognizes from the requests that used to come up to the piano. On the new one on top there's still a little tag of blue paper where the stub was torn. *Rilla Toller's Late Set. Arrangements performed by the Lark house orchestra.*

“And here's the printer's proof for next season,” says Alis. “There was a little room above the dates, so I had him use the larger type.”

Rilla lifts the sheet and finds her name in blue capitals. They used to give her that size when she was filling the place herself. She sets her cup down on the middle of it and pulls the fresh marriage contract free. The line for the room is blank. Last year they both knew what would go there before Alis brought the paper.

“Where did you want us?” she asks.

“The ballroom.” Alis draws the good chair up to the table. “You could come in when you liked. Take a place by the floor, or the big table where you get a draught when the door's open. You always wanted that table when it was too hot.”

Rilla lifts her cup enough to expose the lettering. “You've allowed me quite a lot of ink for a woman at a table.”

“It's the same amount of ink as it ever was. We're already paying to have the dates in blue. I thought you'd be pleased to see the size they'd given you. I was pleased. I brought it back from the printer's without folding it.”

Alis smooths the corner of the proof with her left thumbnail, the one with the nick from opening the mail. There isn't a crease anywhere. It would have been a cold walk from the printer's on Booth Street. She might have taken the tram, holding the thing high out of the way of everybody's umbrellas.

“I'll tell you what will happen,” Rilla says. “I'll take your good table and listen, and for ten minutes the piano player will have a lovely time showing me what he can do. Then he'll ask what I'd do with the tune, and he'll get off the bench before I've answered. Everybody's going to have an old favourite ready. I used to make them wait until they had the decency to ask for one I could play. They'll come with lists now.”

“I'll tell him before he goes up. He's playing the whole set even if you're sitting ten feet from him.”

“Will you be in the chair next to mine if he comes over anyhow?”

Alis lets go of the proof. “I'd like to. I'd like you to dance with me, too. There's a rather good chance I've forgotten how to get around the floor with you, because I've spent eleven months getting to the end of every Saturday as quickly as I know how. Freda said, 'If you feel up to it.' I had to look down at her shoes to be sure. They never used to ask how I felt before they'd take me around a room.”

The music downstairs has passed into a tune that begins with eight bars of piano. The new man plays them in a surprisingly light hand. Rilla lets him finish. “Put the band room in for another year. I'll bring the folding chairs out and we can have people up. I'd have Freda, if you want. I could tell her which tune I used to fit round that knee of hers, and you could sit where you like and have somebody pour you a drink for once.”

“I'd still have to leave the floor to see you. I'd say, 'Excuse me, I have something to do upstairs,' and they'd let me go because they know me well enough to give me an hour to myself.” Alis sets an elbow down on the table. “If you're in the ballroom, I can take you over to Freda and ask her for another chair. That's the chair I'm asking you for.”

If Freda came up she could sit at the corner of the table. Rilla moves the plate to see how much space there'd be and the spoon catches under it. Alis bent the neck trying to get a tram fare out of a crack in the floor. When a board creaks on the landing Alis is halfway out of her chair. They listen to the steps go on past the door.

“What's so fine about the ballroom if you're still going to be at the till?” Rilla says. “You come up here with half a hand frozen every Saturday. Suppose I want the whole hand.”

Alis presses the cold one between her knees. “I thought I'd count the money on Sundays. I could sit with you as soon as I'd taken in the last of the tickets.”

Rilla puts a finger on the line for the room. “Let's sign for the band room. I'll have a think who's to come up first.”

Alis picks up the contract. Rilla expects her to turn it so it can be read from both seats, as well as a thing can be read sideways. Alis keeps it square to herself, her hand across both places for their names. “I can do another year downstairs. I've had a lot of practice at that part. I'll have a hall full of people who think I've got nobody, and my wife up here where I'll see her as soon as I'm finished with everybody else. I would rather leave the room blank.”

“When did you start thinking you could let it run out?”

“I'll marry you in the ballroom, if you'll have me there. I came up to ask in plenty of time because I thought you'd say yes. I've thought about leaving it blank since you put the cup down.”

Alis's damp sleeve has left an oblong on the tabletop. Rilla watches her move the paper out of its reach. *Since you put the cup down*. Rilla took longer than that to get the good chair out. She puts a hand on Alis's cuff and can feel the notch where her wrist bends. If Rilla closes her fingers she can get two of them under the cuff, to the bare skin. Alis lets her. The face of her watch is just where Rilla can see it. They have twenty minutes. The little beat in the wrist comes against the side of her finger. She holds on a little harder now and has to think what she meant to take hold of, Alis's wrist or the paper.

The bass cuts out and the grille gives one last knock against the skirting. A man's voice comes up through it. “Can we have 'The Red Fan' the way she played it?” Music begins below before Rilla has altogether stopped waiting for the pianist to call back. In the hall, when she'd done “The Red Fan,” the drummer used to roll so softly in the measure before the first chord that she could hear the cashbox shut across the floor.

“I promised you 'The Red Fan' at eleven. The anniversary when you had the satin shoes. When eleven came somebody wanted to dance one with his daughter before they caught the tram. I played that first. Then I saw you fetch your chair up close, and I thought I'd better get the requests out of the way if I was going to have some time for you. A boy came to the piano with three more written on the back of a programme. I could have closed up when I'd played for the girl. I took the programme out of his hand. You were looking straight at me and I was quite pleased with myself because I could play the whole list and still have you sitting there when I'd finished.”

Alis picks the spoon up with her free hand. “I sat there till half past twelve, and then I took my shoes up to the landing outside this door. You can hear the piano particularly well on the stairs. You played 'The Red Fan' for the last dance, and I got to hear it without anybody asking me why I was in my stockings. I put the shoes back on when I heard you cover the keys. I wanted you to think I'd just come up.”

Rilla lets go of the cuff and takes a drink of tea. It has the taste it gets when she leaves the bag in the cup. She had come up slowly that night, playing the last phrase to herself in case she remembered how to improve it, and found Alis on her feet at the top of the stairs with a hand to the front of her dress where it tended to gap. Rilla had been so pleased to have someone to take home while the rest of the band went looking for their cases. She would have taken the stairs two at a time if she'd known how long Alis had been on them. She would still have made her wait for the man and his daughter.

Rilla lays Freda's oldest ticket beside the proof. In the ballroom she could have Alis in the next chair and still have her looking past to see if Freda had got the ticket out of that bag yet. “If we have the ballroom, take my name off the bills for the year. Give us a Saturday a month down there with the doors open to anybody who wants to dance. Let them keep their money. I want to spend that evening with you.”

Alis keeps hold of the spoon and tugs at the proof. The cup slides with it. “Would you mind moving your cup another inch? I can't see the whole line.” Rilla lifts it. Alis takes the pencil from the apron pocket. It runs from the first letter of Rilla's name to the last, breaking halfway on the damp spot from the cup. She goes back over the break.

The first time they'd announced her name for the late set after she died, Rilla had stood up and straightened her skirt on the way to the door. The next Saturday she was already on her feet by the grille when they said it. She turns the proof far enough towards herself to see she can still read the blue letters through the pencil.

“I can lose the name,” Alis says. “They'll have to decide if they like the band for themselves, which is about time. I can't lose a whole Saturday on top of it. It costs the very same to heat the hall if everybody walks in off the street. I could give you a Tuesday for free. I'll pay a pianist myself and keep it open till the tram stops.”

There would be a lovely small party on a Tuesday. A pianist who knew two dances, and ladies who meant to stop after eight for fear of how they'd get home. “Freda comes on Saturdays. Let's have the room on a night when she's there and we really will have to ask her for a chair.”

Alis lifts the oldest of Freda's tickets from beside the proof. “Would you settle for the late set? The first set pays for the room, generally, and a good late set pays for the band. I'd keep the orchestra on its regular money. I'd stand the difference on those nights. We could make it the first Saturday of every month.” She hands the ticket to Rilla.

Rilla turns the ticket over. *21 December*, in Freda's handwriting. She gives the regulars three Saturdays to stop looking for her name on the new tickets. From the table in the draught she'll want to cross the floor before the first note. “If we're keeping the time for us, I want you to come out from behind the till,” she says. “Leave your apron with somebody else. I'll sit with you in the draught or dance or watch you dance if you want to. Give me an hour to see you by yourself after closing on the other Saturdays.”

“After I've locked up,” Alis says. “Last Saturday I was still at the cloakroom at a quarter past twelve having the difference between two brown hats explained to me. I want the hour to begin when I turn the key.” She rests her hand on the back of Rilla's chair. “And you can ask me to dance on the free nights. I want that in the bargain too.”

“Put down that we'll spend the time together. We'll find out which one of us can ask fastest.” Rilla moves her chair back enough to give Alis the table. “Write the room first.”

Alis writes *The Ballroom, Lark Assembly Rooms, Fellcross.* The pencil is too blunt for the space under it. Rilla gives her the fountain pen she put behind the upright of the radiator to get it warm. Alis fits in the change to the bills and the free late set on the first Saturday of every month, then hands Rilla the pen. Rilla adds their hour after locking up on the other Saturdays. She writes *together* with enough room around the word to see it well. She sets down the pen and holds the foot of the page while Alis hangs her apron over the back of the chair. Alis signs. Rilla puts her own name down facing the paper straight on, in a hand that has come back to her since the last time.

Downstairs the bandleader calls the last number of the paid set and the drummer counts them in. Alis stands to pick up the marked proof and the contract. “I could pay them to stay for one more dance when that's over. I'll have to catch the drummer before he gets his coat on. Come when you like.” She puts the papers back down and presses her mouth to the side of Rilla's head. “I'll be at the table in the draught.”

Alis goes out carrying the proof and the contract under the arm Rilla has never been able to get warm all the way to the elbow. Rilla watches the heel of her shoe come down on the landing, where the wood has worn smooth, and waits for the clock with her own bare toes against the brass saddle. Just before twelve, she puts on her shoes. The band holds the last note till there's time for one clear stamp of the drummer's foot. The first stroke of twelve comes up the stair behind it. Rilla lets the room go.

* * *

The ballroom comes back to her with a draught. The doors are open to the tram turnaround and the smell of the wet street comes in where a bank of coats used to keep it out. She arrives at the end of a banquette, close enough to the floor to feel the small separate shocks of the people putting chairs back under their tables. Across the room one of the staff closes the cash drawer and calls out that the orchestra will play one more. Anybody who wants it can come in for free. A couple just outside stop looking for their money.

A man backs away from the coat rail and knocks his elbow against her. He turns and puts a hand on her forearm. “Excuse me, love,” he says, and lifts a woman's raincoat clear of her head. There's a nudge between Rilla's shoulder blades as another couple squeezes round her. She moves out onto the edge of the floor. The stage is nearer than she remembered it. There is a clean place on the piano where the little gold lamp stood that used to show up the biscuit crumbs on the keys.

At the bench the new man is spreading a stack of music with both hands. Rilla puts her hand on the rail at the foot of the steps and sees the blue binding of her book at the bottom of the stack. She could open it at “First Night on the Hill” by putting her thumb in the split beside the spine. The rail is worn smooth all along the part where her hand fits. She lets go of it and walks on as far as the big table in the draught.

Alis has taken the chair that faces the door. She has her winter cardigan on over the green dress, with both ends of the belt hanging free. Rilla can see the place where her hand will fit when they stand up, between the last small button and the seam at Alis's waist. She gets close enough for Alis to reach her before she asks.

“Will you dance with me, Alis?”

Alis puts a hand out at once. As she gets to her feet the pianist strikes a chord. Rilla can hear the drummer's brushes skimming the snare under it. Alis has hold of the wrong hand. Rilla takes two steps the way they do at home before she changes hands and leads her in among the dancers.

Freda and her husband are in front of them. She's in the soft shoes now, and Mr. Searle is moving his feet very fast for a man who's meant to wait for his wife to get around. “Give us a second, Rilla,” Freda says, and leans on him to make the turn. Rilla steers Alis further in, where it takes the weight of a few couples to feel the springs. As Alis comes round the floor goes down under both of them and rises under Rilla's heel.
