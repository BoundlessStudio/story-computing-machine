# For the Other Thing

I'd put the rye where I could get at it from the back porch without so much as opening the door to the kitchen. I put two glasses out too, our good ones, with a little slope in the bottoms that makes any amount look generous if you see it from above. I told Renna I'd hang on to her for one quick drink when the wedding was over, and she said I could hang on to her for two if I gave her time to get out of the dress.

I'd told the emcee to expect us around five. I'd see Renna in her dress, give her as much time as she wanted before she took my arm, and text him when it was time for the walk. I'd sent the bridesmaids down to the field and pulled the old curtain across the porch windows myself. I wanted those last few minutes for Renna and me. By then Callum's grandfather had him down by the arbor shaking hands with half his own side of the family. I'd hoped to get a word with him too, but from where I stood I could see only his forehead.

We'd had three days of rain at the end of June and the grass had dried only on top. The screen door had swollen at the bottom and needed a lift with the heel of my hand. I came in on the last twenty or so bars of a tune the fiddler had been playing since I left the house. Out the end window I caught a sideways look down the rows. Yesterday, after Renna and I did the chairs, we'd been able to see Cooper's Ridge straight over their backs. I could see it now a piece at a time as people got up to let other people past.

Renna was on the bench where we'd done the hem. She'd taken her good shoes off and put her bare feet in the boot tray, which smelled of hot rubber in the afternoon. There was a white grain stuck to the side of her heel. I had been prepared, one way and another, to meet any objection to the shoes.

“I-I-I can't do this!” She said it to the tray, then looked right up at me. “I can't marry him!”

We'd had dinner cards for seven hundred and twenty-eight. There were seventeen left on the table when I came up to the house. I did have the figure in my head. “Um … there is over seven hundred guests outside.”

“Tell them to go home!”

She left it there for me to do, fair and square. I had hold of the cupboard knob by the time I thought to ask, “Or, I could give you some hard liquor and a pep talk?”

The screen door was still open a couple of inches. Renna gathered the dress off her knees and got as far as curling her fingers around the edge of the door. When it moved, a few heads turned in the nearest row. She sat back hard enough to shake the bench.

“Could you shut it first, Mum?” she said. I took the door by the bottom rail and lifted it clear of the sill till it shut.

I brought the rye down and poured for two. We had worked up to seven hundred by putting “and household” on both lists. I could name any number of boys whose presence mattered a great deal to their mothers, and Renna could name each of their girlfriends. I had been extravagant with practically everything else by then. I gave us both a good inch.

“First thing, feet flat. Get that dress clear of your shoes.” I took her glass over. “You can look at me right up until we get to Callum. People will try to get your eye, but you needn't give it to them. I can go slow enough for that walk to take us till Tuesday. Nobody can do a blessed thing about it once we've started.”

She put up her hand before I could start on the advantages of taking any walk with me. There was a pin still caught in the cuff of my sleeve. She had put it there for me at the fitting when I kept putting a hand down for the pincushion and finding her shoulder. She took it out now. For a little while she held it by its head in the same hand she had raised to stop me talking.

“Could you give me one for the other thing?” she said.

I looked at the glass I was still holding.

“The pep talk,” she said. “For calling it off. I want that one.”

She set the pin down on the bench where I could see it and took her glass. I had thought what she needed was an interruption. She took a drink, gave it a second, and came back with her eyes watering and nothing altered in the way she was looking at me.

“If it's the people, I can put you in the kitchen with him. I'll borrow two people who know how to sit still and the rest of them can eat as much as they want.” I had already begun to go over possible witnesses in my head. So many people had said they'd do anything for us, and I had naturally been in no position at the time to ask what they meant by anything.

“He offered me that on Thursday,” she said. “We were supposed to be writing what we'd say to each other. I had a whole side of paper by then. It was mostly thanks for things he'd done. Thank you for coming to get me the day they called about your surgery. Thank you for driving me to the hospital and sitting out in the car with the heat on till I could stand to go inside. All that part I could have said to him in front of as many people as you like.”

I'd been much put out with her that day because she'd let me spend half an hour alone with a bare foot out of the blanket. I'd heard somebody go by with coffee twice and been able to think of nothing except who should be there to bring me some.

“He saw I couldn't get started on the part that was a promise,” she went on. “He put both his hands flat on the table and said we could wait, or say it to each other in the kitchen with witnesses. I shut my eyes and tried it. I could see his face far better with nobody else around. Once I pictured telling him I couldn't promise it, I could breathe right down to my belt, and I hadn't done that all through supper. I opened my eyes and knew he'd see in a second what I'd been thinking, and I told him I'd be all right if he poured me some tea.”

I heard people all of a sudden. For a while I'd been thinking the fiddler was making the whole noise. There was a distinct scrape of chairs and a low, busy talking that carried all the way up to the house. From the corner of the end window I could see someone turning round to find out what she had missed.

“What do you suppose Callum would have said if you'd told him over supper?”

“He'd have shoved the kettle out of the road and let me. He's not afraid of listening to me when I'm in a state.” She looked at my glass. “You could have some of that, you know.”

I had it. I'd bought the rye because I liked it, and because I could still taste it about as long as it took me to walk from the porch to the end of the garden. It was a considerable distance just then. I sat my glass on the sill and took my phone out of my pocket.

“Suppose I say you don't feel well,” I said. “I could say it came on all of a sudden and I had the best look at you and wanted everyone to clear out. I can have them gone without anybody needing to ask you whether you can marry him.”

“Can you say I'm already upstairs?”

“I expect I can.” I got as far as typing that Renna had taken ill. Then I thought of my sister, who had once persuaded Renna to eat a hard-boiled egg on a faultless stomach by saying in my hearing that it was a cure for nausea. She would be straight up our back steps with eggs. Every third person I could see through the window would be able to tell precisely how well my daughter had been five minutes earlier.

“Wait,” Renna said. I had her dress across my shoe by then. “Don't write that yet. I want to get it out with you first. You're always doing the thing that comes next while I'm still getting one thing out.”

I was about to put the phone down in a way that would show her how unfair that was. She bent to clear the dress from my shoe and held the whole lot against her chest. We had left twelve feet between the chairs for it, on the understanding that it would be trailing loose and that I would be beside it. There had been plenty of things about the day I could have done without. I'd wanted those twelve feet. The worst of it was I had secured them quite early on and thought the rest of it was for her. I could see now the trouble she was having just to get the dress off my foot.

“Leave that here with me,” I said, and took the hem. I cleared a space on the bench so I could sit with her. “Now look. You get both feet down where you stood while I fixed the hem, and look at me straight. It's the same as I was going to say before, except you don't have to walk anywhere, because here's the person you need to tell. If you need a mother who looks disappointed, I can do that, and it won't require a bit of acting.”

Her mouth went crooked. I had my phone open against my knee with an answer already in it. I turned it face down.

“Say the one thing first,” I said. “I'll leave the rest of the people where they are till you're done.”

She put her feet on the lino and looked at me. I could hear both of us breathing, and the sound differed from what I remembered about fitting the dress, when I'd ask her to hold still and she'd go so still I would ask her to breathe. I waited to hear how she was going to begin. When she leaned forward she kept her eyes on mine, though she could have looked straight down into the boot tray if she'd wanted to.

“Mum, I'm calling off the wedding.” She let that much sit there. “Please tell them for me. I want you to say I called it off. And could you stay here with me till they've gone? I don't want you to be on your own out there either.”

I picked up the phone. There was plenty of cause for any of us to want to keep what I'd typed. We could have had a nice time in a week's time with a few of our own people, telling them what she'd had without coming in any closer to what had happened. They would have told me there had been something going around. I was on the very edge of thinking what I could have next if I kept that story. Then I looked at the room I'd kept everybody out of and thought how little company I had given her since I came in.

I erased what I'd written. On the next try I said Renna had called off her wedding. Then I asked the emcee to send everybody home for me. It was the kind of thing I'd have said from that door any other day if so much as a dozen people had stayed past when we wanted them.

“I'll sit right here,” I said. “Does that sound like what you want me to tell them? Everybody gets that part. You can have the rest with whoever you like.”

She leaned in to read it and I felt her weight where it came against my arm. “All right. I'll tell Callum before we send it.”

She sent Callum the news and asked him to come to the front door later so they could talk. While we waited for him to see it, a couple of people came up our end of the aisle and put their heads together above the flowers. I had to sit on my hands to keep from getting up to say it would just be a few minutes. Renna's phone lit up where she was holding it on her dress. She showed me that he'd read what she'd sent. I sent mine.

The emcee's voice came from the speakers where we'd been hearing the fiddle. There was a stillness while he talked and for a second after. Then people began to talk in the particular way they talk when everybody wants to be the one who has understood. Their heads weren't turned uphill by then. At the far end of the field, where it was easiest to get out, people were making for the gap by threes and fours. Down in front, somebody was having to go the width of a row with a hand raised to show a person at the other end she'd wait. I had been expecting to find out how the whole company would look on its feet. It was quite different to see them stand up at their own times.

I had left my glass on the sill. Renna got it for me before I got as far as turning round, and set it in my hand. Her train was still somewhere in my lap. I had meant to stand in that porch with half the buttons undone down the back of her dress so she could let it drop the rest of the way herself. I could remember looking at the row of those little things when we tried it on and thinking what a pleasure it was going to be, come evening, to undo them one by one.

I put my finger against the top button. “Do you want out of it?”

“In a bit,” she said. She turned the glass for a look at what she'd left in it, took a little more, and eased nearer to me on the bench. All up the field there was the scrape of chairs as people took their coats off the backs. When the cars started out, one after the other, there was enough of a gap between them to hear each one pick up the loose stones at our bend. We sat there long enough to lose count.
