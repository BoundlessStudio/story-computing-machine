# For the Other Thing

I'd put the rye where I could get at it from the back porch without so much as opening the door to the kitchen. I put two glasses out too, our good ones, with a little slope in the bottoms that makes any amount look generous if you see it from above. I told Renna I'd hang on to her for one quick drink when the wedding was over, and she said I could hang on to her for two if I gave her time to get out of the dress.

The fiddle was still going behind me when I came up the back steps from the field, a little before five. Down by the arbor, Callum's grandfather was bringing over another armful of family for him to shake hands with. All I had to do to start the walk was get Renna on my arm and text the emcee.

Three days of rain at the end of June and our screen door had swollen at the bottom. I lifted it with the heel of my hand and went in. I'd sent the bridesmaids out before I drew the old curtain, so now I had the porch to myself with Renna. Out the end window I caught a sideways look down the rows. Yesterday, after Renna and I did the chairs, we'd been able to see Cooper's Ridge straight over their backs. I could see it now a piece at a time as people got up to let other people past.

Renna was on the bench where we'd done the hem. She'd taken her good shoes off and put her bare feet in the boot tray, which smelled of hot rubber in the afternoon. There was a white grain stuck to the side of her heel. I had been prepared, one way and another, to meet any objection to the shoes.

“I-I-I can't do this!” She said it to the tray, then looked right up at me. “I can't marry him!”

We'd had dinner cards for seven hundred and twenty-eight. There were seventeen left on the table when I came up to the house. “Um … there is over seven hundred guests outside.”

“Tell them to go home!”

I had hold of the cupboard knob by the time I thought to ask, “Or, I could give you some hard liquor and a pep talk?”

The screen door was still ajar behind me. Renna gathered the dress off her knees and reached from the bench to ease the door shut. The bottom caught. She had to move it out a little before she could pull it in, and a few heads turned at the scrape. Her hand came back into her lap.

“Could you shut that, Mum?” she said. I lifted the door clear of the sill and fitted it back in the frame.

I brought down the rye. We'd both put “and household” on the invitations. As far as my sisters were concerned that still took in their grown boys and anybody they brought along to Sunday dinner. I gave us both a good inch.

“First thing, feet flat. Get that dress clear of your shoes.” I took her glass over. “You can look at me right up until we get to Callum. People will try to get your eye, but you needn't give it to them. I can go slow enough for that walk to take us till Tuesday. Nobody can do a blessed thing about it once we've started.”

She put up her hand before I could start on the advantages of taking any walk with me. There was a pin still caught in the cuff of my sleeve. She had put it there for me at the fitting when I kept putting a hand down for the pincushion and finding her shoulder. She took it out now. For a little while she held it by its head in the same hand she had raised to stop me talking.

“Could you give me one for the other thing?” she said.

I looked at the glass I was still holding.

“The pep talk,” she said. “For calling it off. I want that one.”

She set the pin down on the bench where I could see it and took her glass. She took a drink, gave it a second, and came back with her eyes watering and nothing altered in the way she was looking at me.

“If it's the people, I can clear the kitchen and borrow two witnesses who know how to sit still. The rest of them can eat as much as they want.” So many people had said they'd do anything for us, and I had naturally been in no position at the time to ask what they meant by anything.

“Callum asked me on Thursday if I wanted to do that or if I'd rather wait,” she said. “We were supposed to be writing what we'd say to each other. I had a whole side of paper by then. It was mostly thanks for things he'd done. I thanked him for coming to get me the day of your surgery. He drove me to the hospital and sat out in the car with the heat on till I could stand to go inside. All that part I could have said to him in front of as many people as you like.”

I'd been much put out with her that day because she'd let me spend half an hour alone with a bare foot out of the blanket. I'd heard somebody go by with coffee twice and been able to think of nothing except who should be there to bring me some.

“He saw I couldn't get started on the part that was a promise,” she went on. “He had both his hands flat on the table when he asked. I shut my eyes and pictured us saying the words right there where we were. I could see his face far better with nobody else around. Once I pictured telling him I couldn't promise it, I could breathe right down to my belt, and I hadn't done that all through supper. I opened my eyes and knew he'd see in a second what I'd been thinking, and I told him I'd be all right if he poured me some tea.”

I heard people all of a sudden. For a while I'd been thinking the fiddler was making the whole noise. There was a distinct scrape of chairs and a low, busy talking that carried all the way up to the house. From the corner of the end window I could see someone turning round to find out what she had missed.

“What do you suppose Callum would have said if you'd told him over supper?”

“He'd have shoved the kettle out of the road and let me. He's not afraid of listening to me when I'm in a state.” She looked at my glass. “You could have some of that, you know.”

I drank some and set my glass on the sill. The rye stayed hot under my tongue while I dug my phone out of my pocket.

“Suppose I say you don't feel well,” I said. “I could say it came on all of a sudden and I had the best look at you and wanted everyone to clear out. I can have them gone without anybody needing to ask you whether you can marry him.”

“Can you say I'm already upstairs?”

“I expect I can.” I got as far as typing that Renna had taken ill. My sister would be up the back steps before the rest had stood, with the hard-boiled egg she says will settle your stomach as soon as you get it down.

“Wait.” Renna leaned forward on the bench to see what I was typing. Her dress slipped off her knees and over the toes of my shoes. “Don't write that yet. I want to get it out with you first. You're always doing the thing that comes next while I'm still getting one thing out.”

I was about to put the phone down in a way that would show her how unfair that was. She bent to clear the dress from my shoe and held the whole lot against her chest. We had left twelve feet between the chairs for it, on the understanding that it would be trailing loose and that I would be beside it. There had been plenty of things about the day I could have done without. I'd wanted those twelve feet. The worst of it was I had secured them quite early on and thought the rest of it was for her. I could see now the trouble she was having just to get the dress off my foot.

“Leave that here with me,” I said, and took the hem. I cleared a space on the bench so I could sit with her. “Now look. You get both feet down where you stood while I fixed the hem, and look at me straight. Same as before, only I'm the one you're telling. If you need a mother who looks disappointed, I can do that, and it won't require a bit of acting.”

Her mouth went crooked. The line about her being sick was lit up on the phone against my knee. I turned it face down.

“Say the one thing first,” I said. “I'll leave the rest of the people where they are till you're done.”

She put her feet on the lino and looked at me. At the fitting I'd ask her to hold still, and she'd go so still I would have to ask her to let a breath out. I could hear it now, coming rather fast, and could hear my own too. She opened her mouth once and waited for the next breath. I'd got my fingers curled into the cloth in my lap. When I spread them flat, she was still looking at me.

“Mum, I'm calling off the wedding.” She let that much sit there. “Please tell them for me. I want you to say I called it off. And could you stay here with me till they've gone? I don't want you to be on your own out there either.”

I could have gone down to the field with the story about her being sick. At the front row they'd have made room for me to sit, and somebody would have taken my arm. They would have told me there had been something going around.

“Oh, Ren,” I said. “I wanted that walk with you so badly.” That was as far as I got. She reached for my cuff and pinched up a little fold of cloth where the pin had been.

“I'd be glad of the company,” I said. “I'll sit right here.”

I picked up the phone and erased what I'd written. On the next try I said Renna had called off her wedding. Then I asked the emcee to send everybody home for me. It was the kind of thing I'd have said from that door any other day if so much as a dozen people had stayed past when we wanted them.

I turned the screen toward her. “That's all they'll hear from me. You can have the rest with whoever you like.”

She leaned in to read it and I felt her weight where it came against my arm. “All right. I'll tell Callum before we send it.”

She sent Callum the news and asked him to come to the front door later so they could talk. While we waited for him to see it, a couple of people came up our end of the aisle and put their heads together above the flowers. I had to sit on my hands to keep from getting up to say it would just be a few minutes. Renna's phone lit up where she was holding it on her dress. She showed me that he'd read what she'd sent. I sent mine.

The emcee's voice came from the speakers where we'd been hearing the fiddle. There was a stillness while he talked and for a second after. Then people began to talk in the particular way they talk when everybody wants to be the one who has understood. Their heads weren't turned uphill by then. At the far end of the field, where it was easiest to get out, people were making for the gap by threes and fours. Down in front a woman stood at the end of a row with one hand up, waiting while the rest of her party came past the empty chairs. I had been expecting to find out how the whole company would look on its feet. It was quite different to see them stand up at their own times.

I had left my glass on the sill. Renna got it for me before I got as far as turning round, and set it in my hand. Her train was still somewhere in my lap. I had meant to stand in that porch with half the buttons undone down the back of her dress so she could let it drop the rest of the way herself. I could remember looking at the row of those little things when we tried it on and thinking what a pleasure it was going to be, come evening, to undo them one by one.

I put my finger against the top button. “Do you want out of it?”

“In a bit,” she said. She turned the glass for a look at what she'd left in it, took a little more, and eased nearer to me on the bench. All up the field there was the scrape of chairs as people took their coats off the backs. When the cars started out, one after the other, there was enough of a gap between them to hear each one pick up the loose stones at our bend. We sat there long enough to lose count.
