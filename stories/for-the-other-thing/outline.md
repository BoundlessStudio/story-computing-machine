# For the Other Thing

## Promised experience

The prompt gives a bride an urgent refusal, more than seven hundred guests outside, and the extraordinary adequacy her companion claims for “some hard liquor and a pep talk.” The later direction puts the wedding “just at their home (home weeding) between mother and daughter” and asks for fresh material after the earlier attempt. The wedding takes place at their home and these are the two people whose decisions matter on the page. I hear the comedy in the mother's jump from the size of the problem to something she can fetch herself. I expect to stay for the real answer to “Tell them to go home!” and to feel what either answer will cost once so many people have arrived. The reason for the refusal, the outcome, and the temper beyond that comic exchange are open.

## Takes considered

- **The pep talk for calling it off.** In the minutes before the porch door is due to open, the bride asks for all seven hundred to be sent home. Her mother offers to talk her through the wedding and ends up having to work just as hard to talk her through stopping it. The scene worth staying for is the argument over what her mother can say to make it easier and what the bride still needs to say herself.
- **The witnesses.** The mother sees that her daughter still wants the man and takes the distress for a request to get herself out of the room. The daughter instead wants her mother to turn a wedding she has spent months inviting people to into one her own daughter would choose to attend. The scene I would want is the mother being asked to stay as a witness after agreeing to send everybody else away.
- **What she told them.** Begin at dusk, after the people have gone. The daughter asks her mother to tell the story of what she said to make them go. In the same porch, they recover their exchange and see they have been protecting each other with two incompatible versions of it. The main reckoning would come after the choice instead of while it can still go either way.

The first take gives the whiskey and both women something to do while the guests are still waiting. It also lets “I can't marry him” stand up to a mother who has every ordinary reason to hope the bride means stage fright.

## Provisional direction

Renna and her mother Bess Corliss are used to being a good team with a houseful of people. Renna catches what needs doing before Bess asks, and Bess has mistaken this ease between them for the ability to tell when her daughter really wants something. They both said yes to whole households on both lists. Bess comes to fetch her from the house where they live and where, for now, the only company Renna can bear is the woman who wants to open the door.

Bess fetches the rye she brought out for a private drink after the ceremony. She starts by talking about getting Renna down the aisle in the yard. Renna stops her to ask for the pep talk for the other thing. She tells Bess how Callum privately offered to postpone, how long she sat there hoping he would spare her from answering, and what she said to spare him. Renna also wants Bess to take the refusal out to the guests. Bess is tempted to do it with a reassuring lie. The offer is genuinely kind, and it would let Renna repeat the evasion she has just told Bess about. Bess has put time, hospitality, and her own pride into this day. Her new pep talk has some anger and some exact help in it.

Renna tells Callum herself in a voice message from the house, while there is still time for him to hear from her first. She makes the decision about the wedding and leaves further conversation with him to come. Bess asks if she can tell people simply that the wedding is off and they're to head home, without publicly offering a reason. Renna asks her to say the bride asked her to tell them. Bess relays exactly that through the waiting emcee and stays in the porch with her daughter.

## Craft plan

**Teller.** Close third, past, tied to Renna's busy eye and her horror of having put herself in a position where the smallest answer will make the most trouble. Trial it against Bess telling it in first person past, wry and inclined to enumerate good intentions until she is up against one that went awry.

**Renna.** Adult daughter, quick with objects and directions, often good at guessing what Bess is about to ask. When she can get a sentence out under pressure she is literal and willing to finish it with an uncomfortable detail. What her mother has heard her call jitters includes the actual wish to stop. She thinks asking Bess to fix what the guests know is the same as asking her to believe her. Keep the pause and effort behind her opening stammer, then give her an unhurried account of Callum's offer and her answer.

**Bess.** Warm, proud, practiced at getting people fed and moving, a little hurt when her ingenuity goes unwanted. She bids for time with conditional sentences and offers more options while she works out what she is afraid the answer will be. Her momentary “Um” gives way to the improbable confidence of the quoted offer. She knows the head count and who is waiting on her signal. She wrongly takes Renna's previous assurances as consent to the marriage. She wants to be useful even when she can't make the outcome feel good. The groom and guests remain offstage. The request supplies the two opening voices and has no images or established characters.

**Place.** The Corlisses' enclosed back porch on Hennick Road, with the ceremony on the wide mown home field. Late June, close to five, enough sun after rain for the damp place to steam. At home this is where boots dry and vegetables come in from the garden. A chipped lino seam catches Renna's train. Rye and the rubber boot tray hang in the air. The old screen door scrapes on its spring, the sound of chairs carries off the open ground, and damp hems come away cold from the sill. The door is supposed to open onto the head of the aisle. The window gives them a sideways look at all the rows from the places where they usually put their boots on. The inside kitchen would take that direct pressure off them. The Corliss front bedroom would make the bride's need to hide easy to stage but strand her mother in the bridal preparations.

**Conversation owed.** Does Renna want help getting through the wedding or help stopping it, and can Bess stand behind her in front of all the people they invited? Renna tells the account of the private offer now because Bess begins by treating the nerves.

## Scene briefs

1. **The door, a few minutes to five (20%).** Bess wants Renna to take her arm and come, and thinks she's being helpful by reporting how many guests have made it. Renna wants the demand to send them home to be heard at face value. The quoted exchange arrives with them both on the porch. The cue to look for is the screen door dipping when she puts her weight on it. Renna makes Bess stop trying to open it.
2. **The long scene, on the porch (65%).** Each wants help on different terms. Bess is prepared to ask for a few more minutes, then to give the guests a loving excuse. Renna has to tell the whole thing about Callum and ask for the pep talk she actually needs. Bess tries to get a laugh out of the tiny dose she pours. When the laugh does not come, she has to find what she can honestly say to help Renna speak and what they can leave for later. The wedding music ends its current piece and the layers of talk outside come back through the screen. Renna can still go out the door or let Bess give the guests a false reason for sending them home. She sends Callum the first sentence herself. Keep the two glasses in reach so Bess can be seen as someone who once expected to toast a marriage here that evening.
3. **The word gets out, same porch (15%).** Renna wants the refusal to be hers after the hurt of actually making it. Bess wants to know what version of it she is being trusted to give. She relays Renna's words. Stay with the women as the sounds outside change from waiting for one door to the scrape and traffic of hundreds of people leaving. They sit on the low bench where Bess would have stood to adjust the dress, and Renna finally takes the glass she'd been holding. Boots, the room laid aside for a private toast, and the hand on the screen door load the ending. The proposal and the weeks of wedding arrangements occur only as needed in talk or summary. The groom's private response and any public address stay off the page.
