I paid the rent first. Four years, in advance, and I made the landlord write out the date I could stay until. He wanted to give me one of his ordinary little slips, the kind he pulls from a book and signs before you have counted the money. I asked for a full sheet of paper. There was room on it for the date, my name and the address, and I could fold it without folding any of those things.

Then, on my next payday, I bought a coat. That part wasn't sensible. I had a coat, and when the lining came away I had work thread to mend it with, but I wanted one that hadn't been repaired. The salesman kept telling me the new lining was silk. I would have paid the same if it had been ordinary cotton, provided it was all attached.

Clary says she knew I would buy clothes. She is mistaken. She asked what I meant to buy and I said I hadn't decided. She has filled in the rest since, as people do when they helped you and want to remember it being simple.

At four that November afternoon I was mending her shoulder. I was on my stool at the first plinth in the Haldane Gallery, still in my coat, with her power cable against my cheek. It was warm. They all run warm, and you mustn't hang a coat over one, although the guests do, and then I have to find out whose coat it is. Clary was holding her phone up to the window light, where there was hardly any light left. Behind her clear ear I could see the fingers of my own left hand.

“There. When I lift my arm,” she said. “It sounds as though someone is eating toast behind me.”

I felt under the socket where the cable entered her shoulder. Her loose shirt was made of the same resin as the arm beneath it. Before I was born, Clary had paid to have her consciousness taken out of flesh, then paid again to have that shirt reproduced as part of her new body. I had to work through a little opening in a fold; whoever had cast her had been more interested in preserving the shirt than making it easy to repair. Amber light lay across my fingers. The phone kept going dark, and she would jab it awake without looking away from the window.

“They've changed the lighting again,” she said. “Ask them to put it back.”

“It's November, Clary. I've changed nothing. Lift your arm once more, and leave the phone down.”

She obeyed, with the air of doing me a favour. I eased a bent contact away from the socket rim and the scraping stopped. At the far end of the hall a man sat forward over his own screen, tapping his heel against his black plinth. Another of the seven stood with one hand covering his face. He had been like that since lunch; I had asked whether he wanted company, and he had said he wanted the tapping to stop.

My phone vibrated in my trouser pocket. Clary asked why I had been checking it all afternoon.

“The clinic. I wanted to make sure they were still paying six hundred for a year. They're confirming tomorrow.”

“Six hundred?” She laid her phone flat on her palm and found a different page. “Come and look at what they're charging for mine.”

I remember having to wipe the grease off my thumb before she would let me touch it.

On her screen was a photograph of Clary in the hall, head bowed over the very phone she was showing me. Her exhibition fee was eighteen thousand marks, with a year's continuance charged separately at twelve thousand. She spread two fingers across the phone to enlarge the second price. I filled the feeders and checked their balances every morning: one year bought from a living body, one year spent keeping a preserved person awake.

“I could put a year into your feeder myself,” I said. “What are they doing with the other eleven thousand four hundred?”

“Making sure you never meet the buyer, apparently. Don't go tomorrow. Sell it here tonight.”

She said this while searching for something else on her phone, as if tonight were a restaurant with an empty table. I looked down at my hands. I was thirty-eight, forty-nine on the clinic's chart, with eleven years of sales making up the difference. I blamed the sold ones whenever one of my knees felt older than the other. The clinic paid immediately. I had two months' rent to find, and my wages were already divided between food, fares and the arrears on the room's heating.

“Your buyer won't want mine in particular.”

“He might. He likes to know where things came from. And you'll be here. You can show him.” She turned the phone back to herself. “There should have been photographs of you in the first place. They keep sending him pictures of me from the left. I have another side.”

On the next plinth Damil tipped back his close-curled head, following the cable over her shoulder to my hands. He wore a backpack made transparent with the rest of him; its straps sat permanently over the folds of his jacket. He hooked his thumb under one.

“Will you still get your exhibition money?” he asked Clary.

“Of course. The buyer's already paying for the year; I want it to go to Nera instead of the clinic. I think that's rather generous of everyone.”

“You haven't given anything away.”

“Then call it clever. You make every agreeable thing harder than it needs to be.”

Damil turned toward me. I was still on the stool; he had to look up a little.

“I once bought five years from a woman I met at a clinic. I asked to meet her because I didn't like thinking my continuance came out of a bottle. Afterward I sent her flowers for my birthday. She wrote to thank me, and the following year I asked her to come to dinner. I paid her fare and her hotel. For eleven birthdays she came. In the twelfth year she said she couldn't. I offered more money, although she hadn't asked for any, and when she still wouldn't come I wrote a very unpleasant letter. About friendship. I had bought five years and I thought she ought to keep being pleased that I was alive.”

Clary had stopped scrolling.

“What did she say?” I asked.

“She didn't answer. I asked the clinic for her new address. They refused to give it to me. At the time I thought that was disgraceful.” He rubbed his thumb along the clear strap.

I had serviced Damil for six years. I had known he liked birthday cake put beside his plinth, although he couldn't eat it.

“I'm not proposing a birthday dinner,” Clary said. “I'm proposing Nera gets paid. The rest can be refused if anyone asks for it.”

I put the cap back on her socket and climbed off the stool. When I crouched to put my tools away, her trainers were beside my head. Their clear laces were tied in loops that would never need doing again.

“Would you mind if I didn't sell it to you?” I asked. “If I asked tonight and then went to the clinic after all?”

“I should mind the clinic getting it. I wouldn't mind you.” She looked at Damil, then back at me. “I don't want to be as difficult as he is about a person coming to dinner.”

“Tell the director I'd like to ask,” I said. I wasn't cancelling the appointment yet.

***

At six the director set her tablet on my stool. Glasses clinked behind her as the waiter arranged his table. “I've changed the payee for the year,” she said. “Nera, you'll have to fill in this bit.”

The collector had come down with her. He wanted to meet me before deciding.

“Nera has been with us for six years,” she said. “She knows everybody here better than I do.”

The collector shook my hand. He had grey hair and broad, well-kept hands, and he waited for me to put my tool case down instead of making me hold it while we talked. I liked that. I also liked that he asked me how to pronounce my name and said it back properly.

“Clary tells me you can make the transfer here.”

“The equipment's here. I'd need to connect it to her feeder. Until I start the intake there's nothing taken from me.”

“Please go ahead with the connection. Then we can all see what we're discussing.”

I put my name and account number into the empty spaces. Beyond the tablet, a waiter was arranging little rounds of potato on a long slate. There was fish on them and something white he was squeezing from a bag. I hadn't eaten since the bread I had brought for lunch. Citrus polish caught in my throat every time I swallowed.

I fetched the donor cuff from the service cabinet and took its lead up the ladder. The lift went only to the reception floor; the open grating above the hall was reached by twenty-three rungs, which I knew without counting. When I was three quarters of the way up, the collector moved back so he could watch. His guests looked up with him. There was a torn place in the seat of my trousers which I had mended in black thread because I had run out of grey.

At Clary's box I checked her balance, traced her cable by hand and connected the lead to the service port. The feeder smelled of hot dust. Down below she was watching her phone. Her hair had been drawn up into a high, untidy bun when she was cast, with a loose strand touching her cheek. My own hair was coming out of its clip. I couldn't tuck it back until I had finished the continuity check.

“She has her own stool,” Clary was telling the guests. “Nobody must borrow it. We've had terrible scenes.”

I had found it in a skip and brought it to work on two buses. I looked down through the grating and she raised her face to me, grinning.

“You've still got it,” she called. “Don't look at me like that.”

The year lead hung in a loose curve below the service box. I secured it away from her ordinary cable, climbed down and put the cuff on the stool. The collector asked whether I wanted some wine.

“Water, please. I can't drink wine before an intake.”

He brought the water himself. I drank too quickly and got a mouthful of air, which I tried to swallow without anybody hearing. Then I pulled my right arm out of my coat, leaving it hanging from the other shoulder. The loose lining came out with my hand. I laid the empty sleeve across my lap, rolled my shirt sleeve above the elbow and fastened the cuff.

I was sitting lower than Clary. When I laid my right arm on my knee for the baseline, she stepped down from the plinth. Her clear trainers made two small, hard sounds on the floor. She reached for my left hand and waited until I put it in hers.

“You don't have to,” I said.

“I know what I have to do. I should like to do this.”

Her hand was warm and a little smoother than skin. The light from her chest shone through the knuckles, and I could see the shadow of my fingers against her palm. Holding my hand had put her phone awkwardly in the crook of her other arm. She would not put it down on the floor.

The collector came to the end of the stool. He looked at our hands for quite a long time. The baseline had finished by the time he spoke.

“Clary, would you mind staying there?” He pulled a chair nearer and sat facing us. “Nera, this is very different from the photograph I was sent. I wish we'd been able to discuss it sooner.”

“We only thought of doing it this afternoon,” I said.

“Of course. That is partly why it interests me.” He asked the waiter to leave the slate on a low table where I could reach it. “I'd like to acquire this arrangement, as well as the year. The two of you here, like this. I would pay you thirty-six thousand for the year and your participation in the exhibition. I'd bring people to see you together over the next twelve months, with at least a day's notice. Just sitting with Clary, as you're doing now. We might talk, or we might leave you to talk to one another. I wouldn't ask you to pretend to be someone else.”

Clary's thumb stopped moving over the back of my hand.

I looked at the director. She was changing the number on her tablet. When she had finished, she handed it to the collector.

My rent was two hundred and forty a month. I got as far as ten years before I lost my place in the sum. I could pay off the heating account and leave the room, not just keep it. I could find a room nearer work, or stop coming to work and spend a while finding out what else I could do. The cuff was heavy on my arm.

“You could have that place you were telling me about,” Clary said. “With the door onto the garden.”

It was a bedsit I had seen advertised in spring. The advertisement had promised a garden door and the photograph had shown a paved square with one chair in it. I had wanted it badly enough to look up the bus route.

“And will Clary be paid more?”

“My fee stays as it is,” she said quickly. “The extra is yours. All of it. Don't worry about me for once.”

I was worrying that I wanted the extra. I tried to imagine coming back the following night with the money already safe, sitting on my stool for a few hours while people looked, and taking a taxi home. It was easy enough. I had been looked at all evening for nothing.

“There'd have to be dates,” I said. “A day each month that we agree now. Twelve evenings. I could make twelve evenings work.”

“I'm afraid that would change the work.”

“I'd take less. I don't expect thirty-six for twelve visits.”

He sat back. His glass had left a ring on the floor beside his chair, and I noticed it because I would have to clean it up. He followed my glance and lifted the glass onto the table.

“I appreciate the offer. But if I book twelve dates now, I have bought twelve performances. I want to be able to come when I need to see you together, and bring someone when I think they should see it. That's what I am offering to pay for.”

“How many times do you think you would need?”

“I don't know yet.”

I looked down the hall toward Damil. He had come to the front of his plinth. The backpack lifted a little against his shoulders as his cable took the weight, and he stepped back, tipping his face toward Clary's feeder.

“You don't go anywhere in the evenings,” Clary said. “You tell me you're too tired. Forgive me, but this wouldn't be like hauling that case around. You could read. He might hardly come at all. We spend whole days here without anybody coming.”

“Would you want me here if you didn't come?” I asked the collector.

“Only when I give notice.”

“But every day I would have to look to see whether you'd given it.”

“You look at your telephone every day now,” Clary said.

I tried to pull my hand away to reach my phone. She let go at once. I opened the calculator and stared at it. I could come here and hold her hand instead of doing my job. There would be someone else on the ladder, and I would watch for her loose parts and hope she hadn't been given my stool. The collector waited for me, as patiently as if I had been finding him a price.

“I can't leave every evening for you to choose,” I told him. “I'd like to sell the year on its own.”

Clary put her free hand over her face, exactly as the man at the end of the room had done. She took it away before she spoke.

“Nera, do take a minute. You've been at these clinics for years. If I'd meant you to come out with six hundred again, I'd have left you alone this afternoon.”

“I've thought about it. I'm still willing to sell this one.”

The collector stood. “I understand. But I was hoping for more than the single transfer. I can't buy Clary's exhibition at the original asking price.” He handed the tablet back to the director without signing.

Clary watched its screen as the director lowered it to her side.

I put the phone in my pocket and peeled back the cuff's fastening. The contact plate lifted from my skin, leaving a pale oval. I laid the band beside me and caught up my empty coat sleeve. The lining was still inside out; I started turning it back with my fingers.

“Take six thousand off my fee,” Clary said.

The director lifted the tablet again. “Clary, are you certain?”

“Twelve thousand for my exhibition. Twelve thousand for Nera's year. He can bring his guests to see me whenever he books the hall, as planned. Nera comes to work when she's working.” She shifted the phone and held her hand out to me again. “I should like to get on with it.”

“You were very happy with eighteen,” the collector said.

“I was. I'll be less happy with twelve. I'm offering it anyway.”

He looked at me. I could have picked up the extra money again by saying I had changed my mind. I knew this because I nearly did. The director was waiting, too, and the food on the slate was cooling within reach of my left hand.

“One year,” I said. “Twelve thousand. No appearances afterward.”

The director amended the payment screen and turned the tablet toward the collector. He signed, then she set it on my knee. I read my part twice, signed with the little pen on its cord and fitted it back into the tablet.

I fastened the cuff again. Only then did I put my hand into Clary's.

I laid the cuff's little control block on my right knee, with its oval pad under my thumb. I had used the same model at the clinics; I had repaired this one twice. I held the pad until the setting turned from one year in pale type to one year in dark type. Below it was my clinic age, forty-nine. The lead moved slightly as the feeder above us took the intake. Clary looked up at the box, lifting her chin so the light gathered under her jaw.

The heat came under the band first. I was used to that. Then it spread up under my shirt and I thought I should have taken the coat off altogether. My face was damp. I was conscious of the collector watching the side of my head, where the hair had escaped its clip, and I wanted to smooth it back. I had to keep my thumb on the pad. My other hand was still in Clary's and I held that more tightly instead.

I looked at my right hand. There was a white mark beside the thumb where I had cut myself on the service grating years ago, and a yellow stain on the middle finger from a bottle of contact cleaner that had leaked that morning. I knew how those had got there. While I watched, the age on the display changed to fifty. I tried to find a difference in the hand. A fold at the wrist looked deeper until I straightened it; then I couldn't tell. I tried to get both feet beneath the stool, pushed one heel back, felt the pull in that knee and set it forward again.

At my last birthday Clary had asked me how many candles. I had made a joke about needing a second cake. Now I wished I had given her the proper number. Thirty-eight. I counted the years I had lived again, in case I had got them wrong, and kept coming to the same answer. The pad was growing slippery under my thumb. I pressed harder.

She leaned forward. “Are you all right?”

“Don't move your shoulder,” I said. “Please. Stay where you are.”

She straightened slowly. I watched the joint I had mended instead of looking at my own arm. A steady brightness filled her chest; it lit the loose folds of the shirt and brought out the worn pattern cast into her trainers. Now I knew exactly how much longer she would have to look at her phone, find the light too dim and tell me the shoulder needed doing again. I wanted that year of hers to go well. I also wanted her to stop talking until the intake was over.

The cuff loosened. I took my thumb away. A knife scraped the slate beside us; I turned my head because the sound was so close. There were the seven feeders again, and a shoe sliding on the floor, and somebody laughing at the far end of the hall. I hadn't realised how much of the room I had stopped hearing. I listened for the knife again before I did anything else.

Clary's balance came up on the control block, a year higher. My clinic age stayed at fifty on the display. I turned my hand over and touched each fingertip to my thumb, slowly, then scraped at the yellow stain with my nail. The hand could still do what I asked. I kept looking at it anyway.

“Finished,” I said. “I need a minute.” My mouth was dry and I reached for the water.

Clary kept her hand open while I drank. Her palm had the little indentations my fingers had made in it; they slowly filled out. She watched them disappear.

The director crouched by the stool. “I could book a second evening like this. We'd bring somebody else in as the donor. Would you run it?”

“I need to disconnect the equipment,” I said.

“Don't answer now. Work out your fee.” She stood to speak to the collector. I stayed seated, letting the last of the water cool my tongue, until I was ready to take the cuff off.

***

The money arrived while I was putting the cuff away. I got my arm back into the coat, then opened the payment notice, closed it and opened the account itself. Twelve thousand. My balance had been forty-three marks that morning. I cancelled the clinic appointment before I could think of any reason to keep it.

Standing up was worse than the intake. My knees had stiffened while I sat, and I put a hand on the stool until the floor was level again. Damil asked whether he should call the director over. He was still watching from beneath his raised cable, one hand on the backpack strap.

“No, thank you. I only need to stay still.”

“I'll tell her that if she comes back.”

A few minutes later I went up to disconnect the service lead, coiled it, and came down again to check Clary's shoulder. The hall was warmer now, with the guests in it. The smell of fish had settled over the polish. Somebody had moved the slate out of my reach while I sat, but Clary asked the waiter to bring it back.

“Don't clear it yet. Nera hasn't had any.”

“In a minute,” I said. “Lift your arm for me.”

She lifted it, and the joint moved without a sound. I lifted the socket cap to check the contact, then followed the cable to the back of her neck to make sure she hadn't twisted it when she stepped down. The loose strand of hair still rested on her cheek. My own hand shook when I tried to seat the cap, so I put it down on her plinth and waited.

“I nearly took the thirty-six,” I said.

“I wish you had.”

I looked at her. She pressed her phone between her arm and her side, leaving both hands free.

“I don't mean I wish you'd been made to sit here,” she said. “I can remember what it was like to have money enough to stop adding things up. You could have had that garden. I would have liked to hear about it when you came in.”

I picked up the cap. This time it seated properly. “If I wanted to stop coming after I'd spent the money, how would I have paid him back?”

Clary looked at the collector. He was talking to one of his guests at the far end of the hall, under the trees reflected in the window. He had taken his chair with him.

“He might have been kinder than that.”

“I didn't want to have to ask him.”

She ran a fingertip over the rim of her phone. I wished I had said something about the six thousand first. It was embarrassing to keep explaining myself to a person who had just given up so much money, and it was making me cross with her again.

“Thank you for changing your price,” I said.

“He'll expect it next year.”

“You can tell him it was just this once.”

“I shall. We'll see whether he believes me.” She shifted her weight in the trainers. “Would you still have come to work tomorrow if I hadn't done it?”

“I'd have come after the clinic.”

“You don't have to make that sound quite so awful.”

I put the tools back in their case one by one. I wanted her to see that I was tired without having to make a claim for being tired. The collector was coming back toward us. There wasn't much time left in which to have the conversation badly.

“I would have been angry,” I said. “I would still have come.”

She stood with her phone in her hand, looking down at it. Then she switched it off.

“What will you buy?”

“The rent. I haven't decided after that.”

“Let me show you the food before they take it away. The white stuff is horseradish. You used to like it, unless you've gone off it.”

I took a potato round from the slate. The fish was cold and the potato had gone leathery at the edges, but the horseradish filled my nose and made my eyes water. I had to wait before I could speak.

The collector stopped beside us.

“Before you go, would you take her hand again? Just as you were doing earlier.”

I swallowed. “No. I'm going home.”

He looked at Clary, who was still standing off her plinth. She put her phone back under her arm, but she didn't reach for me. I closed the tool case.

The waiter offered to wrap a few of the remaining rounds. I let him. There was grease on the paper by the time I put it in my pocket, above the loose coat lining. Tomorrow I would need to clean the floor where the collector's glass had been. I would need to check seven balances and the contact in Clary's shoulder. I knew where I would set the stool.

I carried the case past the window, outside the reach of the last cable. In the glass, the seven warm bodies stood among the bare trees on Bellfoundry Street. My reflection moved across them as I went to the door. I could taste the horseradish all the way to the lift.
