# The Attendant

I used to do my sums in the goods lift. It took a long time to reach the gallery, and the little mirror above the emergency button made it harder to lie about what another sale would cost me. The broker had given me photographs once: my face as it might look if I sold six years, or ten, or fourteen. All the faces looked like women to whom I would have offered my seat. I sold six and wished, as soon as I had done it, that I had taken ten. The fee was the same. You lose a surprising amount of your life to the fee.

On the evening Bellac came I had a fresh printout in my pocket. Eighteen months, to be taken the next morning. My landlord’s letter was folded around it. People have asked me why I did not move to a cheaper room. I had looked at one. There were four flights of stairs between the room and the toilet, and I had to allow for what another eighteen months would do to my knees.

The gate jammed at the Crenna Gallery’s floor, which the old indicator still called Bedding and Linen. I put my shoulder under it and shoved until the track gave. Bellac later had that shoulder reproduced with extraordinary care, but the new one has never had to open a gate like that.

Alis was reading when I came in. Beyond the high glass, Etram’s streetlights had come on among the bare branches. Her young face was inclined over the phone, clear fingers resting on its edge, and the light inside her made a long amber streak across the polished floor. She had once told me she’d paid six hundred and forty for the trousers, back when trousers still wore out for her. I remembered the sum every time I knelt beside them.

The last chord of the piano recording was dying away. Under it, the cabinet relays clicked. I switched on my earpiece.

“I would like you to listen before you use the meter,” she said. “You look so pleased when the meter says I am comfortable.”

I was not pleased. I was trying to get home. But she had put the phone down, which meant she really was in trouble, and I got out my pad.

The cleaner had gone hard at the plinths with orange oil. It caught in my throat as I lowered myself, first on my good knee, then the other. In the phone lying against Alis’s thigh I could see the dark bend of my back and the ceiling lead entering her shoulder above it. A strand had escaped her clear bun and wound itself round the coupling. I lifted it away.

“Tell me when the sound changes.”

“It’s stopped. No, there it is again. Don’t put the hair back.”

“I wasn’t going to. You could wear it down sometimes.”

“I like it up. I was promised it wouldn’t fall out, not that it would behave.”

I held the weight of the lead in my palm. The vibration quietened. When I took my hand away it came back, a very small rattling, like a seed loose in a pod. She had been hearing it all afternoon.

“The strain collar’s slipping. I can clamp it for tonight.”

Alis turned her head just far enough to look at me. Her face held the same warm light as her throat; the wall showed through her cheek, and through it I saw a shadow of my own hand.

“You clamped it on Monday.”

“A replacement means taking the load off the socket. I need the lifting arm out of the cupboard. I’ll miss the eight forty-two tram.”

She picked up the phone. I thought I had lost her, but she was opening a timetable. I could see the little figures backward through the glass.

“There’s another at nine fourteen.”

“That one doesn’t stop at Rowe Street.”

“How far is Rowe Street from where it does stop?”

“Too far for these.” I put my hand on my knee, which she could not see through.

Alis closed the timetable. “Then you had better charge for a taxi. The buyer’s coming tonight. I don’t want to spend another evening pretending that noise is somebody talking at the far end of the room.”

I wondered whether I would have to send my three unpaid invoices to yet another address.

I went for the lifting arm. Its wheels had little rubber tyres to protect the floor, although the cupboard had no ramp.

The seated man’s annual refill was waiting on the cabinet ledge behind the service screen. ONE YEAR was printed on its yellow band. In October I had sold another three years. I tried to lift the scalloped tab with my right thumb, then had to put the cell down and get the pliers. Once I had fitted it into his feed, the counter advanced by three hundred and sixty-five days. The white lead climbed to the ceiling and came down again into his bright shoulder. He went on reading while I put the wrapping in my case.

I hauled the lifting arm over the lip with both hands and brought it back past the others: the seated man bent over his handset, a woman touching her forehead, the curly-haired man with his head tipped back as though a bird had got into the ceiling. His backpack was as transparent as his shirt. He had asked to keep it when he was transferred. I had never asked what he had carried in it.

“Dena,” he said in my ear. “Could you lower my head before you go? It’s pulling again.”

“After Alis, Fedel. I haven’t forgotten you.”

I had forgotten him. I set his number blinking on the meter so I wouldn’t do it twice.

I fitted the lifting arm under Alis’s lead and let it take the weight. She stopped reading. Then her fingers spread on the phone.

“That’s better. You see? I wasn’t doing it to keep you.”

“You’ll have to think of something else.”

“I usually do.” She had begun reading again before she finished speaking.

The goods lift shuddered. By then I was at Fedel’s plinth, easing the cable behind his raised chin. The gate opened on a man in a grey overcoat. He stood well away from my case, waited until I had seated Fedel’s collar, and said my name as though he had made an appointment with me.

“I’m Bellac,” he said. “I believe they told you I was coming.”

“They told Alis. I’m still here because she wants the job done properly.”

“I hope you’ll charge for the extra time.” He took off his gloves. “I’m buying the collection. The gallery too, if we can come to terms over the lease.”

I set Fedel’s neck a little lower. He closed his eyes. The new angle brought the curls away from his forehead, where a small patch of light had been trembling.

“Don’t try looking right up yet,” I told him. “Let that settle.”

Bellac waited while I packed Alis’s old collar. His coat smelled of cold wool. In the breast pocket he had a pair of spectacles, although he wore another pair; I liked that and distrusted myself for liking it.

“I would like to commission a body for you,” he said.

“I don’t sell them. I can give you the maker’s number.”

“A body for you to inhabit, Miss Ors. Preserved here, as part of the collection. I have funding for the extraction and continuing upkeep. I’ve called the proposal *The Attendant*.”

Alis’s thumb stopped moving on her phone. I was trying to close the collar’s box with a flap that was meant to go underneath.

“You’ve named it already,” I said.

“I had to call the proposal something. The name can be discussed.”

I turned the box over and tried again.

“What would you want me doing?”

“What you do now. You would have a carriage on the overhead rail. Enough range to reach every plinth, and a supply matched to the work.” He brought a drawing out of his coat. “I saw you here a fortnight ago. You were waiting while Alis finished something she was reading. You had your hand behind her shoulder, supporting the lead. I couldn’t hear the conversation. Then she laughed, and you lowered her cable very carefully before you laughed too.”

I remembered the occasion. Alis had shown me a letter she had sent to the gallery about the music. She had described the piano recording as an unauthorised addition to her consciousness.

“I thought the collection would lose something when you ceased to be here,” Bellac said. “That particular attention. The person who knows when to wait.”

“Then hire me. On a salary. You could do that before putting in a rail.”

“I could, certainly. You might leave, or become too unwell, or die. Eventually her care would pass to someone else. I am interested in your continuing here, recognisably yourself, among the other preserved people. The relationship would be part of the work.”

“The relationship is mostly that they can’t reach the backs of their own necks.”

“You could send a replacement technician and never come again. Yet here you are.”

It sounded very fine with my case closed and the floor clean around us. I wanted to show him the three unpaid reminders I had sent for last month’s call-outs. I also wanted him to go on talking about keeping me alive.

“Dena,” Alis said in my ear. “Would you put me on the room speaker? I would rather ask him things myself.”

I set the meter on her plinth and put her on the room speaker. Her next words sounded thin in the big concrete room, less intimate than they had inside my ear.

“Mr. Bellac, have you allowed for somebody maintaining Dena? She’ll be very difficult about being serviced. I can tell you that now. She sometimes holds a tool in her teeth rather than ask me to take it.”

“An outside contractor, under the same provision as the rest of the collection.”

“And the allowance? She won’t need food, but there are things she likes.”

I drew the meter back toward me. “Alis, you could let me be here for this.”

She looked up. A strand of hair lay across one eye; she could see through it, so she never thought to move it.

“I would like you to be here for a great deal more of it,” she said.

Bellac had opened a narrow padded case. Inside lay a transparent hand with part of a wrist. He asked before putting it into my palm. It was a maker’s sample, heavier than it looked. I bent the forefinger, then the thumb. The joints gave smoothly. My own thumb had begun catching when I closed it, a little hitch I could sometimes avoid by holding it at the wrong angle.

“Your movement can be reproduced without the damage,” he said. “The fitter would work with you. I should prefer the clothes you ordinarily wear.”

“These have a patch in the seat.”

“So they do.” He looked away from the trousers with an awkwardness that pleased me more than his attention had.

The sample’s palm was cold. In service it would be warm all the way through. My knees were making it difficult to stand still, and I shifted my weight while turning the little hand from side to side.

Alis said, “You ought at least to hear the offer. He’s keeping us together. You know what the other place was proposing.”

The other bidder had wanted three of the people here for a private dining room. Alis and Fedel would have gone to different houses. I had spent the previous week refusing to tell either of them that it would be a nice change.

“Did you get an offer like this?” I asked her. “When you were transferred?”

“I bought my transfer. I was quite rich.”

“I meant when you came here.”

Alis looked down at the phone. I could see the old correspondence on it, columns of a business letter that had been forwarded so often the first lines occupied a strip no wider than her finger. She moved it aside and opened a blank page.

“I had six shoe shops,” she said. “Three were good shops and three had addresses I was vain about. When my heart began to fail I sold two, bought preservation, and chose this body. Forty-four, I was. I wanted to look like somebody for whom wearing trainers to lunch had been a decision. I had a house with a track through every room. A little curved one went out to the balcony. I could stand among the plant pots and complain about the street.”

Bellac folded his gloves together. He had stopped trying to show me the drawing.

“The business went on for another twenty years. I knew a great deal about shoes that people had stopped wanting. For a long time I thought that meant the customers would come back. Eventually there was one shop, then there wasn’t a shop. I owed for upkeep, for stock, for the house. The house went first. I moved to a preservation residence, which was respectable and very ugly. I hated having people remember me there.”

“The gallery bought your debts?” I asked.

“The gallery bought my body. I used the money to pay the debts. I had an allowance, upkeep for as long as I existed, and a position here. It was a sale. There wasn’t a little line I failed to read. They brought the agreement to the residence and I made a solicitor come and read it to me, and then I signed it.”

She had both hands round the phone. Her fingers shone where the light caught their edges.

“You could have waited,” I said. “Tried something else.”

“I could have spent the last of my credit trying. I was tired of being frightened whenever a message arrived. I liked the look of this room. They showed it to me in the afternoon, when the trees were in leaf. I asked to be near the window.”

The glass was black now. Beyond it, the top branches of the street trees showed in the lamps. I had been here for years and had never asked Alis why she was first in the row.

“And you’re glad?”

“I didn’t say that. I wanted to live. I still want to live. There are days when being here makes me so angry I compose letters until morning, and I have to be very careful who I send them to. But if you put me back in the residence with that agreement, I would sign it.”

Bellac brought the drawing between me and the black window. “Your upkeep would continue whether you were working or not. The endowment belongs to the collection.”

“And if I get sick of fixing them? I could wake up one morning and not want to walk over to her.” I couldn’t look at Alis while I said it.

“We call the contractor. You remain here.”

I looked at the drawing. Its line ran from plinth to plinth, turning in a clean arc before the goods lift. He had included the lifting arm, the cupboard and a place to sit.

“I’d want it to reach the door. Through the door, I mean.”

“The commission is for this gallery. There would be a resting place at the end of the row, quite separate from the working area. Hours could be set aside.”

“When people could look at me resting?”

“You would be a living exhibit, Miss Ors. I don’t want to pretend otherwise.”

Farther along the row, Fedel’s head jerked. I heard the collar knock once. His eyes opened and remained on the ceiling.

I put the clear hand back in its case. “I have to see to him.”

Bellac moved his coat out of my way. Alis watched me go.

Fedel’s cable had settled out of the clamp. With the lifting arm under it I could turn his head down a fraction. He asked for a little more. The angle he wanted would put the feed against his neck again, so I held it clear while he found a position he could bear. He was looking toward Alis. I bent to the work.

“Could we still call you?” he asked.

“If I was transferred?”

“If you were over there, resting.”

“You could call. I might tell you to wait.”

He considered that. “You tell me now.”

I stayed with my hand under his cable longer than I needed to. The palm was going numb. Bellac was waiting by the open case, looking neither offended nor impatient; this was what he had come to see. I could have let the contractor take over after that night. I had worked in other galleries. I would have to find one I could still get into with my case.

When I returned, Alis held the phone low against her thigh, with the screen pressed to her trousers.

“Why did you let me think you’d been put here?” I asked. “You knew that was what I thought.”

“It was easier. You were kinder to me when you thought somebody had done it.”

“Were you going to tell me after I was standing beside you?”

She brought the phone up between her hands and stared at the back of it.

“For a minute, when he began, I hoped you wouldn’t ask. I thought you might agree, and I could have you here without having to say anything disagreeable. But you would have asked eventually. I don’t want to spend the next fifty years hearing you wonder why I didn’t warn you. I do want you here. I want somebody who knows the difference between the fan and my shoulder, and I want you to finish telling me things instead of looking at the time.”

I wanted to sit down. I had been sitting on a chair at the sales office the day before, looking at the clerk’s hand while she turned my appointment slip round for me to sign. She had been about thirty-seven, my actual age. Her thumb had moved without catching. I had tried to put the difference out of my head by calculating how much I would get after the fee.

I picked up Bellac’s pencil and drew a branch from his tidy rail into the space behind the service screen.

“I want to be able to go in there.”

“That is the stock alcove.”

“The cabinet doesn’t fill it. There’s room for me.”

He stepped over to look. Beside the screen a man stood with his palm raised toward the wall. His hand was a few inches short of touching it. Bellac had to turn sideways between the plinth and the screen; I followed him through, into the smell of warm insulation. He opened the cabinet to see how far the door would swing past the proposed rail.

There were five-year cells on the lower shelf. I picked one up. The yellow band went all the way round it, and I had to turn the cell to read the number.

“Would you put one of these on my feed?”

“I had allowed a year to begin with. The endowment renews the stock as it is used, whichever size we install.”

“You said. Could I start with this one?”

The money from tomorrow’s eighteen months was already divided up: the room and the broker’s fee. Even the change was going on food until the next invoice cleared. I had done the sums so often I knew the last few coins without looking. I understood that one year and a promise to replenish it would keep me alive just as well. But I wanted these five together, with my name against them, before anybody could tell me how much of them was left over.

I put the cell beside the drawing, where the new feed would be. Bellac wrote the larger reserve into the specification. I watched his pen make the five.

Then he looked from the branch I had drawn to the service screen.

“It would put you out of sight,” he said.

“I could close the screen myself.”

“I’m concerned about making a commission whose principal figure may be absent for much of the day.”

“I’d still be in it. I want somewhere I can be awake without somebody seeing me. I don’t want to book it with the gallery. I don’t want to ask a visitor to excuse me, or have someone decide I’ve had long enough and come and open it. I could stay in there a whole afternoon.”

He looked back down the hall at the plinths, then at the branch. The screen was an ugly thing in that room, scratched grey panels on small feet. He drew the line of its top with his finger, measuring what it would hide.

“I could set aside the hour before each viewing. You would be entirely undisturbed.”

“I might not want it then. I want to be able to go in while somebody’s here.”

“I could bring people to see the work and find you absent.”

“You could. I can’t promise to come out because you’ve brought somebody.”

He took the drawing from the cabinet. For a little while I thought he was going to mark the branch. Instead he folded the paper along its old creases, keeping the new line on the inside.

“Perhaps I’ve mistaken what can be preserved here,” he said.

He went back out to the plinth and laid the sample hand in its case, fitting each finger to the padding while I held my thumb flat against the screen with my other hand and tried to find a way to call him back without taking the hour. I could read for an hour. On a good night I had that much now, after the tools were cleaned and before I needed to sleep, and I was good at persuading myself I’d wanted no more. The case lid moved; I kept my hand where it was.

“You’ll have to replace that screen,” Alis said. “I’ll be the one looking at it when she doesn’t want to see me.”

Bellac left the case open. “You would accept her being in there all afternoon?”

“I would be annoyed. I can’t tell you she’ll make it convenient for me.”

He turned toward me. I wanted to take my hand off the screen before he saw me holding the thumb straight, but I stayed where I was.

“I won’t do it without somewhere I can close myself in,” I said.

Bellac looked at the space he had left between the hand and the case lid. He took out the drawing again and laid it flat. This time he joined my branch to the rail in ink.

“A screen you control. No timetable. The rail still stops at the gallery door.”

I looked through the gap at the lift gate. There was an advertisement in the car for the January recital at Leston Hall. I had been meaning to go since the posters went up. I would not be able to go to that, or to another one. Even if somebody brought me the music, there would be no arriving late and finding my seat, no annoyance at the coat on the chair beside me.

My thumb caught again. I opened the hand against my thigh and worked it loose. There would be new posters next year. I wanted to be here to dislike them. I wanted to find out whether Alis ever sent the letter she kept rewriting. I could not make myself want him to withdraw the offer.

“I’ll need to read the agreement,” I said. “With that on it.”

“Of course. I can send it tonight.”

“There’s a sale booked for tomorrow.”

“You needn’t sell again. If you accept, the commission will cover your room until the transfer. It takes several weeks to prepare.”

I took out my own phone. Its case was cracked across one corner, and orange polish from my thumb made a crescent on the screen. He sent the document while I stood there. I read the part about the screen twice, and the part about the body, and had him add that the screen would be operated from inside. He waited. Alis waited too, with more difficulty.

Then I signed, and opened the broker’s appointment message. It wanted a reason for cancellation. There was a list. I chose *Other* and left the space beneath it empty.

* * *

Four weeks later I gave the landlord my key. Bellac paid the final room bill and the clinic, and I arrived at the gallery in a packing case with my own name on the handling sheet. A living technician connected the lead behind my shoulder. I could not reach the coupling; I had to ask her to turn it a little, then a little more, until it stopped pulling. She had red marks across her knuckles from the cold. I watched them while she worked.

For the first viewing I wore the shape of my work shirt, with the sleeves rolled unevenly, and the trousers with the patch. They had made my short hair clear too. In the fitting mirror it had looked wet. My boots could no longer be taken off, although the laces appeared to go in and out of their little holes.

The gallery smelled of orange oil. At six the windows were black, with snow in the forks of the street trees. The piano recording began again. Somebody had polished the floor and left a damp arc beside the lift. I avoided it, an old habit; my new soles would not slip. Above me the carriage clicked past a junction and followed when I turned. I wanted to do it again, simply to feel it keep up.

Alis beckoned with the phone.

“Will you look at my wrist? It’s been catching. I was waiting for you.”

She had to use the other hand to turn the screen toward me. Her clear cuff had slipped down over the joint. I put my case beside the plinth and reached for my pad, although my knees no longer hurt.

“Does it catch when you put the phone down?”

“When I turn it back. I can move it the other way quite easily, but there’s a point where it takes more effort than it ought. I didn’t want somebody to come and tighten everything until I couldn’t move it either way.”

I knelt. Inside her wrist a bearing had shifted out of its seat. I needed her to relax the thumb while I held the hand open. My own thumb closed round hers without catching. I was ready for the little hitch and kept bracing for it; each time the joint moved freely, I pressed a fraction too far. Alis drew her hand back.

“Dena. Gently.”

I set my palm against the edge of the plinth and tried the movement twice before touching her again. I could keep a steady pressure without the ache building up my arm.

By then Bellac had brought the visitors over. There were three of them, in coats they had not needed to remove. One came between me and the lamp, and a brown sleeve passed through the space I could see inside Alis’s arm. I stopped moving the bearing.

“Was this arranged for us?” the visitor asked Bellac.

“It’s an ordinary adjustment. The piece has been active since this afternoon.”

The visitor leaned closer. He had a pleasant face, a little pink from the cold, and his coat was lying across my open case.

“Would you mind moving back?” I asked him. “I need that tool beside your cuff.”

He looked down at me, then at the case, and gathered his coat with both hands. I thanked him and took the fine driver out. His shadow cleared Alis’s wrist. Through her hand I could see my own, and through both the small bright bearing I had to settle.

Alis watched my fingers. Her bun had loosened at the back, and the strand I had moved away from her socket on that first evening lay across her collar again. She was holding the phone against her chest with her free hand.

“You’re steadier,” she said. “I’d like my hand back now.”

I turned the bearing once, then eased her wrist back. She tried it without my hand under hers and brought the phone up to the old familiar angle. Her face went down toward it, intent, but she had not begun reading.

“I’d like a little time in the alcove,” I told her. “I’ll leave the channel on.”

She looked at the screen, then at me. “I have something I want to show you when you come back.”

“Save it for me.”

I stood without the old pause halfway up. The pad lay flat on the floor. I picked it up, folded it, and put it away. One of the visitors stepped aside before I asked. In the polished floor his dark outline ran across the light from my legs.

The carriage followed me past Fedel. He lowered his chin to look at me, and I saw the clear backpack straps move against his jacket. I passed the seated man and the woman with her hand at her forehead. At the turn, the raised palm was level with my face. Beyond it the concrete wall was bare. I took the branch I had drawn with Bellac’s pencil and felt the carriage change direction above my head.

The stock cabinet was warm. Beside it the installer had fixed my supply panel. FIVE YEARS was printed on the yellow break-seal. They had put in the size I asked for. My thumb found the scalloped edge without catching. The old sale receipt was still inside my phone case, with three serials on it. I rested my hand against the band and felt the pulse of the supply through it, steady against the clear pad of my finger.

There was room to sit if I moved my case. I did that, reached up and drew the screen. Alis’s bent head disappeared panel by panel. The visitors’ shoes disappeared. I set the catch from inside, and nobody touched it.

Above the screen my lead descended from the rail in a long white curve. Its reflection lay across the cabinet door. I took my phone out and settled it in my new hand. Light from my chest shone through the clear fingers underneath it. I began to read.
