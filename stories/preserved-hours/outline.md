# Provisional outline

## The experience promised

The request explicitly joins purchasable immortality, poor people selling actual years of life, a struggling technician, and an exclusive gallery of conscious people in transparent artificial bodies, tethered overhead and collected as living art. Its distinctive opportunity is to make beautiful, apparently still objects become people while the labour and unequal bargains keeping them luminous become felt. I infer seductive unease and class dread from “preserved souls,” “sell years,” and “displayed”; the request does not prescribe horror, a rebellion, a happy ending, a particular relationship, or a scientific account. The supplied original supports cold monumental space, warm translucent bodies, ordinary contemporary clothes and absorbed human gestures. Those are visual and casting references, not instructions in an attached document. Scale, names, motives, ownership rules, teller, and outcome remain the writer’s choices.

## Three takes considered

1. **The sale, played straight.** During a collector’s private viewing, a technician discovers that the proposed new acquisition is her own indefinitely preserved working life. The scene to turn to is the technician, a conscious exhibit, and the buyer examining the body in which she could escape poverty and death at the price of becoming a possession.
2. **The public refusal.** During an auction, the technician releases the exhibited people’s movement and voices; the souls use their exquisite bodies to obstruct their own sale. The strongest scene is the row abandoning its poses together, but sustaining a credible escape would crowd the people with security and system mechanics.
3. **The long view.** An already preserved technician recounts a century of changing collectors through three repairs of the same phone-holding woman. The scene to turn to is the woman refusing a fashionable replacement body; the span risks making the present-day cost of selling years remote.

**Choose 1:** its central transaction needs every distinctive part of this request and can stay with three people in one gallery. The bleak outcome is a provisional choice, not a requirement. This will end in the commodification of maintenance, not in maintenance celebrated as a sufficient moral victory.

## Provisional direction

Dena Ors, thirty-seven by the calendar and much older after successive year sales, services the Crenna Gallery before a private evening viewing. Her next extraction appointment is booked: wages will not cover her renewed room lease. Her trade lets her hear the exhibits over a local maintenance channel, although visitors ordinarily encounter their curated silence. She is particularly familiar with Alis Varo, the clear young woman with the bun and phone nearest the entrance. Alis bought preservation when rich; after her fortune failed, her upkeep and body passed into a collection. She still wants to live and has no intention of becoming Dena’s cautionary parable.

The purchaser, Mr. Bellac, is buying the collection and commissioning **The Attendant**. He admires how Dena touches a shoulder connector while listening to the person inside it. He offers extraction and perpetual gallery-funded upkeep for Dena herself, with her skill and unscripted work as the artwork. She can refuse and remain mortal, poor, and employed elsewhere if she can find employment. She cannot afford the private, freely mobile preservation Bellac plans for himself. The offer is not a concealed murder or a fraudulent trick: its terrible attraction must survive clear knowledge of its terms.

The long central scene is the inspection and bargaining. Dena seeks a real life rather than a beautiful captivity; Bellac wants an authentic worker whose attention cannot be manufactured; Alis wants Dena to stay because she trusts her hands, fears replacement, and likes her company. Alis tells Dena, in order and at length, how preservation became ownership, including the choices she made willingly. She also admits that she would make the original choice again. This denies Dena a convenient spokeswoman for either death or freedom. Dena tests the price of mobility and refusal, then chooses survival with the knowledge that someone poorer will supply the years keeping her alive. Her agreement is a consequential compromise, not proof that the bargain is fair.

After a short interval, the final scene inhabits Dena’s first viewing as the acquisition. Her clear working body moves between the existing plinths under a short ceiling carriage; its range ends at the gallery door. She does a real repair while Bellac’s visitors look at her doing it. Her own new chest brightens on another seller’s life. No institution falls, and no narrator pronounces her redeemed or defeated. She has acquired the future she wanted and become an exhibit whose work includes concealing its cost.

## Craft plan

**Teller and length.** Approximately 4,000–4,800 words; close third past, principally inside Dena’s impatient, materially exact, intermittently hungry mind. Her eye catches a connector before an expression, then has to return to the expression. Trial this against a more expansive first-person Dena looking back from preservation, defensive about the choice she made. The voice must contain appetite and embarrassment as well as diagnosis.

**The three people.** Dena is good with delicate fittings and bad at accepting a bargain without checking its workmanship. She wants enough money to stop selling years; secretly she wants to see what happens in the world after she would otherwise die. She knows the equipment, wrongly treats Alis’s attitude as merely the result of captivity, and does not know she is being assessed for acquisition. Her speech is practical, sometimes rambling when offended, with jokes from work and a reluctance to ask for kindness. **Alis** knows the long history of her own bargain, not Dena’s finances or Bellac’s commission. She wants careful maintenance and company, but also wants Dena to choose her rather than pity her. She is socially assured, vain, and unsentimental about her own fear: expansive, circumstantial speech, capable of a disagreeable admission instead of a wise verdict. **Bellac** knows the commission and Dena’s sale history, not what the two women say privately. He mistakes purchasing an escape from death for settling the question of consent. He speaks courteously in complete, accumulating explanations; when challenged he becomes more particular, not louder. Their off-page speech trials concern the same bad meal and do not sound interchangeable.

**Conversation owed.** What does a preserved person actually get to do with the life someone has bought for them, and what would make Dena accept that life? Alis gives her account now because Dena is considering entering the same arrangement and has the technical attention to hear it without treating her as a novelty. Bellac remains present for the uncomfortable parts; this is negotiation among interested people, not a private therapeutic exchange.

**Reference study.** The pictured group shares warm internal light, transparent flesh and clothes, black separate plinths, shoulder/upper-back feeds, and ordinary absorbed poses; they occupy a huge room without possessing it. They will remain a visible, conscious chorus. The foreground woman becomes Alis: loose bun and stray hair, downturned but concentrated face, loose trousers and trainers, both hands engaged with a translucent phone. Her attention is chosen as well as imposed: she checks the visitor’s reflection in the phone while pretending absorption; her translucent fingers hold it harder during her account; the bun and bent head return when she studies Dena’s completed body rather than the old screen. The chin-up, curly-haired backpacked man stands with the air of someone still waiting for an announcement: his lifted throat exposes the connector Dena must reach, and his head turns a little to hear the bargaining. The seated phone user, farther raised palm, and hand-to-forehead figure retain their distinct gestures without receiving miniature biographies or names. The image leaves histories and attitudes open; this casting reads absorption, self-possession, and distance rather than turning everyone into a uniform pleading victim. No nudity or physical horror is needed; transparent clothing and anatomy remain like the original. Dena and Bellac are new people, not identities claimed for the distant silhouette.

**Place.** Considered: a collector’s mansion, an auction-house loading floor, and the pictured public-facing monumental gallery. Choose the **Crenna Gallery in Etram**, a private viewing hall above a former department store, because the contrast between a worker’s access and a buyer’s access can happen in the same beautiful room. None of the established universe locations fits this modern commercial setting; no crossover is imposed. First snow, late afternoon through evening; the last scene is several weeks later. Staff polish plinths, clean finger grease from fittings, time the display light, and prepare immaculate empty space whether or not a sale happens. Five anchors: cold blue glass facing bare street trees; amber cores reflected twice in the polished floor; an overhead trolley’s quiet click; sharp orange-oil polish over warmed insulation; concrete chill through Dena’s knees. The old goods lift still shudders before the buyers emerge. Fixed feeds make walking to someone a privilege and moving an exhibit a two-person operation. The ceiling service carriage makes Dena’s eventual movement possible while visibly limiting it. Its circuit returns her to the people she used to visit from outside.

**One speculative economy.** Purchased human years maintain an extracted mind’s continuity; the overhead supply delivers that life together with the power running its shell. Selling a year makes the seller’s biological life a year shorter. Explain this through Dena’s appointment and the gallery’s upkeep, not through a lesson in its physics. An extracted person is the same morally consequential person, not an error or an imitation.

## Inhabited scenes and ending

1. **Before the viewing, 25%.** Dena checks Alis’s overheating shoulder feed in the cold hall. Dena wants an easy finish before her sale appointment; Alis wants her to stay and repair properly rather than fob her off with a temporary fitting. Alis knows the new buyer is coming; Dena thinks this is ordinary resale. The year sale and its bodily cost must become intelligible through Dena’s immediate reckoning. The curled-back man interrupts through the channel over his own uncomfortable neck angle. **Turn:** Bellac arrives and asks Dena to remain; his questions concern her attention rather than the repair invoice. Remember Dena kneeling in the reflected internal light.
2. **The private viewing, LONG, 55%.** Same hall, blue daylight gone; Dena, Alis, Bellac. Reveal the commission early enough to give refusal and attraction room. They inspect the proposed working body and its overhead range. Dena wants movement, control over her work, and an answer to what happens if she stops; Bellac wants her willing absorption, not an impersonation. Alis wants Dena there but must risk telling the unflattering truth about her own life. Speak the limits plainly, tell Alis’s history fully, allow argument about a concrete adjustment to the proposed body, and let the room interrupt when another soul needs attention. **Turn:** Dena stops treating extraction as an implausible fantasy and makes the informed, costly choice. Remember her reaching toward the clear replacement hand without touching it.
3. **First viewing, 20%.** Several weeks later, same actual room, now felt through Dena’s artificial body. Alis wants Dena’s familiar attention and tests whether the new hand is as steady; Dena wants the ordinary repair to remain something she does rather than something people consume. Bellac wants visitors to notice its authenticity. **Turn/payoff:** a real technical need and a buyer’s gaze become inseparable. Return the kneeling posture, the phone’s reflection, and the carriage’s limiting click. Stay in Dena’s experience of a whole minute, with a repair she cannot finish until the visitor gives her room; no dry final exchange, motto, or explanation of the moral.

Summarize extraction without a procedure scene. Omit a tour of the year market, a separate antagonist, a wider conspiracy, and the history of the technology. The ending’s beauty must remain difficult to refuse: the person who has repeatedly sold away her future is finally inside one, and the overhead line makes its ownership visible.
