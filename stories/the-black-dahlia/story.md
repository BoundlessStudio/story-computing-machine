# The Dedication

Vernon Saye carried a piece of election cake through the velvet and asked the Black Dahlia to take a bite for luck. She had put on her gloves. He cut a corner on the edge of the fork and held it up for her. She kept the cake on her tongue until he had set down the plate and come close enough for her to give him back all the sugar she could bear. He had a little cut from shaving beside his mouth. She took care over that.

Out front the waiters were collecting the knives. The trumpet player sat in shirtsleeves on a kitchen chair, blowing through his horn for warmth. Plates were going past him into the kitchen and the hot smell of onions was coming out the other way. Through a join in the curtains she could see Emil Corbett in his usual place, holding his plate out level for a second piece of cake. He ate that way in company, slowly and with a little appetite left over. She had seen him spend more appetite on less.

She leaned back against a brass post and tipped her head for Vernon to lift the hair off her neck. The post was cold all through her dress, even where the dress had layers to it.

“Leave a light for me,” she sang into his waistcoat, warming her voice.

“I can wait.” He always spoke his part. He could do a good imitation of a man singing when there were votes in it, and he had once sung into a radio microphone with the mayor's entire choir, but with her he let himself have the voice he'd been given.

She had got his first office for him from the other side of this curtain. The alderman had been eating lemon pie on the night she gave the order. Now Emil had cake crumbs in the turn of his black cuff and Vernon was breathing against the spot where she had put on her perfume, taking his time with it.

“I've got you something,” he said. “You're going to have the Oriole.”

She caught his hand before he could put her hair back. “Keep talking. I was promised a whole street of Oriole Rooms.”

“I want you to have it out of my hand. You come down to the office tomorrow and we'll see to it. We'll put your name up the length of the building if you want it up there. Or we can take the signs down altogether and never let another soul in here till you feel like it. I don't suppose anybody's ever made you that offer.”

On the floor a toast went up for the next mayor of Merrow and ran out of strength about halfway back. The alderman used to have the whole room with him when they toasted him. Everybody knew you did a little extra drinking at the Oriole so Emil would see how well you were enjoying what you bought from him. Leave a glass full too long and he sent a fresh one.

“I was reckoning on the rooms down Bell Street,” she said. “You'd have all the other trades who pay Emil. I'd collect on the music, and the police could take their time getting to Bell Street if anybody complained how late it went.”

He smoothed a place on the velvet with the back of his hand. Now she was going to hear about the glass on their new kitchen door or the window at the end of the bath. He had a little white house picked out up above the tram depot and had drawn her plans of the rooms on the backs of his speeches. In his plans there was always another window.

“I went down Bell Street this summer, honey. It was daylight and you could see the men leaning out to shake their sheets. I saw a drummer you know put half a pound of butter on his windowsill to keep cool. I came back uptown that afternoon and sat down with some men who offered me a way to win. They had a whole new street laid out. Storefronts as wide as this room. Flats with breakfast rooms in them, if you can conceive of that. I've given them Bell Street, and they want to start with empty blocks. We'll close the clubs tomorrow.”

He said it into her hair. If she kept still she could have a little more of his mouth where it was.

“Did you tell them what you’d promised me before you promised them Bell Street?”

“You could sing on the worst night in February and clear more in here than you’d get out of the lot of them in a month.” He took her by the shoulders and looked down at her as if she might have missed the good part of what he had said. “Remember the night you did for the alderman? When I came over to you I had lemon all over my fingers. I thought I could disguise the fact that I was shaking if I kept on eating. You gave me a napkin and asked what I wanted. That was the first time anybody ever asked me. Now I want a little corner of the city I can shut the door on, with you on the same side of the door.”

She could see how such a night would go. She'd sing a little and he would fetch ice and always return, which was a pretty good trick for a man in office. There were women she knew who would pay to spend a winter in a house where he came back every night. If he listened with his eyes half shut she could still lose all sense of how a minute was measured.

The ice man brought twenty sacks to the Oriole on a Saturday. At the end of the night he started his rounds down Bell Street, when the last set was just beginning. Her favorite room down there was six doors from the bakery. The singer had to stand still if she wanted both her heels on the platform and the house got so hot she’d seen a woman take off her wig and hold it in her lap through a ballad. It ought to be worth a good deal more than anybody was paying for it. Once there were fresh storefronts and flats with breakfast rooms in them, people would still be talking about how it had sounded.

“Emil will want to know about Bell Street,” she said. She had promised Vernon that Emil would die during the set. It bothered her that Vernon had made a gift of the man’s room with Emil still fit to take a second piece of cake.

“Let him ask. Come sit with me when you're done. I've got our lamp.” He squeezed her wrist and took hold of the curtain where her hand would have gone. Beyond the heavy red fold the pianist was already playing.

For the first few steps the light was in her eyes and she could smell the wool of someone's wet coat. She knew the last board by the way it gave under her toe, and set her foot so the slit in her dress would fall open over her thigh. The bass came up through the thin soles of her shoes. When she reached for the ribbed head of the microphone and put the palm of her other hand out, the talk diminished so fast she heard a piece of ice settle in a glass.

She took the first number on the bright side. She let the words get almost away from the bass and brought the two in together at the end of the line, so that the people who had heard the tune a hundred times raised their heads to see where she had got it from. The trumpet answered with a little figure she could sing back to him. She did it twice and the second time he gave her back the first figure with the middle taken out. Still chewing his cake, Emil Corbett laughed. That was the right place to laugh. There were men all over the room she could have paid to laugh where she wanted them to, and Emil could still do it for nothing.

She looked down on the people he had seated for Vernon. He had put two men who hated each other at the same table, with their wives between them, and a judge with a painful touch of gout facing the stairs he was going to have to go up. The chief of police had the place beside Emil. On certain nights the chief used the Oriole as a second office. You could wait your turn on the carpet and get a permit after supper the same as you might go into the station for one, though there was some expense to the convenience. He'd come to hear what tomorrow looked like. She knew a little about what he hoped it would look like from a man who had kept her beside him till somebody came in with breakfast and then asked what the chief ought to be paid for a week's peace. She had given him a figure. He had thought she was being funny.

She gave the song to the piano for a while. Emil was rising and she'd only to beckon him the rest of the way over. A few weeks after he'd bought the piano, he'd had the stage raised three inches so he could see the singer's shoes from his chair. He walked up the steps tonight with his head thrown back, listening for a note he knew was coming. The band let him pass just far enough for the light over the apron to get him. She moved away from the microphone to meet him there and laid a glove on his sleeve.

“The door sticks when it's wet,” Emil said. “The man you liked on the door has been going out every ten minutes to let somebody in, and his feet are soaked. They'll blame him for it if the show starts late. I hope you'll remember who's been paying him to wait out there for your people.”

“I've always liked that man. I was thinking I'd ask you to move him to my favorite room down Bell Street. Nicer door down there. It looks like it'll be shut for good.”

Emil looked at the slit in her dress and then at her face. “I have profitable places on Bell Street. They make a good dinner for you down there as soon as they hear you've come in, and there's a reason for that.”

“Vernon's given the blocks to his backers. The police start closing doors tomorrow. They'd certainly close yours on Bell Street, darling. He told me at the curtain.”

For a while Emil listened to the piano. “There's invested money in those rooms,” he said finally. “Vernon knows that. We'd have to be compensated.”

“I suppose you'd give the compensation to the men whose money you took.”

Emil's cheek moved as though he still had something to chew. One November day a long time ago, she'd got him to take an afternoon off. She had worn a slip with a lace strap that left a mark on her shoulder, and Emil had taken time to rub the mark away with the flat of his thumb. He'd wanted her to agree the men who'd given him their money to buy into a garage wouldn't mind it going into the clubs. When she asked him what they thought they owned, he'd laughed to find a woman so eager to talk about garages when she had just taken her stockings off. The man beside the judge tonight was one of them. From the stage she could see him working a piece of cake out of his teeth with a fingernail.

Emil took a step back, very pleasantly, as if she'd said something nice about his clothes. “Money moves around in a town this size. Men who come to me with a proposition expect me to see it earns.”

“Does that man sitting beside the judge know he bought a nightclub? I could ask him. I know his name in full.”

The pianist was up delightfully high now, trying the same few notes in different places. He could do it for five minutes and she would never know where the time had gone. She took her hand off Emil's sleeve and let him see how easily she could reach the microphone.

“You'd make a fool of me in my own house, Dahlia, and for what? I put you in this room when you didn't have a second dress. I gave you leave to bring in that boy to tune the piano when my own man swore those notes couldn't be put right. Do you remember what the boy said when you were finished with him? ‘I suppose I can stay another hour.’ He stayed all day. I sent him a sandwich.” Emil wet his lips. “I'm asking what you want from me.”

“Bell Street. The club money comes to me. I want the chief to hear it from you in front of his own people.”

“If the doors are all shutting, what money are we talking about?”

“You can stay up here while I see to the doors.”

His hands opened. She remembered how well he used to hold two shoes by the backs, careful to set them both down without sending either sliding under her bed. “If you keep the clubs open and keep my partners out of your mouth,” he said, “you'll have my word in front of the chief and anybody else in the room. I'll be right here to give it to you.”

She turned back in time to take the note the piano left her. It was a lovely thing to have a note waiting for her at that particular height. She took it and went looking for the others in the spaces that opened beneath it, and the top of the piano gleamed and caught her reflection with the long black fall of her hair. By the time the trumpet came in beside her for the last phrase, Vernon had got up out of his chair. He waited until she drew her hand off the microphone to applaud. He was looking at Emil with the splendid, rather boyish insolence of a man standing next to a prize he hadn't yet been handed. Then he moved up to the stage.

“Sing the one from my old place,” he said.

She held out her hand for the pianist to wait. He took up with the blues he played whenever anybody came to the stage with something to say. “What do you mean to do when you get to that office in the morning, Vernon?”

“I’ve sent for the chief at nine. You could come down with me, if you wanted to see what an office looks like before anybody puts up a photograph. We'd have to get somebody to bring a lamp. I remember the bulb in that office being out at four in the afternoon.”

“Tell the chief to leave Bell Street alone.”

He moved closer so she could hear him. He thought it was a matter of being heard. “I had to give those men something that would still be theirs in the morning. I told them to bring me the names of the first stores they'd put in so they'd know I was talking about a real street. One of them wanted to put in a place where you can get a pair of shoes made with one foot a different size to the other. I said we could all use something that fits.” He set his hands on the edge of the stage. “I want your rooms to be as handsome as you are. I thought I’d managed to get you one.”

She swept her hair behind her. She could lean low enough for him to touch it again. “I thought we were going to take the city,” she said. “When you drew the upstairs of that house on the back of a speech, you put in a window I could look down from clear to Bell Street. I knew where to put our bed.”

“I'd still like to see where you put it.”

She had first sung the song to him in a walk-up with a stain on the ceiling that used to get the rain before the floor did. He had been running for a minor office with some chance of losing, and they lay there till past noon. It was a song she knew from after-hours on Bell Street. A woman had sung it once on a platform she could only cross sideways, and filled the pause after the first line with a sound so quiet somebody put a cigarette down halfway to his mouth. When the Dahlia tried that pause beside Vernon, he took it for an invitation. She'd wanted to hear his part again the next morning and he was a little shy of giving it to her awake and dressed. She’d sung her line until he did.

“If you walk in here tomorrow night,” she said, “you won't be able to see all the way down that street. They'll put paper in the windows before dark. Did you think what I'd sing for you when you told me?”

“I thought of that one. I could hear you sing it to me and look out anywhere you wanted. There's a house right up in the sky if you want it, with a fancy garden in the glass on the door. You can walk around in it barefoot. I don’t need the house. I needed to be able to offer it to you.” He slid one hand a long way along the stage till it was almost touching the toe of her shoe. “I want to hear my song.”

She straightened up. A man lost his silver fork from his plate and she saw him make an attempt at retrieving it with his shoe. Emil stood at the corner of the stage with the full room in view. When she lifted the microphone clear of its clip the pianist looked up past her shoulder to see if it was time for him to stop. She sang the first few notes without him. He recognized the song and set the bass on its way under her, so evenly they could have been playing all along.

“Leave a light for me,” she sang to Vernon. She had an extra breath for it. The glove on her free hand had wrinkled at the wrist where his fingers had held it and she felt an absurd wish to fix the wrinkle before she did anything else. If she smoothed it she could keep her hand busy clear through the start of the song. She could sing him anything else he wanted and go out later, without Emil, and see if Vernon remembered the street as they'd walk it home. He would still come down to his office in the morning and send his word to the police. If she went home with him he might put his head on her breast while he waited to hear that the doors had shut.

Here came the part where she left him time. He took the breath she knew to expect in that place. It pleased her that he would say it here with the whole room looking, as though they'd left the two of them in that small room for seven years and got up to find them exactly where they had been. She had never quite understood that about a live audience. She could have loved the whole lot of them for waiting.

She wanted him to know she was looking at him. She found his eyes and moved her fingers. There was time, while the bass came up to meet her again, to see Vernon look at her glove and understand her. His mouth lost the shape it had been making for her. He seemed about to say her name. Instead he got a foot sideways and started to turn, and the waiter with the tray put it down to catch him.

She came in on the next phrase. The waiter had got both hands into the shoulders of Vernon's beautiful suit and the whole weight of him was bearing down through those two handfuls of cloth. A woman at the next table went on smiling for the time it took to see the waiter try and get his knee under him. Emil walked down from the stage as if about to go along the aisle. Then he saw the waiter losing hold, and turned back to pull Vernon's chair out clear of the table. He held the head up himself while the waiter got the body down into the seat. Behind them there was the scrape of the chair leg catching the carpet. A little piece of her voice went through it as plainly as it had gone through his waistcoat.

The piano took the song for a while. Emil bent to put Vernon's collar straight. The chief of police came over and put his fingers at Vernon's throat. Somebody asked if he wanted a doctor.

“Ask the doctor to finish his supper,” the chief said. “Mr. Saye’s dead.”

Emil kept his hand on the back of the chair. His suit had gone a little crooked where he had stooped. He would have stopped in the glass by the front door to see about that if this had been any other night. “Chief, I wish you'd listen to the lady on the stage,” he said. “I've given her my word about the rooms on Bell Street.”

They were looking at Emil even now. She brought the microphone up. “The rooms will open tomorrow,” she said. “Everybody who's been paying Mr. Corbett to stay open can pay me.”

The chief looked up at her, then along the row of tables. “I’ll get word to the precincts in the morning.”

“Tonight, please. They can tell the owners to go ahead and put their supper on.” She let the chief go far enough down the aisle to see the men move their chairs to let him by. Only the waiters could ordinarily get the length of that aisle with nobody after them for a word.

She found a faint sweetness left in the back of her mouth that even the long breaths hadn't worn away. Vernon would have eaten a little of the dry cake underneath the icing if he’d had time. He had always liked the parts she left over. With his head at that angle she could see the cut beside his mouth from where she was standing. The lamp he had put on their table was still on. A waiter brought a hand up to the switch and Emil, watching her, touched him on the cuff to stop him.

The piano came back to the place where she could get into the song again. By then the men who had been looking to Vernon had turned their chairs toward her. She gave them the first line and left them all the time she'd given him to answer. Nobody sang in his place.
