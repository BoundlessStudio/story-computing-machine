# The Dedication

Vernon Saye, the new mayor of Merrow, carried a piece of his election cake through the velvet and asked the Black Dahlia to take a bite for luck. She already had her long black gloves on. He cut a piece with the edge of the fork and held it up for her. There was apricot jam under the icing, and she took her time with it because he was watching. He put down the plate and drew his handkerchief for a fleck of icing on the red of her mouth. Her tongue got it first. She took hold of his lapel and pulled him down to kiss him. It pleased her that he couldn't put the handkerchief away without taking his mouth away first, so that he had to stand where she wanted him with the square of linen dangling in his hand.

Out front the waiters were collecting the knives. The trumpet player sat in shirtsleeves on a kitchen chair, blowing through his horn for warmth. Plates were going past him into the kitchen and the hot smell of onions was coming out the other way. Through a join in the curtains she could see Emil Corbett in his usual place, holding his plate out level for a second piece of cake. He ate that way in company, slowly and with a little appetite left over. She had promised Vernon that Emil would die during the set.

She leaned back against a brass post and tipped her head for Vernon to lift the hair off her neck. The brass laid a streak of cold down the bare skin between her shoulders.

“Leave a light for me,” she sang into his waistcoat, warming her voice.

“I can wait.” That was the answer he'd made up to her sung line. He could sing well enough with a radio choir when there were votes in it. With her he spoke his part in the voice he'd been given.

She had got Vernon's first office for him from the other side of this curtain. He liked to tell her that he'd seen her hand move from all the way in the back. Right after, he'd watched the alderman's wife bring her napkin up to catch her husband's chin when his head fell. The alderman had been eating lemon pie. Now Emil had cake crumbs in the turn of his black cuff and Vernon was breathing against the spot where she had put on her perfume, taking his time with it.

“I've got you something,” he said. “You're going to have the Oriole.”

She caught his hand before he could put her hair back. “Keep talking. I was promised a whole street of Oriole Rooms.”

“I want you to have it out of my hand. You come down to the office tomorrow and we'll see to it. We'll put your name up the length of the building if you want it up there. Or we can take the signs down altogether and never let another soul in here till you feel like it. I don't suppose anybody's ever made you that offer.”

Out front another toast went up for Vernon and ran out of strength about halfway back. The alderman used to have the whole room with him when they toasted him. Everybody knew you did some extra drinking at the Oriole so Emil would see how well you were enjoying what you bought from him. Leave a glass full too long and he sent a fresh one.

“I was reckoning on the rooms down Bell Street,” she said. “The clubs were going to pay me what they pay Emil. You'd get what the bookmakers and all the rest pay him. The police could take their time getting to Bell Street if anybody complained how late the music went.”

He smoothed a place on the velvet with the back of his hand. Now she was going to hear about the glass on their new kitchen door or the window at the end of the bath. He had a white house picked out up above the tram depot and had drawn her plans of the rooms on the backs of his speeches. In his plans there was always another window.

“I went down Bell Street this summer, honey. It was daylight and you could see the men leaning out to shake their sheets. I saw a drummer you know put half a pound of butter on his windowsill to keep cool. I came back uptown that afternoon and sat down with some men who offered me a way to win. They had a whole new street laid out. Storefronts as wide as this room. Flats with breakfast rooms in them, if you can conceive of that. I've given them Bell Street, and they want to start with empty blocks. We'll close the clubs tomorrow.”

He said it into her hair. If she kept still she could have a little more of his mouth where it was.

“Did you tell them what you’d promised me before you promised them Bell Street?”

“You could sing on the worst night in February and clear more in here than you’d get out of the lot of them in a month.” He took her by the shoulders to make sure she had heard the best part. “Remember the night you did for the alderman? When I came over to you I had lemon all over my fingers. I thought I could disguise the fact that I was shaking if I kept on eating. You gave me a napkin and asked what I wanted. I said I wanted his office all the way down to the hat rack. I thought it would feel good hanging up my hat where he'd left his. And it did, for about the length of a morning. Now I want a corner of the city I can shut the door on, with you on the same side of the door.”

She could see how such a night would go. She'd sing and he would fetch ice and always return, which was a pretty good trick for a man in office. There were women she knew who would pay to spend a winter in a house where he came back every night. If he listened with his eyes half shut she might sing a bridge twice, to hear what happened in her throat the second time.

At the end of the Oriole's night the iceman started his rounds down Bell Street, just when the last sets were getting good. At the Pelican, six doors from the bakery, a singer had to stand still if she wanted both heels on the platform. The Pelican got so hot she’d seen a woman take off her wig and hold it in her lap through a ballad. The Dahlia had been going to buy the empty shop next door and give the band room for another horn. She had a player picked out, a man who could take a solo till the ice all went to water.

“Emil will want to know about Bell Street,” she said. She'd have to find that man with the good horn and tell him to take the other job he'd been kind enough to turn down. She could tell him what a fine thing a Saturday night off would be. She couldn't imagine telling him with his back turned, and Vernon had managed to tell her with his mouth against her neck.

“Let him ask. Come sit with me when you're done. I've got our lamp.” He squeezed her wrist and took hold of the curtain where her hand would have gone. Beyond the heavy crimson fold the pianist was already playing.

For the first few steps the light was in her eyes and she could smell the wool of someone's wet coat. She knew the last board by the way it gave under her toe, and set her foot so the slit in the black satin would fall open over her thigh. The bass came up through the thin soles of her shoes. When she reached for the ribbed head of the microphone and put the palm of her other hand out, the talk dropped so fast she heard a piece of ice settle in a glass.

She took the first number on the bright side. Her first low note had a grain to it. She kept that sound on a word whenever she wanted a man to think it had come out rough because he'd got her to say it. She let the words get almost away from the bass and brought the two in together at the end of the line, so that the people who had heard the tune a hundred times raised their heads to see where she had got it from. The trumpet answered with a figure she could sing back to him. She did it twice and the second time he gave her back the first figure with the middle taken out. Still chewing his cake, Emil Corbett laughed. That was the right place to laugh. There were men all over the room she could have paid to laugh where she wanted them to, and Emil could still do it for nothing.

She looked down on the people Emil had seated for Vernon. He had put two men who hated each other at the same table, with their wives between them, and a judge with painful gout facing the stairs he was going to have to go up. The chief of police had the place beside Emil. On certain nights the chief used the Oriole as a second office. A permit cost more here than at the station, but it came to the table before dessert. One night a freight man had asked her in bed what the chief would charge to leave his drivers alone for a week. She'd given him a figure. The freight man had thought she was being funny.

She gave the song to the piano. Emil was rising, and she beckoned him to the apron. A few weeks after he'd bought the piano, he'd had the stage raised three inches so he could see the singer's shoes from his chair. He walked up the steps tonight with his head thrown back, listening for a note he knew was coming. She moved away from the microphone to meet him at the edge of the light and laid a glove on his sleeve.

“The door sticks when it's wet,” Emil said. “The doorman you like has been going out to let people in all night. His feet are soaked. I pay him to go out there so we don't leave your people on the wrong side of the door.”

“I was going to ask you to move him to the Pelican. It'd be easier on his shoes. Vernon gave Bell Street to his backers and the police are going to shut the clubs tomorrow.”

Emil looked at the slit in her dress and then at her face. “I've got money in those rooms, Dahlia.”

“So does Mr. Lyle.” She looked straight at the man beside the judge. “Shall I ask him how he likes his nightclubs?”

Emil's cheek moved as though he still had something to chew. One November afternoon she'd invited him up to her place and asked him to stay till dark. Afterward they lay bare under the sheet and when Emil crossed an ankle over hers she felt the weight of him through the length of her leg. She put his hand back on her shoulder where the strap of her slip had left its mark and asked how much a man needed in his pocket to buy a room down Bell Street. Composing himself to name a quantity, he'd drawn the sheet up to his chin. She asked where he'd got that much himself. He'd taken money from men who thought they were buying a garage. It had bought him clubs on Bell Street and he thought those men ought to be glad of what he'd done with it. She asked whether she knew any of them. He laughed at a woman who wanted to talk about garages when she'd just taken her stockings off, and told her their names. From the stage she could see Mr. Lyle working a piece of cake out of his teeth with a fingernail.

Emil took a step back, very pleasantly, as if she'd said something nice about his clothes. “Money moves around in a town this size. Men who come to me with a proposition expect me to see it earns.”

The pianist was up high now, trying the same three notes in different places. She took her hand off Emil's sleeve and let him see how easily she could reach the microphone.

“You'd make a fool of me in my own house, Dahlia, and for what? I put you in this room when you didn't have a second dress. I gave you leave to bring in that boy to tune the piano when my own man swore those notes couldn't be put right. Do you remember what the boy said when you were finished with him? ‘I suppose I can stay another hour.’ He stayed all day. I sent him a sandwich.” Emil wet his lips. “I'm asking what you want from me.”

“I'm going to keep the clubs open. I want what they pay you, and I want the chief to hear it from you.”

“What are you going to do about Vernon?”

“You can stay up here and see.”

He looked down at Vernon's table. His hands opened. She remembered how well he used to hold two shoes by the backs, careful to set them both down without sending either sliding under her bed. “If you keep the clubs open and keep my partners out of your mouth,” he said, “you'll have my word in front of the chief and anybody else in the room. I'll be right here to give it to you.”

She turned back in time to take the note the piano left her. It was a lovely thing to have a note waiting for her at that particular height. She took it and went looking for the others in the spaces that opened beneath it, and the top of the piano gleamed and caught her reflection with the long black fall of her hair. By the time the trumpet came in beside her for the last phrase, Vernon had got up out of his chair. He clapped when she took her hand off the microphone. His eyes were on Emil, and even up where she stood she could see him trying to keep his grin down. Then he moved up to the stage.

“Sing the one you sang in my old walk-up,” he said.

She held out her hand for the pianist to wait. He took up with the blues he played whenever anybody came to the stage with something to say. “What do you mean to do when you get to that office in the morning, Vernon?”

“I’ve sent for the chief at nine. You could come down with me, if you wanted to see what an office looks like before anybody puts up a photograph. We'd have to get somebody to bring a lamp. I remember the bulb in that office being out at four in the afternoon.”

“I'll come down to your office at nine, Vernon. I'll find a bulb for your lamp and listen to what you owe those men. They can have stores bigger than this room if they've got the money for them. There are other streets. I want you to tell the chief to leave the rooms on Bell Street alone. You could tell him now, while we're all here.”

He moved closer so she could hear him. He thought it was a matter of being heard. “I had to give those men something that would still be theirs in the morning. I told them to bring me the names of the first stores they'd put in so they'd know I was talking about a real street. One of them wanted to put in a place where you can get a pair of shoes made with one foot a different size to the other. I said we could all use something that fits.” He set his hands on the edge of the stage. “I want your rooms to be as handsome as you are. I thought I’d managed to get you one.”

He turned his head so the chief could see he wanted a word. The chief laid his spoon across his plate. “Give the club owners on Bell Street the morning to get their things out,” Vernon told him. “We can have the doors closed by supper.”

The chief stood up and said he would see to it. He was good at getting to his feet in a way that made a man feel his business had already been done.

She swept her hair behind her. She could lean low enough for him to touch it again. “I thought we were going to take the city,” she said. “When you drew the upstairs of that house on the back of a speech, you put in a window I could look down from clear to Bell Street. I knew where to put our bed.”

“I'd still like to see where you put it.”

She had first sung the song to him seven years ago in his walk-up, with the stain on the ceiling that used to get the rain before the floor did. He had been running for a minor office with some chance of losing. One morning they stayed in bed past noon and by the time they made love the stove had gone out. She sang afterward with his bare chest warm against her back and his arm lying heavy across her ribs. It was a song she knew from after-hours on Bell Street. A woman had sung it once on a platform she could only cross sideways, and filled the pause after the first line with a sound so quiet somebody put a cigarette down halfway to his mouth. When the Dahlia gave him that pause she felt his breath come in against the skin below her ear a beat before he said his line. The next morning he went shy of giving it to her awake and dressed. She’d sung her line until he did.

“Suppose you and I walk down Bell Street tomorrow,” she said. “They'll put paper in the windows before dark. You could come in here every night for the rest of your life and never once have to look at those windows. Did you think what I'd sing for you when you told me?”

“I thought of that one. I could hear you singing it to me by the window. There's a house right up in the sky if you want it, with a fancy garden in the glass on the door. You can go upstairs barefoot and see the whole city.” He slid one hand a long way along the stage till it was almost touching the toe of her shoe. “I want to hear my song.”

She straightened up. A man at the table next to Vernon's lost his silver fork from his plate and tried to get it back with his shoe. A waiter came up to him, holding a tray of empties. Emil stood at the corner of the stage with the full room in view. When she lifted the microphone clear of its clip the pianist looked up past her shoulder to see if it was time for him to stop. She sang the first few notes without him. He recognized the song and set the bass on its way under her, so evenly they could have been playing all along.

“Leave a light for me,” she sang. She let the rough edge go out of her voice until it was as soft as speech through the cloth of a waistcoat. The glove on her free hand had wrinkled at the wrist where Vernon's fingers had held it and she felt an absurd wish to fix the wrinkle before she did anything else. If she smoothed it she could keep her hand busy clear through the start of the song. She could give the order for Emil and go home with Vernon and see if he remembered the street as they walked it home. He would still go to his office in the morning to make sure the chief had done as he said. He might put his head on her breast while he waited to hear that the doors had shut.

Here came the part where she left him time. Vernon took the breath she always felt first when they were in bed. He was looking at Emil as he took it, waiting to see what she was going to give him besides the song. She had liked that look on him when it had been the alderman's turn. He brought his eyes right back to her with his mouth already opening. She could have loved the whole room for waiting on him like that.

She wanted him to know she'd chosen him. She raised her open hand, felt the glove pull tight across her knuckles, and moved her fingers. There was time, while the bass came up to meet her again, to see Vernon look at the glove and lose the shape his mouth had made for her. He took his hand off the stage so fast he left a mark in the dust. Emil straightened in the light and watched him get a foot sideways. The waiter put his tray down to catch him.

She came in on the next phrase. The waiter had got both hands into the shoulders of Vernon's beautiful suit and the whole weight of him was bearing down through those two handfuls of cloth. A woman at the next table went on smiling for the time it took to see the waiter try and get his knee under him. Emil came down the steps and started along the aisle. Then he saw the waiter losing hold. He turned back, carried Vernon's chair from the table, and set it behind the waiter. Emil held the head up himself while the waiter got the body down into the seat. Behind them there was the scrape of the chair leg catching the carpet. A little piece of her voice went through it as plainly as it had gone through Vernon's waistcoat.

The piano took the song. She watched Emil try to put Vernon's collar straight, and the head kept falling toward his hand in the way it used to fall when she touched Vernon's neck and he wanted her to go on touching it. The chief came over and put his fingers at the throat Emil was trying to cover. Somebody at the table asked if he wanted a doctor.

“Ask the doctor to finish his supper,” the chief said. “Mr. Saye’s dead.”

Emil kept his hand on the back of the chair. His suit had gone crooked where he had stooped. He would have stopped in the glass by the front door to see about that if this had been any other night. “Chief, I wish you'd listen to the lady on the stage,” he said. “I've given her my word about the rooms on Bell Street.”

The men at Vernon's table were still looking at Emil. She brought the microphone up. “The rooms will open tomorrow,” she said. “Everybody who's been paying Mr. Corbett to stay open can pay me.”

The chief looked up at her, then along the row of tables. “I’ll get word to the precincts in the morning.”

“Tonight, please. They can tell the owners to go ahead and put their supper on.” The chief went straight for the telephone. At the first table, one of the men raising money to clear Bell Street stood and pushed his chair all the way under the table.

She found a faint sweetness left in the back of her mouth that even the long breaths hadn't worn away. Vernon would have eaten a little of the dry cake underneath the icing if he’d had time. He had always liked the parts she left over. A fold of his handkerchief still stood above his breast pocket. She could see as much from where she stood, with the lamp he had put on their table still on. A waiter brought a hand up to the switch and Emil, watching her, touched him on the cuff to stop him.

The piano came back to the place where she could get into the song again. By then the men who had been looking to Vernon had turned their chairs toward her. She gave them the first line and left them all the time she'd given him to answer. Nobody sang in his place.
