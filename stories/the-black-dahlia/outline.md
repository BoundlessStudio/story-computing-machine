# The Dedication

## Promised experience

The Black Dahlia is the most dangerous woman in a city run by mobsters and crooked politicians. She has spent years getting powerful men to give her secrets through desire and intimacy. The distinctive pleasure is watching a jazz singer use a glance or a nearly unnoticeable movement to decide who dies, in full view beneath crimson velvet, without losing her place in the song. The image suggests sensual confidence, an amused command of the room, and a night that can turn vicious without losing its glamour. The city's rulers make the stakes wider than the stage. “up the rating for this story.” calls for greater intensity. Adult desire, complicity, and the immediate consequences of a killing can give it Mature weight. The request leaves her relationships, motives, precise means, and the outcome open.

## Takes considered

- **The succession set, chosen.** On an election night, two men expect the Dahlia to finish a deal she helped make. Her dedication claims a greater share of the city and costs her the one man she would have liked to share it with. The long scene lets her improvise as a singer and a power in the room at once.
- **The night of the request.** A crooked judge in the audience realizes his lover has already decided to kill him and tries to buy one last song. The encounter at the lip of the stage could be intimate and frightening. His dread would carry the story and leave less space for the pleasure she takes in ruling the room.
- **The voices on Shellac.** Years after the Dahlia disappears, a pianist staying late with the last surviving master hears the private voices of men dead during that show. The old set plays out as he uses the recording to decide whom to protect. The uncanny listening scene makes the old secrets live again, with her decisive performance at one remove.

## Provisional direction

In the lake city of Merrow, crooked mayor-elect Vernon Saye and nightclub boss Emil Corbett owe their standing to the Dahlia, and each supposes her influence ends where his begins. Tonight's election party at Corbett's Oriole Room is to double as a private transfer of power. The Dahlia has agreed to remove Corbett during the set and let Saye inherit his protection business. She expects to take charge of the city's clubs as her own part of the deal. Saye, her lover as well as her ally, makes the offer he thinks will bring her closer to him. He'll give her the Oriole outright. He has already promised the rest of the rooms will close, clearing their blocks for his backers. He wants her to keep her bargain and imagines they'll have evenings together while ordinary men go about clearing the doors of the clubs where she learned to sing.

She goes on and opens with a song both men know. She uses what Corbett once told her in bed, that he took money held in trust for his own partners to buy those threatened clubs, to make him stay and listen to a new bargain. She can keep his theft private and his partners earning, and he can put his club men and their paid police at her disposal. Saye, watching Corbett approach the stage in apparent tribute, thinks she is carrying out the agreement. He asks for the song they have kept private. At the edge of the stage she tells him plainly what he offered and what she wanted from him. She gives him that song and, with a small motion while she sings, chooses his death. The act is seen through recognition and aftermath. Corbett sees her perform the promise she just made him. He yields in front of the people he used to order, and the men who came to hear who would close the clubs hear her say they will open.

## Craft plan

**Teller.** Try the Dahlia close in third person, in the moment and alert to everything a singer must do to own the room, against her first-person account told later, expansive, seductive, and exacting about what other people claim happened. Give her pleasure in the music itself and time to want her lover while she decides.

**Cast.** The Dahlia wants her share of the city and wants Saye to know singing is what drew her to it. She has taken his delight in her voice for an understanding of what the other rooms mean to her. She prolongs a sentence when she likes watching someone wait and can speak very plainly when she wants an adult answer. Saye wants her to honor their agreement and wants a private life with the woman who has heard him at his worst. He knows what he promised the backers and mistakes exclusivity for generosity. A campaign speaker, he paints the whole happy future and takes pains over a small domestic detail when he reaches the part he fears she'll refuse. Corbett wants to keep his place and his partners' trust. He knows what he stole and has mistaken her years of discretion for loyalty. He addresses an adversary as a paying guest and counts what she owes him in remembered things, down to who paid to fix the piano. The night costs each of the men the answer he expected from an ally.

**Casting from the original.** The adult singer in the source has long black hair, wine-dark lips, long black opera gloves, and a reflective black gown slit high, over heels. Her left glove rests near the ribbed microphone, and the open right hand looks as easy as the lifted face. She offers, listens, holds, and dares. Keep her commanding and visibly enjoying the gaze. The hair has to be swept clear when she leans down to speak to Saye, who once swept it back for her in private. The lit line of a leg already makes front-row men lean toward the stage. The gloved hand starts as a musician's generous invitation and ends the private song with a meaning Saye understands. The band belongs behind her, watching her and playing to her, with the brass and piano catching light in the folds of the crimson curtains.

**The Oriole Room.** Choose it over the Lake Crown Hotel ballroom, which would give political people the advantage of an official party, and Chaffin's Cellar, which would lose the ceremonial visibility. At the Oriole, politicians and their racketeers have to come to the singer's platform to speak privately, within sight of everyone they want to impress. Near midnight in November, wet wool and fried onions crowd the air, the carpet dies at the raised stage, cuff links tick on glass, shaded lamps make bowls of light on the tables, and the brass trim is cold enough to raise bumps on a bare shoulder. Waiters know whose supper is a standing order. The first few tables can hear the unamplified singer during a piano break, while the rest of the room only gets what she gives the microphone. Ground the singer's physical business and the late show in the period material. ## Scene briefs

1. **At the curtain, one fifth.** The last dinner tables are being cleared. She wants the license arrangement from Saye. He wants her to see her name on the Oriole and picture going home together after her show. Let him tell her how he promised to empty the rest of the clubs and what he believes he's bought for them with that promise, in his own unhurried vocabulary. She goes on with their plan already changing. Hold the image of his hand lifting the red velvet for her.
2. **The set, three fifths.** The long scene includes the conversation owed. Saye and the Dahlia have to find out what they actually promised one another, both out of bed and in it. Piano breaks make room for talk and keep demanding she return to the song. Corbett wants her to swear she will keep the theft private. She wants him to say aloud who can give his men orders when the set ends. Saye comes up to ask for their private song, expecting to share the night with her after Corbett dies. Let them speak of an evening they both remember and differ about what it meant. She sings to him and chooses his death in the space where she normally gives him the next phrase. He has time to recognize it's his song. Stay with her pleasure in his listening, the temptation to let him hear the finish, and her choice. Her music continues through the moment the closest table understands he is dead.
3. **The return, one fifth.** The chief of police is among the guests. Corbett has to yield his hold on him in public. The Dahlia wants the men who have been awaiting Saye's closing orders to ask her what to do. She gives the new order from the microphone and starts another number. Summarize the old confidences through one unhurried memory in the set. Leave the killing's means, the campaign, and the machinery of the next administration outside the frame.

**Ending.** Let the dead man's table lamp keep burning as the others turn their chairs to take their instruction from the stage. She opens the next song and leaves the space in it where Saye used to murmur the first line back to her. His willingness to listen in private, his attempt to buy the last room in which she could sing, and the dedication he wanted load that space. The people listening now need her alive and are afraid of whom she might sing to next.
