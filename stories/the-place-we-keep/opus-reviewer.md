# Opus review: The Place We Keep

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the prompt's hinge question is muddled, and Father's decisive answer closes on a tense correction plus an aphorism the narration then endorses.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, varied and well staged; only small object and staging slips.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — every prompt element lands, length is in band (about 3,250 words), and no internal or universe contradictions.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; I agree for most exchanges and disagree on the central question and Father's answer to it.

This story mostly avoids the collection's house voice. Selvi talks like a
sleep-deprived new mother (sleep, eggs, "Could someone move the fish?"), Father
is sentimental, proud and wrong about his memories, Mother is literal and
enjoys her body, and several exchanges turn on real misreadings ("I don't mean
the house") instead of neat rulings. The failures cluster at the hinge. The
prompt-required question arrives as a three-part abstraction with a dangling
"it". Father's answer then wins the point by correcting her tense and ends on a
thematic epigram, and "That stopped me" endorses it. The edit should let the
narrator's real fear about Ibby leak into the question and should make Father's
dodge of that fear visible, because L263–L273 depend on the question still
being open. Everything else is polish: two pronouns that resolve late, an
ambiguous "birds", two reflexive retorts, a few narrator glosses and a
metronome of "Mother said".

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt elements that must survive: Father in an iron body, Selvi living in the river, Mother who *prefers* being a cat (she can still change; L185), the annual eclipse at the ruined home, Selvi arriving with a child, and the narrator explicitly asking her parents what being human means. The narrator is the only one who still looks like her baby photograph:
> I was the only one of us whose baby picture remained much use for identification.
- First person, past tense; 2,500–4,000 words (currently about 3,250); broadly accessible and non-graphic. Breastfeeding stays matter-of-fact as it is now. Craft profile prospective-2026-08-23.
- Universe: `rules.md` "Living agency and magic" (LOCKED, only life can use magic). Father's living core works the iron by magic (L23); keep that. `style-guide.md` "Personhood and moral consequence" (LOCKED): no body form may reduce anyone's personhood. "No default water infrastructure" (CANON): the river stays a river.
- Story facts the edits depend on: Ibby was born liquid and Selvi did not change her; Ibby's father was born in the river; Selvi cannot leave the water or hear from the windowsill; Mother took the six-month photo while Father was away with his brother; Father asked for the photo last year; Mother stayed in the house eight years after the daughters left; the birthday toothache and the dancing afterwards; Ibby turns four months on the ninth; Selvi lives eight miles away.
- What works structurally: Mother corrects the narrator's assumptions until the narrator starts asking open questions ("What did you like?", "What did you do?") and Mother tells stories instead. Keep that arc. Keep the ending on an action with no stated moral.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Hinge question is muddled and not rooted in the moment

- Where: L121–L125, the riverbank, just after Father's "Our chin"
- Text:
> "Our chin," Father said happily.
> "What does being human mean?" I asked them.
> "If she has our chin, does that make her human? Or if she doesn't? What do you mean when you say it?"
- Problem: Ground/referent at the prompt's hinge. The first sentence copies the prompt's wording almost exactly, as if a seminar question had been dropped into a riverbank scene. "Or if she doesn't?" leaves the reader to fill in its missing verb. "What do you mean when you say it?" could mean "human", which neither parent has said on the page, or "our chin", which Father has just said. The reader stalls twice. The narrator's actual fear is that Ibby might not be "still" one of them (L79 "changed her", L265 "Whether she's still—"), and it is missing from the question, so the parents answer an abstraction.
- Fix: Keep an explicit question to the parents about what being human means, because the prompt requires it. Root it in what she has just looked at: Father's "Our chin", his fixed iron face, Mother's whiskers. Use one question and one concrete follow-up. Cut "Or if she doesn't?" and the dangling "it". Father's L127 reply ("She's your sister's child. Of course she's human.") must still follow. Illustrative only: "What does being human mean, then?" I asked them. "Is it the chin? She's got it, and neither of you has."

### D2 · Major · Decisive answer turns on a tense correction and a thematic last word

- Where: L137–L145, the riverbank, Father's answer about himself
- Text:
> "I'm human," he said. "This doesn't alter that."
> "You remember being a man," I said. "She won't."
> "I am a man," Father said. "I don't spend all day remembering it."
> That stopped me.
- Problem: Correction ping-pong, the Philosopher and a narration gloss, at the decisive exchange. The narrator's line reads as if built so Father can correct "being" to "am". His second sentence states the story's theme (she lives in memory, he lives now) as a polished comeback, and "That stopped me" confirms it landed. Nowhere else does Father talk like this. "She won't" copies the grammar of "remember being a man", which can't apply to Ibby, so the reader has to rebuild "she'll have no earlier body to remember". Father then drops the Ibby half without the text noticing, so the reader can't tell whether he dodged it or won the argument. L263–L265 ("That's what I'm trying to ask about") only works if he dodged it.
- Fix: Father already says he is human at L137. Keep at most one plain assertion from him. Rewrite the narrator's challenge so it is about Ibby, not his tense. Cut "I don't spend all day remembering it" and "That stopped me." Show him not answering the Ibby part: a look at Ibby or the photograph, then the existing "He eased himself down" beat and the illness speech at L147, which is concrete and answers well. Illustrative only: "You had years looking like me first," I said. "She never will."

### D3 · Minor · "Those birds" points at the wrong birds

- Where: L203–L219, the birthday photograph
- Text:
> Selvi and I wore strings of birds over our dresses. I had made them from the thin paper used between prints.
> We had danced round the table until we tore our paper garlands on the chair backs.
> "I thought you'd make those birds forever," I told Mother.
- Problem: Ground/referent. The sentence just before the line is about the paper garlands, which the narrator says she made herself. The first reading of "those birds" is therefore the paper birds, and crediting Mother with them looks like a continuity slip. Mother's reply (wings, icing) is what shows that she means the sugar birds on the cake.
- Fix: Name the object in the line ("those sugar birds" or "the cake birds"). No other change is needed.

### D4 · Minor · Two pronouns resolve late or to the wrong person

- Where: L137–L139, Father's chest; L277, Ibby's laugh
- Text:
> "I'm human," he said. "This doesn't alter that."
> He tapped his chest, gently because Mother disliked the sound.
> "For him," Selvi said. "Apparently I'm not funny."
- Problem: Ground/referent. "This" only resolves when the tap comes after it. At L277, the last "him" Selvi used (L263, "She'll know him like this") was Father, so "For him" first reads as Father. Then Father chuckles and Ibby stares at him instead of laughing, which seems to contradict it until the reader works out that she means Ibby's father.
- Fix: Put the tap before or inside Father's line. Change "For him" to "For her dad" or "For her father".

### D5 · Minor · Mother's first two lines are reflexive retorts

- Where: L17–L19, the windowsill; L33–L37, the fish
- Text:
> Mother opened an eye. "Then don't put them under my feet."
> "Every year," I said.
> "Yes, but I don't know that till you arrive," Mother said.
- Problem: Correction ping-pong as a habit. Before any correction carries a revelation, Mother's first two exchanges are logic retorts that change nothing. L33–L37 is a three-line loop of small adjustments ("You remembered" / "Every year" / "Yes, but…"). Later corrections (toothache, invented names, "I tried") are the story's engine. They land harder if her first lines don't already sound like a debating habit.
- Fix: Roughen one or both. Mother can respond with appetite or body (reaching for the fish, not moving at all) instead of a reasoned reply, or L37 can be cut so "You remembered" stands. Do not touch the later corrections listed under Preserve.

### D6 · Minor · Narration explains lines the reader already understands

- Where: L121, L195, L225, L319
- Text:
> "Our chin," Father said happily.
> I stroked her between the ears, in the place she liked; it was a small contact and I had asked for a large one.
> It was close enough to agreement that I tried to leave it there.
> "I want to know about the hedges," I said, although part of me still wanted to know when she was coming back.
- Problem: Glosses and an adverb tag. Each sentence tells the reader what the action has already shown: the cat stepping onto her leg after "It's easier to hug you"; the narrator pressing "Look how happy you are"; her choosing the hedges over the return. "Happily" does the job that a beat could do. The narrator's first-person reflection elsewhere (L191, L243, L251, L305) earns its place; these four do not.
- Fix: Drop "happily" or replace it with a beat, such as him bending closer. End L195 at "in the place she liked." Cut the L225 sentence and go straight to "Look how happy you are." End the L319 line at "I said."

### D7 · Minor · Tag on almost every line in two-person runs

- Where: L167–L197 and L287–L315, the narrator and Mother alone
- Text:
> "That part's enjoying the blanket too," Mother said.
> "Well, there you are," Mother said.
- Problem: The story carries 25 "Mother said" and about 30 "I said/asked" tags. In stretches where only the narrator and Mother talk, a tag on every line creates a metronome that feels like a tennis match (L181, L185, L299, L303, L309, L315).
- Fix: In those two-person alternations, drop roughly half the tags where the previous line is already tagged or has a beat. Keep every tag after a narration paragraph and wherever Father or Selvi has just spoken. Do not strip tags from the four-person passages (L93–L133, L207–L213, L263–L281, L343–L359).

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Small staging and object slips

- Where: L21, the windowsill greeting; L53, first sight of Ibby
- Text:
> The iron had been warming all the way down the hill.
> She settled with her chin between two rivets, and he lifted his other hand to embrace me.
> I had been imagining how Selvi would keep a woollen shawl dry.
- Problem: Nothing establishes who came "down the hill" or when, so the reader can't place the warming. People embrace with arms, not a hand. "Shawl" reads as a second object when the story has just called it the baby blanket (L31), and could be read as something for Selvi to wear.
- Fix: Anchor the warming, for example in the sun all morning. Use "arm". Call it "the blanket" or "a woollen blanket".

## Recurring patterns

- Correction and reframing rhythm: about 12 narrator-assumption / Mother-reframe pairs (L19, L37, L177, L181, L223, L227, L245, L299, L309, L315), plus Father at L143. This is mostly the story's intended engine. Only D2 (L143) and D5 (L19, L37) need work.
- Narration glossing subtext: L145, L195, L225, L319 (D2, D6).
- Tag metronome: "Mother said" 25 times; it is densest in the two-person runs (D7).
- Late or misleading referents: L137, L217, L277 (D3, D4).

## Preserve

- Selvi's barbed welcome, and the later warmth over the leaves:
> "Your aunt has brought you something you can't use. Say thank you anyway."
- The suspicion and silence around Ibby's birth:
> "I thought perhaps you had changed her," I said.
- The sleep exchange and Father's "With our first" retreat under Mother's look:
> Last night I woke him up to tell him she was asleep."
- The sisters' real grievance, with the unravelled border as the unspoken excuse:
> "You could have visited," Selvi said, without looking at me.
- Father's comic misreading and Selvi's irritation. It is play, not a ruling, and Selvi's "Ask about her father" (L273) picks it back up:
> "I know he's decent," Selvi said. "I live with him."
- Father's illness speech, with its concrete wants:
> I wasn't deciding to become some new thing. I was scared I'd die before the work was finished.
- The pear and blue-cover memory tangle (L149–L163), including "Then perhaps".
- Mother's "Are you human?" run. The wit is earned and it turns on a real question:
> "There isn't a woman tucked under my fur."
> "Yes. I was one on Tuesday," Mother said. "We played cards. I lost rather a lot."
> "Did you want me to be?"
- The birthday banter (L207–L213) and the toothache revelation (L227).
- The genuine missed contact:
> "I don't mean the house," I said.
> "Then I don't know what you mean," Mother said.
- "You never asked." / "I did… Last year." (L257–L259).
- Selvi's refusal, which answers the unfinished word:
> "Still what?" Selvi asked. "She's only just got here."
- Mother's plainest line of love:
> "I liked you very much when you could find your own socks. I like you now."
- The eclipse beat:
> "Yes," Selvi told her. "It does that."
- The final exchange and the closing line, with no moral added:
> "I didn't marry your feet," Mother said.
> She stood up on my knees to show us.

## Suggested edit order

1. D1: fix the hinge question first; every answer after it depends on what she actually asks.
2. D2: rebuild Father's reply so he dodges Ibby visibly instead of winning with a correction; check that L263–L265 still reads as her returning to an unanswered question.
3. D3, D4: name the sugar birds, move the chest tap, and change "For him".
4. D5: roughen the two opening retorts so Mother's later corrections read as revelations.
5. D6, D7: trim the glosses and redundant tags last, reading the two-person runs aloud.
6. P1: staging polish.
