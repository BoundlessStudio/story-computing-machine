# Outline

## Story

- Premise and central promise: The Place We Keep. At the annual eclipse reunion in their ruined childhood home, a river-born baby makes the narrator ask her transformed parents what being human means. Deliver conflicting answers through family history.
- Focal pressure or attachment: The unnamed narrator brings baby photographs every year. She cherishes the family she remembers and feels responsible for preserving their common past.
- Counterforce or complication: Selvi's infant daughter has never possessed a flesh body. Father still considers himself human; Mother considers herself a cat. Neither description settles what the baby is, and the narrator's affectionate recollections misrepresent some experiences.
- POV, distance, and information limit: First-person past, close to embarrassment, curiosity, and possessiveness. Others' motives emerge through speech and behavior; no universal answer.
- Governing movement and time shape: One afternoon, from arrivals through totality into returning light. Conversation circles a few photographs and expands their histories. Changes occurred separately after the daughters grew up. Target 2,500–4,000 words.
- Speculative rule or ordinary-world constraint: Father's living bodily core operates his iron casing. Mother reversibly becomes a living, speaking cat. Selvi is a liquid organism who can raise a visible upper body while immersed, but cannot walk on land or hear remotely. Ibby is independently alive, born in liquid form after gestation, not a separated memory or copy. A stable local moon cycle brings this valley an eclipse each year.
- Dialogue promise: An adult daughter receives answers unavailable in childhood; her sister introduces a child whose life began differently.
- Dialogic medium: Conversation, interruptions, contested photographs, feline movements, and infant sounds. Everyone shares the riverbank within hearing.
- Dialogue engine: Attempts to define humanity reveal incompatible accounts of being this family. Corrections change whom the narrator believes and what she asks next.

## Voice

- Narrative texture: Notice mismatches between anticipated reunion and actual bodies; let recollection widen into a question before somebody's inconvenient action interrupts.
- Conversational texture: Affectionate overlap, unnecessary corrections, incomplete anecdotes, sudden earnestness, and baby-directed speech; explanation need not become argument.
- Rhetorical ownership: Father trusts continuity and genealogy, omitting things sentimentally. Mother owns sensory precision, teasing, and complaints. Selvi explains birth plainly but will not speak for Ibby's future. The narrator remembers images better than circumstances and mistakes correction for rejection.
- Pressure behavior: Father repeats details before yielding one. Mother becomes literal when called a disguised human. Selvi loses patience with irrelevant abstraction. The narrator replaces defensiveness with specific questions.
- Relationship movement: The narrator wants reassurance that their shared childhood mattered; asking exposes fear of being left behind. Father wants his daughters to recognize him without denying his fear of death. Mother wants her present pleasures believed alongside her past love. Selvi wants interested kin without a verdict on Ibby's identity. Exchanges redistribute authority over family memory.
- Anti-default: Avoid successive eloquent definitions followed by a crisis in which everybody proves the same lesson.

## Beats

1. The narrator arrives with photographs and customary food. Father's humming resonates inside his iron ribs; Mother occupies his sun-warmed shoulder. The roof fell after the family dispersed. Establish pleasure and irritation before any question becomes thematic.
2. Selvi arrives along the river with four-month-old Ibby, born since the last reunion. The baby has a distinct liquid body and startlingly familiar facial proportions. Selvi identifies her as her child with an unnamed living river-dwelling partner and answers ordinary questions about pregnancy, feeding, and sleep. Allow curiosity before the narrator asks what being human means.
3. Father grounds his answer in his own continuation through illness into iron and in descent. A cherished baby photograph supports his confidence until Mother corrects his account of taking it: he was elsewhere that afternoon. His mistake is ordinary fallibility. The narrator presses the difference between remembering a human life and beginning without one.
4. Mother refuses the assumption that her cat body is only clothing. Its tastes, fears, and pleasures are real; she becomes a woman to visit friends, but prefers feline life. A kitchen photograph releases incompatible memories of a family celebration: the daughters' delight, Mother's exhausted work, Father's fond but partial account. Preserve the affection within the contradiction. The narrator's effort to defend the happy memory makes her stop listening.
5. Selvi interrupts this inherited argument to distinguish Ibby's birth from her own transformation. She can explain what happened without forecasting her daughter's eventual self-description. The narrator finally asks Mother about an experience rather than challenging her category. Mother's answer includes both love for her children and something she was glad to relinquish; Father admits one remembered absence without turning it into a comprehensive confession.
6. Totality briefly interrupts the conversation because everyone came to see it; it confers no revelation or transformation. In returning light, the narrator invites Mother to finish the less flattering account of the celebration, past the point where family retellings usually stop. Let the ending remain inside that newly possible telling, with Father still entitled to disagree and Ibby pursuing her own small interests. Do not resolve the question through a unanimous definition, final photograph, or explanatory moral.
