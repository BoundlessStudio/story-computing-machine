# Provisional direction

Close third person through Iona, eight, during an ordinary afternoon game of hide-and-seek with her sister and two friends. Found first in two previous rounds, she has spotted a promising recessed shop doorway around a blind corner. She hurries toward it and runs face first into a marble child's chest. The small statue stands on a low, broad plinth belonging to the shop, with short arms extending along both sides of the corner, its hands carved over its eyes. Establish the doorway, shelf edges, and old scuffs before any of them solve the game. The statue blinks between its fingers, then shifts with an unmistakable stone scrape. Her nose hurts; its count is ruined. It has watched and privately shifted for years, but was copying the children counting in the square today, hoping that someone would answer its “ready or not.”

Iona first wants to get past the statue to her doorway and stay in her own game. The statue has watched many games and knows good hiding places, but wants a turn at hiding rather than a lifetime of pretending to seek. Its feet, skirt hem, and the small circular base beneath them are one piece: it can turn and slide that base on the plinth, move its upper body, and crouch only awkwardly, but cannot walk. An attempt to conceal Iona is noisy and gives her away for a third loss. Her sister stops, stares, and tests the startling animation, then calls Iona back to take her seeking turn. The others have their own game and ordinary interests; the new one should not erase their real wonder or turn them into villains.

Iona has to choose between recovering her standing in the familiar game and following the peculiar one she has started. She asks her sister to play the next round without her, then agrees to count for the statue. No cloak or kindness makes the marble disappear. Its first hiding attempt tests how little room it has. Then they discover the particular advantage of the corner: Iona cannot see both faces of the building at once, while the shelf reaches around it. The statue can slide just out of sight as she comes around one side, although its scrape betrays it. A short, increasingly absorbed pursuit develops through the two streets. Iona tests quiet approaches and misdirection, then deliberately calls absurd guesses about its hiding place in its solemn counting voice. The stone person laughs involuntarily; her play has caused its betraying delight rather than persuaded it to gift her a victory.

Possible ending: Iona follows the laugh and claims a find with her hand on its cold chest. The statue wants another turn, this time seeking. They agree on a return-to-base game: Iona hides, then has to get back and touch the statue itself; it wins if it spots her and calls her name before she touches it. Her original recessed doorway supplies real cover, and its sliding/turning now becomes a way to watch her approach. This rule prevents permanent evasion and defines an attainable finish without giving the stone feet. She makes it count properly, hands over its eyes, and runs to the doorway she had wanted at the beginning. The first collision has become reciprocal play; the statue has not left its plinth, and Iona has not acquired a magical cure or public mission. Let the prose find how briefly to establish a game they can honestly continue.
