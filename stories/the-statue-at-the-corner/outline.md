# Provisional direction

Close third person through Iona, eight, during an ordinary afternoon game of hide-and-seek with her sister and two friends. She has spotted a promising place around a shop's blind corner, hurries toward it, and runs face first into a marble child's chest. The small statue stands on a low, broad plinth at the corner, carved with its hands over its eyes. It blinks between its fingers, then shifts with an unmistakable stone scrape. Her nose hurts; its count is ruined. It was copying the children counting in the square today, hoping that someone would answer its “ready or not.”

Iona first wants to use the statue as cover and stay in her own game. The statue has watched many games and knows good hiding places, but wants a turn at hiding rather than a lifetime of pretending to seek. Its feet and the stone beneath them are one piece: it can turn and slide that base on the plinth, move its upper body, and crouch only awkwardly, but cannot walk. The actual attempt to hide is noisy and gives Iona away. The other children are neither cruel nor especially amazed for her benefit; they want their game to move, and her sister calls her back to take her turn.

Iona has to choose between the familiar game and following the peculiar one she has started. She asks her sister to play the next round without her, then agrees to count for the statue. No cloak or kindness makes the marble disappear. A first hiding attempt fails comically. Then they discover the particular advantage of the corner: Iona cannot see both faces of the building at once, while the broad plinth reaches around it. The statue can slide just out of sight as she comes around one side, although its scrape betrays it. A short, increasingly absorbed pursuit develops through the two streets. The child gets inventive about approaching quietly; the stone person is bad at containing its delight.

Possible ending: Iona catches it because it laughs before she comes around the corner. She puts her hand on its cold chest instead of hitting it with her face, and it insists the laugh should not count. She makes it count properly, hands over its eyes, and runs to choose a hiding place it has never seen. The first collision has become a reciprocal game; the statue has not left its plinth, and Iona has not acquired a magical cure or public mission. The prose should test whether this last new round feels earned, and whether the statue's inability to follow a hidden girl makes that ending a promise the story can honestly keep.
