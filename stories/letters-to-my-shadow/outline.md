# Letters to My Shadow — provisional outline

The prompt explicitly gives us repeated love letters from a sentient shadow realm, a speaker who finds the situation routine enough to say “Oh look, another,” and the speaker's belief that their own shadow is not sentient. I infer dry comic fantasy: elaborate supernatural courtship colliding with someone's practical understanding of an ordinary cast shadow. The question “right?” leaves the speaker's certainty open to challenge, but does not require a sentience twist. No particular pairing, scale, gender, or ending is specified. The anticipated pleasure is a misunderstanding that produces increasingly specific consequences, with an ending that makes the opening funnier or newly affectionate.

My provisional choice is a first-person romantic comedy, roughly 2,500–3,500 words, in which the cast shadow really is nonsentient. The adult narrator, Odette, wants the unsolicited courtship mail stopped. Nadrel, an adult shadow-realm suitor, has admired her silhouette's apparent dancing, eccentric changes of shape, and acts of kindness. Neither initially understands what the other is seeing. Nadrel knows Odette writes the replies; enjoying her voice is a new discovery, not another deception. Their chosen romance develops through the letters and a demonstration. It is an elaboration, not a user requirement. The tension is comic embarrassment and a growing wish to continue the correspondence they ostensibly mean to end.

- Open with the prompt's dry complaint while another lavish envelope emerges from the shadow beneath Odette's chair. Earlier letters establish several writers and incompatible ideas of beauty. Nadrel's unusually particular letter admires a silhouette's long-armed rescue of a fallen cyclist. Odette remembers helping him, with her shopping bag caught on her wrist: comic irony, not a concealed reveal. She answers that her shadow cannot accept a date. Their developing replies have particular reciprocal jokes. Possible exchange: she says he is corresponding with a woman in slippers, not a mysterious duchess; he sends a diagram of his own very formal slippers. She begins keeping his replies apart from the nuisance mail. He asks questions about the optical explanation rather than renewing a refused invitation.
- Odette offers a demonstration at the park bandstand at sunset, hoping he will explain the mistake to the other writers. This is expressly an explanation. Nadrel arrives dressed formally, including the slippers. He is a living silhouette who can move freely over the bandstand's connected surfaces. His flowers resemble cut-out holes. At first he tries addressing her shape on the paving, making Odette turn round to follow the conversation. He laughs at her answer rather than conferring all his attention on her outline.
- Physical comedy tests the competing understandings: an ordinary turn looks like a coy retreat; a hand puppet supplies a new head; two battery torches make two shadows. Odette deliberately switches between the beams, making either outline vanish without harm. Nadrel accepts what he has seen. The change is his understanding, not a surprise about the cyclist for the reader. He then volunteers to carry her explicit rejection to the other writers, including withdrawing his own original request. They send that packet through a dark opening beneath the bandstand. This fulfils the practical purpose of the meeting before any romantic invitation; his help is not conditional on her accepting him.
- Nadrel makes a new, affirmative invitation to Odette, drawing on what they have enjoyed in the letters. Rather than generic conversation after an accidental cloud, he positions one torch so her projected hand falls beside his on the bandstand's back wall and asks whether the woman in the slippers would dance with him. He waits for her answer, addressing her. She knowingly makes her cast hand meet his: the ordinary projection becomes a means for two actual people to dance. Her physical turns produce the shapes they had previously misread, now as deliberate shared flirtation; the comedy includes how ridiculous her side of this dance looks. At the close he asks permission to write to her. The next morning the nuisance letters have stopped, and the one permitted envelope bears Odette's name. Her shadow remains ordinary; no independent final wink retracts the premise.

The strongest risk remains a generic lesson about “seeing the real person.” The repair makes Nadrel a source of particular wit and the inventor of their first dance, resolves the mail thread, and makes the corrected understanding of a cast shadow enable the romantic payoff. Exact jokes and choreography remain provisional; protect the causal connection, not these sample sentences. No speech about projection or self-worth is intended.
