# Letters to My Shadow — provisional outline

The prompt explicitly gives us repeated love letters from a sentient shadow realm, a speaker who finds the situation routine enough to say “Oh look, another,” and the speaker's belief that their own shadow is not sentient. I infer dry comic fantasy: elaborate supernatural courtship colliding with someone's practical understanding of an ordinary cast shadow. The question “right?” leaves the speaker's certainty open to challenge, but does not require a sentience twist. No particular pairing, scale, gender, or ending is specified. The anticipated pleasure is a misunderstanding that produces increasingly specific consequences, with an ending that makes the opening funnier or newly affectionate.

My provisional choice is a first-person romantic comedy, roughly 2,500–3,500 words, in which the cast shadow really is nonsentient. The adult narrator, Odette, wants the unsolicited courtship mail stopped. Nadrel, an adult shadow-realm suitor, has admired her silhouette's apparent dancing, eccentric changes of shape, and acts of kindness. Neither has understood what the other is actually seeing. Their chosen relationship develops through the letters and an attempted demonstration; this romance is an elaboration, not a user requirement. There is no villain or compulsory public crisis. The tension is whether two people can get past an embarrassingly literal romantic mistake without making one of them merely foolish.

- Open with the prompt's dry complaint while another lavish envelope emerges from the shadow beneath Odette's chair. A few earlier letters establish a recurrent nuisance and incompatible ideas of beauty. Nadrel's letter is unusually particular: he admired a silhouette bending its long arm to help a fallen cyclist. Odette remembers doing it, with a shopping bag caught on her wrist. She sends a first actual answer explaining that her shadow cannot accept a date. Her pointed replies become the correspondence Nadrel enjoys; he asks questions rather than repeatedly overriding a refusal.
- Odette offers to demonstrate the problem at the park bandstand at sunset, in the hope that Nadrel will carry the explanation back to the other letter writers. This is expressly an explanation, not a date accepted on her shadow's behalf. He arrives formally dressed, with flowers whose petals resemble cut-out holes. The contrast between his courtship manners and her two battery torches creates attraction as well as jokes. He initially addresses the shape on the paving; Odette keeps having to turn round to follow the conversation.
- The demonstration defeats romantic interpretations through physical comedy. An ordinary turn looks like a coy retreat; a hand puppet supplies an alarming new head. Then the two torches produce two shadows, and switching off each beam makes one vanish without anyone being harmed. Nadrel does not cling to a theory once he sees its consequences. The letters and the silhouette's observed exploits are revealed as Odette's words and choices. His embarrassment has an object; she has been making jokes with somebody who can answer them.
- A cloud removes the sunset shadow altogether. With the supposed beloved absent, their conversation and attraction continue. Nadrel apologizes for approaching the wrong person and leaves Odette free to end the meeting; she chooses to prolong it, proposing a walk where the patchwork streetlamps keep multiplying and erasing her silhouette. The first actual invitation is addressed to her. End the next morning with a love letter bearing Odette's name and the opening joke changed by her willingness to open it. Her shadow remains faithfully ordinary; no final independent wink retracts the premise.

The strongest risk is that the pivot to Odette and Nadrel could become a generic lesson about “seeing the real person.” Protect the peculiar pleasure by making the letters, mistaken shadow etiquette, literal geometry, and their growing enjoyment of each other's replies cause the turns and the final invitation. The romance should be comic discovery in action, not a speech about projection or self-worth.
