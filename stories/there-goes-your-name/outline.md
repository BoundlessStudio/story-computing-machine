# Outline

## Story

- Premise / central promise: A first-person narrator trades the magical bond to their given name for an unnamed younger brother's release from a fae's neglected orchard court. The fae coos the prompt's quoted lines and expects the narrator to accept one of its prepared replacement names, but the narrator discovers that this local name magic has removed only a handle attached to them, not their identity, memories, or relationships.
- Character want and pressure: The narrator wants to get their frightened brother through the orchard gate before the fae can revise a completed exchange. Their old name now feels as though it points to a bright thread wound around the fae's finger, while every replacement the fae offers carries a visible strand back to its hand; refusing leaves the narrator grieving and apparently vulnerable to commands spoken through the stolen name.
- Turn: When the fae finally commands the narrator by the stolen name, the captured thread obeys and knots itself into the orchard's thorn-latch while the person remains free. The fae's boast has exposed the spell's narrow flaw: it confused access to a name with ownership of the self.
- Ending: The narrator uses the tangled latch to escape with their brother and leaves the stolen name behind rather than accept another coercive bargain. Safely outside, after acknowledging that the loss still hurts, they freely choose **Sivren**; the brother repeats it in an ordinary, affectionate sentence, giving the new name social meaning without a magical flare.

## Beats

1. Open inside a damp, overgrown orchard house at the instant the bargain is paid. The fae releases the narrator's younger brother, plucks the narrator's connection to their given name into a shining thread, and says verbatim: “There goes your name,” the fae cooed. “Now, go ahead and pick a new one!” The narrator still knows their history and recognizes the brother, but hearing the lost name now feels like hearing someone addressed in another room.
2. Establish the completed exchange and immediate goal: the narrator knowingly offered their name for the brother's freedom, and now needs only to lead him through the visible gate. The brother recognizes and trusts them despite the missing address, preserving the relationship while making the loss personal rather than cosmetic.
3. The fae blocks easy departure with courtesy and a fan of prepared names on leaf-thin slips. Each sounds attractive—brave, beloved, fortunate—but its thread runs back into the fae's sleeve. The narrator stalls by asking what each choice means, learning from the evasions that accepting one would create a fresh route for obligation and command.
4. The brother urges the narrator to take any name so they can go. Instead, the narrator tests what was actually stolen: they recall private memories, state choices and loyalties without a name, and notice that the fae can describe them only through relationships or actions. The pain remains, but nothing essential has vanished.
5. Losing patience, the fae speaks the stolen name and commands its owner to kneel and stay. The narrator feels no compulsion; the bright thread itself jerks downward, drags the fae's hand, and winds around the living thorn-latch. The narrator realizes this particular magic can manipulate its captured identity-link but cannot substitute that link for a whole person.
6. The narrator provokes a second command while pulling the brother toward the gate. More thread cinches the latch open and tangles the fae's reaching arm. The narrator uses an ordinary pruning hook already hanging by the door to hold back the physical brambles, and the siblings cross the threshold by effort rather than a new spell or universal anti-fae trick.
7. From inside the boundary, the fae offers to return the old name if the narrator comes back and accepts one prepared replacement. The narrator refuses. The brother speaks the old name once; both remember whom it used to mean, but the narrator feels its magical address terminate in the orchard and allows the grief to land.
8. At morning on the safe side of the gate, the brother asks what he should call them. The narrator tries sounds without treating the choice as instant healing, then selects **Sivren** because it is theirs to offer rather than the fae's to assign. The brother uses it naturally while they walk home; the close affirms that names matter as chosen, shared human signs even when literal name-power proves overrated.

## Continuity

- Canon used: Names are normally symbolic and socially powerful, while literal name-power is a rare effect created by a bounded local system; taking an identity-line or magical handle does not erase a person's identity or personhood. The story also preserves the binding personhood rule by allowing loss, grief, consent, memory, relationship, and moral choice to remain consequential.
- Boundaries and unknowns: Keep the orchard, era, culture, fae, bargain, thread effect, prepared names, and escape wholly story-local. Establish no universal fae taxonomy, true-name rule, consent fuel, bargain law, name-removal capability, or protection for unnamed people, and imply no connection to Ravel's three-line infrastructure, Calvea's Calling the Name, Deepmarket's route-name access, or another existing magic. The fae's origin, wider reach, and later disposition remain unresolved; the narrator's old name and the orchard's location remain deliberately unspecified.
