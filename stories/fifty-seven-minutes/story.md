# The Tide Will Have Her Answer

“A fair wind for our god!” someone called, and the whole quay laughed because Bruna Elt had built ships that could not be trusted with one.

They made her kneel in the dead-low basin, where green weed lacquered the immersion steps and stranded crabs ticked among the stones. The crown was plaited rush, broad as a basket, with seven old copper scales tied around it. Each scale bore the dent of some former season’s fool. The fishmonger settled it over Bruna’s cropped gray hair and pushed until the reeds scratched her ears.

“We bow our heads,” the crowd recited, bowing with elaborate groans, “and grant you the harbor’s keeping. Its depths attend you. Its tides take your word. Its creatures know your shadow. For fifty-seven minutes, we obey.”

The last line drew the loudest laughter. Nobody in town had obeyed Bruna for three years.

At the top of the steps, a glass water clock began dripping into a copper bowl. Fifty-seven marks had been scratched inside the bowl. When the last mark filled, tradition said, the fool-god must wade from the old step and surrender crown and immortality to the returning tide. Children loved the word *immortality*. Adults loved choosing the person who had to pretend to lose it.

Bruna rose. The numb fingers of her left hand caught in the rushes, and pain climbed from the other knuckles into her wrist. She saw Pavo Drin beside the careening posts, a coil of towline on one shoulder. He had not come to watch. He was inspecting a workboat’s scraped hull while the town inspected her.

Someone cried, “God, send us a whale for supper.”

“God, turn the mud to beer.”

“God, build us a ferry that floats.”

That one left a neat little hollow in the laughter.

Bruna looked from the speaker to the basin. Six fishing boats rested on their bilges along the central run, as they did at every dead low water. Beyond them, a skin of tide still shone between the harbor bar and the open sea. She had once stood above a hundred workers and made a launchway clear with three words. The words came back before prudence did.

“Sea,” she said, “stand clear.”

The harbor emptied.

Not with a wave. The remaining water drew itself flat, shivered like cloth pulled from a table, and ran backward through the bar. Silver fish struck mud. Mooring ropes fell slack. Beyond the outer shoal, the retreating sea rose in a dark blue ridge that climbed without breaking.

Every joke stopped at once.

Bruna felt the water depart as if a hand had been drawn down the length of her spine. Then the sensation opened. The ridge beyond the bar leaned against her awareness with its whole stored weight. She knew the drag of sand beneath it, the cold threads turning below the sun-warmed face, the press of saltwater inside every hull seam it still touched. No yard plan had ever given her such a clean account of loads. No timber had ever spoken so plainly under the adze.

The pain in her right hand was gone. So was the ache in the left fingers, though their old numbness remained. She put two fingertips below her jaw.

Nothing beat there.

“Four marks,” Pavo said.

He had come down from the posts. Mud blackened his boots to the ankle. He did not bow.

The banked water beyond the bar climbed another handspan.

“Bring it all back,” Bruna said.

Pavo caught her wrist. “How much, and where?”

At the first shape of her intention, the blue ridge bulged toward the entrance. Its shadow swept across the bar. Bruna closed her mouth. The water held, quivering.

Pavo released her as if her skin had burned him. “The sea moved. It didn’t vanish. You piled it somewhere.”

“I can see that.”

“You used to see a bad cut and call it fair.”

The remark landed where he meant it. Three years before, Bruna had cut the after hook of a ferry’s keel scarf half a thumb too shallow. She had packed the hollow with an oak slip, sealed it under pitch, and signed the joint herself. The ferry had broken in a late storm. Not at the scarf alone—the inquiry had found poor ballast, an overworked pump, weather no owner should have challenged—but a deckhand had died in the water, and Bruna’s hidden cut had become one cause nobody could argue away.

Pavo had found the slip in the wreckage. Pavo had laid it on her bench and stood there until she stopped lying.

Beyond the bar, something knocked twice against the stored tide.

Bruna turned toward it. The contact ran through her without crossing skin: a hull’s bluff bow, its starboard bilge, a propeller cage buried in silt. An outer workboat lay canted on the seaward flat, stranded when her order stripped the last water from beneath it. Three people clung to the high rail. The returning ridge would roll the boat before it floated.

“There’s a crew on the outer flat,” she said.

Pavo followed her gaze but could see only the black line of hull beyond the bar piles. “Then don’t bring it all back.” He looked at the water clock. Nine marks had filled. “The old words. Do you remember the end?”

“A dip and a soaking while everyone sings.”

“Freely into the tide from the old step before the fifty-seventh drop. ‘Water takes the mantle; blood takes up its labor.’ That is how you become mortal again.”

Bruna flexed her painless hand. “Again.”

“That was always the joke.” His attention stayed on the workboat. “Put a channel to them. No more than the boat needs.”

She almost told him to stop giving orders on her launch. The old irritation rose brisk and familiar. But this was no launch, and the thing waiting on her word was far more obedient than any crew should be.

“A tongue of water,” she said toward the wet edge beyond the bar. She saw the dimensions as she spoke them. “Six feet wide. Two feet deep. From the eastern shoulder of the ridge to the workboat’s stern. Move at walking pace. Spill nothing beyond its banks.”

Water separated from the ridge. It did not arch grandly or shine with festival light. It shouldered into a channel of its own making, brown with lifted silt, carrying bladderweed, three cork floats, and a terrified shoal of finger-long fish. One fish shot over the edge and flapped on the mud.

Pavo scooped it up and tossed it back. “The other stranded ones.”

Bruna could feel them now: scores of small lives beating in warm puddles across the basin. She set a thumb’s depth of water creeping among them, linked by narrow rills to the channel. Gulls descended, discovered their breakfast had acquired walls, and screamed objections.

At the fifteenth mark, she and Pavo went out across the mud.

Bruna no longer grew winded. Her boots dragged, but her thighs did not burn. The crown sat cold and absurd above a body that had ceased making the small complaints by which she had measured every day. Beside her, Pavo breathed hard and used his towline as a staff.

They reached the workboat with forty marks left to fill. Its hull leaned twenty degrees to starboard, keel sunk unevenly. The three adults aboard stared down at Bruna. One had a bleeding scrape along his temple; another held a boathook in both fists.

“Can you lift us?” the one at the rail called.

“The sea can,” Bruna said.

“Same question,” Pavo muttered.

She crouched by the exposed forefoot. Old habits put her hand to the planking, though touch was no longer necessary. The thin channel kissed the rudder and gave her the hull from within: every fastening as a point of interrupted pressure, every warped seam a faint escape. The starboard garboard had softened. A sweeping lift would push through it.

“Two blocks under the quarters,” she said.

Pavo turned the broad command into the questions he had asked as an apprentice. “How broad?”

“Four feet each.”

“How hard?”

“Enough to take weight, not enough to stove her.”

“That isn’t a measure.”

“It is if you can feel the planks.”

He looked at her then—at the crown, perhaps, or the stillness in her throat. “I can’t.”

Bruna revised the pressure down until it would have supported half the boat’s burden on ordinary launching cradles. Water crawled under the hull in two dark rectangular masses. The workboat shuddered. Mud released the keel with a long belch.

“Starboard is rising faster,” Pavo said.

“I know.”

“You always knew.”

She cut the port block deeper and let the other spread rather than climb. The deck leveled by degrees. The crew’s hands loosened on the rail.

The water was not timber. It had no grain to split, no hidden rot, no contrary spring. It took any bearing surface she imagined and reported every ounce of strain back to her. Yet it was full of inconvenient life. Crabs burrowed from her pressure. Weed clogged the temporary run. The cork floats she had carried inland jammed beneath the workboat’s propeller cage until Pavo waded thigh-deep into the narrow channel to wrench them free.

“Release around his legs,” Bruna ordered.

The water dropped away from Pavo so sharply that he staggered onto one knee.

“Gradually,” he snapped.

“I kept you upright.”

“No. You decided I would stay upright, and the rest was luck.”

She could have held him. She could have made the channel clasp his calves, brace his hips, carry him ashore like cargo. The possibility was exquisitely simple.

Instead she said, “A foot of slack around Pavo. Match his movement.”

Water eased back. He stood without taking the hand she offered.

At twenty-six marks, the first water block began sliding shoreward. Bruna moved it one foot, paused to feel the hull settle, then brought the second alongside. It was a launch backward and uphill: keel first through a channel barely wider than the boat, stored sea meted under it in supports. The workboat advanced with sucking sounds while its crew shifted loose gear away from the weakened side.

Halfway to the bar, a buried cable tightened across the channel. The moving water caught it and pulled. Far inside the basin, a moored fishing boat jerked sideways on the mud; one bilge prop toppled.

“Stop,” Pavo said.

Bruna stopped the blocks. The workboat halted so cleanly that its bow wave fell straight down.

She felt along the cable to the mooring it had fouled, then sent a shallow pulse beneath it. Mud let go. Pavo guided the freed length over a post while Bruna held thousands of tons of water waiting on the other side of her thought.

“You hid the oak slip because the owner would have taken the launch from you,” he said, hauling the cable.

“Is this when you want to discuss it?”

His answer came faintly from behind her as well as beside her: *Is this when you want to discuss it?* The words whispered from every salt puddle and wet hull seam in the basin. People on the quay turned. One of the stranded crew swore; the same oath came back in a hundred watery voices, thin as gossip through a wall.

Pavo stared at the channel. “Nothing private over water, then.”

Bruna should have been horrified. Instead a laugh broke out of her, and it too ran shoreward from puddle to puddle. The crowd heard their god laughing and did not join in.

Pavo set the cable down. “You were afraid to lose the work.”

“I lost it.”

“After you made sure no one else could judge the joint.”

Bruna looked at the flawless supports beneath the workboat. “I can judge this.”

“That is what frightens me.”

At thirty-nine marks, the workboat passed between the bar piles. Bruna widened the channel by inches where old stone narrowed it, lifted the softened starboard plank clear of a broken stake, and laid the vessel into protected water. The crew climbed onto the quay without ceremony. One clasped Pavo by both shoulders. None touched Bruna.

The ridge still waited outside, higher now than the harbor wall.

She returned it in stages. First a braided foot of water along the central run, slow enough to fill hollows rather than seal air inside them. Then enough beneath the fishing boats to meet their bilges evenly. She felt each hull accept buoyancy: weight passing from mud to water, ropes drawing taut, rudders waking in their pins. At the forty-third mark, the last boat righted itself. She let the rest of the stored tide cross the bar in low terraces that dissolved one into another without a breaker.

The harbor rose to its expected height. Only Bruna knew how much the west wall pressed against its footing, where a leaking dinghy drank through its seams, how warm runoff feathered across colder salt. The town had once been a scatter of jobs she could no longer do. Now it was a single structure resting in her hands.

People came down the steps and bowed without mockery.

“Keep the water off my cellar,” one said.

“My brother’s skiff is still outside.”

“Wash the silt from the north slips.”

“Leave it. The shellers need the mud.”

They spoke over one another, then quieted whenever Bruna looked their way. Nobody laughed at the crown. Their fear had given the old words fresh weight; their requests kept giving it weight. Worship was not affection. Authority was not trust. Neither distinction loosened the cold rush band around her brow.

Pavo pointed toward the old immersion step. “Fourteen left.”

They walked along the upper quay. The crowd followed until Pavo turned and told them the closing rite required no witnesses. The lie, if it was one, worked because they had begun to fear doing a god’s ritual incorrectly.

At the fiftieth mark, he and Bruna descended behind the net sheds. The path forked ahead. One branch curved back to the immersion step, now washed by the restored tide. The other followed a drainage channel between high stone walls and did not rejoin the basin for half a mile.

Pavo kept toward the step.

Bruna said, “If I go in, what comes back?”

“Your pulse, I suppose. Your sore hand. Hunger. Age.”

“My yard?”

He stopped. Water dripped from his clothes into the channel, each drop a bright point in her awareness.

“No,” he said.

“You might work with me again.”

“No.”

“You trusted me out there.”

“I trusted you to feel the hull. I trusted you after I checked every measure I could.” He rubbed mud from his towline with one thumb. “The deckhand stays dead. The hidden cut stays hidden until I dragged it into daylight. Getting that crew home does not put your bench back where it was.”

Bruna had imagined mortality as a door into some smaller, sounder version of her former life: not the old yard, perhaps, but honest repairs; not forgiveness, perhaps, but Pavo’s respect earned plank by plank. She had made the rescue bear that hope because a load, once named, could be placed.

Pavo would not help her place it.

“Then why are you taking me to the step?” she asked.

“Because I am afraid of needing you.” He looked toward the harbor, where every wet stone carried their words away. “I’m afraid of anyone who can turn a mistake into an order the world obeys. I’ll tell you the rite. I’ll stand there while you do it. I won’t call that forgiveness.”

Five marks remained.

The rescued boat was safe. The banked tide was released. No necessity remained for Bruna to shelter behind.

She put her left hand against the drainage wall. The two numb fingers still gave her nothing. Divinity had not repaired the old nerve damage. It had simply drawn a greater body around it, cold and immense, with currents for tendons and hulls for joints. She wanted its precision. She wanted to wake tomorrow without pain. She wanted fifty-seven years more, and fifty-seven after that, to learn what pressure did to every shape people dared put into water. She wanted an obedient material, though obedience had already made a monster of one flawed joint.

“I want it,” she said. The words traveled down the channel and out across the basin. “Not because they need me now. I want the sea-sense. I want the time. I want my hands steady.”

Pavo lowered his eyes to the towline and picked mud from a splice he had already cleaned.

“Then say that,” he said. “When they ask.”

Bruna turned into the drainage channel.

It was not a dash made in the last dripping seconds. The old step lay behind her, close enough to reach if she turned at once. She walked the other way with four drops remaining, then three. Pavo did not follow. With two left, she heard his boots retreat along the upper path.

When the fifty-seventh drop struck the copper bowl, the crown tightened cold against her living skin.

Bruna stood ankle-deep where the drainage channel met the tide. She told the harbor, carefully, to resume its ordinary course until she spoke again. Water slipped past her legs, inattentive in its eddies, exact in its obedience. From the wet edges of the basin came the town’s voices, faint and simultaneous: requests, thanks, accusations, a prayer already practiced into confidence.

She gave them no answer. She waited through the time in which her next breath should have come, then pressed two fingers below her jaw.

Her heart did not beat.
