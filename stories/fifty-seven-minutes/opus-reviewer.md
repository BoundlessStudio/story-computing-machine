# Opus review: The Tide Will Have Her Answer

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — Ground mostly holds, but the decisive line breaks the story's own pronoun rule, two punchlines rely on manufactured setup questions, and every speaker shares the same deadpan closer.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, varied, well-staged action prose; only minor glosses and one opening clause that needs later context.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the prompt is fully delivered, but the east-cove roofs deadline passes unremarked and several staging details contradict each other.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree on dialogue and internal continuity, agree on prompt and universe.

The premise earns precise speech. A sea that obeys words literally makes Ossa's "Received / Confirm / Say back" and Orselle's load specifications into safety equipment, not affectation. The Rulik–Orselle ferry confrontation is the strongest exchange: he interrupts, dominates and owns his fault, and the work physically breaks up her defense. The failure is spread. The same finished, dry one-liner reaches everyone: a pale rite-keeper, the harbormaster between evacuation orders, a man just dropped into mud, and Orselle on her knees after the salt-breath leaves. Some of those quips need a straight-man question that the triaging harbormaster would never ask. At the decisive beat Orselle speaks a pronoun command ("Turn it"), after the story has spent pages proving such commands dangerous, and the deadline she gives for the coves passes without comment. The edit pass should cut or plainify the quips and setups so that humor comes from need and procedure. It should repair the two decisive-beat problems and leave the ferry exchange, the decision exchange and the final measurement almost untouched.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: a disgraced shipwright is crowned god as a joke, the sea obeys her, and she has fifty-seven minutes (seven brine vessels) to decide; she chooses to let the ritual make her mortal again. The story must stay complete and broadly accessible. The target is 2,500–4,000 words and the story is about 3,800 now, so the pass has roughly 200 words of headroom. Prefer cuts to additions. The active craft profile is `prospective-2026-08-21` (the last `Craft profile:` line). The rewrite request authorizes rewriting dialogue, not changing the plot or the outcome.
- Internal mechanism that edits must respect: within the harbor stones the water obeys Orselle's audible directives literally, supplies any missing object with whatever it can reach, and takes the widest scope her words allow. After L131, pronouns and pointing are known hazards. Test every Orselle imperative written in the edit against this rule. For shore-return she must cross the white boundary onto claimed land and return the harbor right before the last brine. The salt-breath leaves, and she does not age all at once (L487).
- Story facts: she received the sea-gift at thirty-nine, eighty-six years ago. Rulik cut and packed the ferry scarph; Orselle reopened it and certified it without proof. Her seal was struck four years ago. The hearing is at second bell. The damage tally at L473 includes "No roofs went." The goat thread runs through L323, L337, L391 and L487.
- Universe: rules.md "Divinity" (CANON: godhood is the continuing conjunction of worship, power and authority) and "Living agency and magic" (LOCKED: only life uses magic; the rope crown is a nonliving focus). Do not give the crown or the rite book agency. Do not imply godhood without the crowd's observance.
- Keep Ossa's procedural register as her distinguishing voice, keep the uneven fluency of the ferry exchange, and keep the crowd's competing needs at L203 and L335.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · Pronoun command at the decisive beat

- Where: L387, sixth vessel empties and Orselle chooses; rule set at L131; her caution with words at L421
- Text:
> "Turn it," she said.
> Vuret, no pronouns. No pointing.
> If she said lift, it might lift every wet thing in the gate throat.
- Problem: Ground/rule consistency. Three times the story has shown the water filling an open object with the widest thing it can reach ("Hold her", "Let go", "Run it clear"), and Ossa has banned pronouns. At the moment of decision Orselle gives an imperative whose verb the tide can perform and whose object is "it". Nobody reacts and nothing happens, yet thirty lines later she is afraid to say "lift". A reader who has learned the rule will read the most important line as an authorial slip. The line also misses a chance to show that she has learned the rule.
- Fix: Have the decisive line name both its listener and its object, so she makes the choice with the care the story has taught. Keep it short, with no speech around it. Illustrative only: "Sileth. Turn the seventh glass." If a reaction is wanted, use a glance from Sileth or Ossa, not a line.

### D2 · Major · Every speaker shares the same deadpan closer

- Where: L83 (Sileth at the rite book), L139 and L159 (Ossa's evacuation orders), L209 (fishwife), L229 (mud crew), L469 (Orselle after the crown comes off)
- Text:
> "I had gathered."
> The geese can dislike us from higher ground.
> Then keep enough breath to answer. Go.
> "No. They're your next four minutes. Choose."
> "Keep those. We haven't time to find another set," his mate said.
> "Everywhere cheap workmanship usually puts it."
- Problem: Identical Voices and uniform fluency under pressure. Each line is neat on its own. Together they give the same finished comeback to a rattled rite-keeper, a harbormaster in the middle of triage, a man just dropped into mud, and a woman who "could not understand why breathing required so much work". The wit flattens pressure the narration reports ("Sileth had gone pale"), and it ends Ossa's orders on a joke instead of an order. L159 answers the runner's worry with a turn of phrase instead of telling him what to say. L209 copies the fishwife's sentence shape to win the exchange, and its "four minutes" matches nothing on the clock (one vessel runs about eight).
- Fix: Cut these six or make them plain. Sileth should not answer at all, or should keep reading under her breath; fear should slow her, not sharpen her. End Ossa's lines on the order ("Take him. Open the pen.") and answer the runner plainly. Illustrative only: "Tell them Vuret did it and she's holding it. Go." In the fishwife exchange Ossa can concede and repeat the choice, or turn away. Cut the mate's line; the teeth line is enough. At L469 give Orselle a short, literal answer about where it hurts, so that Ossa's irritated look lands on bluntness, not a quip. Keep the cooper's jeer (L15), "Problem is my teeth are full of the harbor." (L227), "Argue narrow. This end's dropping." (L273) and "Ready to fail on purpose" (L315) as the story's share of earned wit.

### D3 · Major · Straight-man questions that exist to set up a punchline

- Where: L319–L329 (lower-yard check, the goat), L341–L347 (last gate-readiness check before the decision), L301–L309 (the east-cove deadline)
- Text:
> "Whose?"
> "Goat won't say."
> "If?"
> "It's timber, Harbormaster. It doesn't sign guarantees."
> Sileth looked at the brine. "This vessel?"
> "The one draining now."
- Problem: Punchline Patch. Ossa "sorted noise into facts" and runs on confirmations, yet with the storm head moving toward two coves she asks whose goat it is. At the last readiness check she asks "If?" so that Rulik can deliver an aphorism echoing Orselle's false certificate. The echo is thematic, but the setup is manufactured, and it comes just before the decision, where the story least needs a laugh line. "This vessel?" asks about something that has no ambiguity (only one vessel drains at a time). It exists to add a round of procedure, and the restated answer then changes the claim (see C1).
- Fix: Cut "Whose?" and "Goat won't say." and go straight to Ossa's order about the goat. At the readiness check, cut "If?" and give Rulik's hedge a concrete need that sets up L415–L423, where the wrong wedge is struck and he drives the cap wedge himself. Illustrative only: "Ready if the high shore holds. Put your steadiest hand on the cap wedge." Cut Sileth's "This vessel?" exchange and state the deadline once (C1).

### D4 · Minor · Rules and rulings delivered as maxims

- Where: L91–L93 (after the punt test), L131 (after the tide misfire), L179–L181 (after the backstory), L225 (after "Down"), L375 (shore assignment)
- Text:
> "It obeys the noun it can supply," Orselle said.
> "And the widest measure allowed by my words."
> "Then use more words."
> Meaning isn't in the order.
> "power, but no observance and no harbor right. Not godhood then."
> "Name the problem. I will name the water."
> "Then assign by the opening, not your sash."
- Problem: The magic's rules are needed, and consequences already teach them well (the punt, the tide, the lifted crew). These lines restate each rule as a neat formula in the same legal register, spoken by three different characters, and that is most of why the talk sounds like people drafting clauses. Orselle says L91 while "her own voice sounded thin", yet it comes out as a grammar-lesson axiom. Sileth's L179 re-explains the godhood conjunction that the crowning already dramatized (L31, L45). L375 closes a live dispute over rank with an epigram.
- Fix: Keep one plain statement of the rule in Orselle's practical terms, and let Ossa's operational orders ("Vuret, no pronouns. No pointing.") carry the rest. Illustrative only: "I said let go and didn't say what. It took everything." Cut "Meaning isn't in the order." Rewrite L225 as an instruction to the crews, not a chiasmus. Have Sileth react to the page at L179 (what it covers and what it doesn't) instead of classifying Orselle, or cut the line; Ossa's next question still works without "Don't tidy it yet". At L375, Orselle's practical reason at L371 is enough; cut the epigram.

### D5 · Minor · Narration explains the line just spoken

- Where: L107, L275, L283, L361, L377, L435, L471
- Text:
> The cruel words stayed behind her teeth.
> the polished shape of Orselle's defense broke apart under its weight
> His voice worked again when the work did.
> "Good," Orselle said, too quickly.
> He looked as if he might answer the history instead of the order.
> She had been Vuret to Sileth all day.
> which was better than reverence
- Problem: Each gloss tells the reader a subtext the exchange already carries: Rulik reclaiming her old rule, her defense collapsing when he takes the beam, his fluency returning with the work, her deflection, the history beneath the order, the shift to her first name, and Ossa's irritation as relief. L435 is also unsupported. Sileth never calls her "Vuret" on the page, so the name shift at L433 is asserted, not earned.
- Fix: Cut most of these; the actions beside them already carry the meaning. Keep at most one (L283 is the least intrusive). For L433–L435, either give Sileth one earlier "Vuret" (the shore-return instructions at L399 are the natural place, just before "Orselle. Now.") and cut L435, or cut L435 and let "Orselle. Now." stand alone.

### D6 · Minor · Unrecoverable referents and small gaps in uptake

- Where: L23–L25 (heckle at the crowning), L155–L157 (runners), L319–L329 (lower-yard check), L399–L405 (the lost verb)
- Text:
> "That's why we chose Vuret," somebody said.
> The runner swallowed.
> "Vuret's shop is locked," a crew leader reported.
> "Checked both floors. One goat."
> "Take it uphill. Leave the doors."
> "The sentence lost a verb."
> "Use return."
- Problem: At L25, "That" points back to "Mockery doesn't excuse bad observance". The joke depends on "observance" meaning her failure to inspect the seam, which the reader does not learn until L257–L263. At L157 Ossa has just spoken to the north-quay lead, and "the runner" appears without being named or sent. At L319–L323 the shop is locked, yet both floors were checked, and "Leave the doors" reads as "leave them shut" until L391 shows them standing open. "Use return." can only be decoded when L445 arrives. At this point Sileth has said only that the sentence about the crossing lost a verb, and it is unclear that "use return" fills that gap.
- Fix: L25: aim the heckle at something the reader already has (the crown, her seal, the court), or cut it. L157: "One of the runners", or have Ossa send a named runner. L319–L329: say the door was forced, and make Ossa's order "Leave the doors open." L403–L405: have Sileth read the broken line aloud with its gap, so the reader sees Orselle's chosen verb fill it.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · An opening clause that needs later context, and a thesis gloss

- Where: L13 (the crowd laughs), L45 (the crowd attends)
- Text:
> and because their god of sound workmanship could enter the shipyard by no other title
> Not forgiving her, but seeing together the thing they had named.
- Problem: The second reason at L13 depends on the ban the reader learns only at L19, so on first reading it is a riddle. L45 is a "not X but Y" reversal that states the story's godhood mechanism (observance makes the god) in narration before the harbor has shown it. Sileth's L179 then says it again (D4).
- Fix: At L13, either bring the ban forward or trim the clause to the laugh itself. At L45, keep "They were choosing to attend." and cut the gloss. The caps coming off at L33 and the water rising at L55 do the rest.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Major · The storm-head deadline passes without consequence

- Where: L293–L311 (the east runner's report and Orselle's estimate at the gate), L331 (the fifth vessel ends), L385 (the edge reaches the east cove during the seventh), L473 (the tally)
- Text:
> Their south path is already under.
> "Before this vessel finishes, the first roofs."
> "The east cove takes water before the sixth begins."
> Beyond the stones, the eastern cove took the first edge of the storm head.
> No roofs went.
- Problem: At L301, Orselle, whose sea-sense has been exact all day, says the first east-cove roofs will go before the fifth vessel finishes. The fifth finishes at L331 and no roofs are reported. The head reaches the cove only after the sixth empties (L385), climbing a road, and at the end "No roofs went." The confirmation at L309 also quietly softens "roofs" to "takes water". At L295 the south path is "already under" before the head she describes as still coming. The countdown's hardest deadline lapses without comment, which weakens the pressure on her decision.
- Fix: Make one consistent prediction and keep to it. The simplest version: Orselle times the first roofs to the end of the last glass, so that bearing through is what floods them, and L385 (the edge climbing a road during the seventh) and L473 stay true. Give Ossa's "Confirm" the same claim restated, not a softer one (after cutting "This vessel?", D3). Change L295's "already under" to "going under", or attribute it to the rain. Illustrative only: "The first roofs about when the last glass runs dry."

### C2 · Minor · The punt test and the lighter are hard to picture

- Where: L87–L89 (punt test), L97–L99 (the lighter released), L211 (the mud crossing)
- Text:
> every north-quay mooring jumped slack while the water drew an inch from the wall
> Moorings cracked taut.
> Orselle released the support.
> She ordered a cart-road ribbon of water to remain beneath the lighter so its keel would not break
- Problem: The punt test is the story's first demonstration of the widest-scope rule. But water drawing away from the wall makes moorings go taut, as the story itself says at L125, not slack, and slack lines would not need retying (L89). At L99 Orselle releases the lighter silently, although the water acts only on spoken words. At L211 she tells water to "remain" under the lighter after she has released it (L99) and the whole tide has been sent past the red stones (L125).
- Fix: Make the consequence of "Let go" physically coherent (for example, the water drops and moorings jerk taut or part, so the boats need retying). Give the release at L99 a spoken, precise word. At L211 have her raise or bring back the ribbon of water instead of "remain".

### C3 · Minor · The gate sequence contradicts itself

- Where: L313 (cuts "driven" before the opening), L395–L397 (Orselle's sequence), L411–L437 (the execution), L421 (Orselle considers lifting water)
- Text:
> They drove the two cuts and left the upper strap.
> Hold the left jamb between the paired shores until water reaches the third course of the storehouse wall. Then north steps.
> Orselle opened her mouth to lift the water away from them.
- Problem: The two cuts are "driven" at L313 and then opened again at L411–L423. Orselle's sequence has the crews hold the jamb until the water reaches the third course and only then leave. In fact the crews leave before she returns the harbor right (L425–L437), so the "third course" detail that Rulik drills (L397) never applies. At L421 the harbor is still being held empty and the load bowing the high shore is the gate's twist, so there is no water to lift away.
- Fix: At L313, write "bored and marked" or "cut to depth" instead of "drove". At L395, make the course mark a signal for something that actually happens after the crews are out, or put "then north steps" first. At L421, cut the sentence about lifting water, or make her hesitation about something she could actually command. Keep "Rulik climbed the brace".

### C4 · Minor · Places and rite references that don't connect

- Where: L161 and L349 (rite text), L191 (relief gate), L335 (the crowd), L399–L455 (the crossing)
- Text:
> The water is admitted through the old tide stone.
> the old tide way opens to receive what was withheld
> Two lower-yard families shouted that Mallowbreak should not buy neighboring roofs with theirs.
> Orselle fell to one knee on the apron.
- Problem: The rite promises a "tide stone" and a "tide way" that open by themselves. Instead the crews break open the relief gate, and nobody links the two, so the reader can't tell whether the gate is the rite's route or a human substitute for it. The lower yard is "abandoned" (L191) and cleared of people, yet lower-yard families talk about their roofs. Orselle makes the shore-return at the white boundary by the yard gate (L399, L437), then falls "on the apron" with no movement in between.
- Fix: Add one clause at L191 or L193 tying the relief gate to Sileth's "old tide way", or have Sileth recognize it. Make the families' loss stores and sheds, matching the tally at L473. Put the crossing and the fall in the same place.

### C5 · Minor · Water infrastructure as texture (style-guide policy)

- Where: L109–L117 (the drydock sluice), L191 onward (the storm-tide relief gate)
- Text:
> crews were trying to close the inner sluice before the squall drove weed into its guides
> the relief gate was a stone throat meant to take storm tide through the abandoned lower yard and across the western waste
- Problem: `universe/style-guide.md` "No default water infrastructure" (CANON) names sluices and flood-control works and applies to authorized revisions. The prompt asks for a shipwright and an obedient sea, not flood works. The sluice at L109 is texture. The relief gate is the plot's release mechanism.
- Fix: Replace the sluice task with shipyard work the prompt already supplies (for example, a launch-cradle or slipway line on the herring boat), keeping the snarled rope, the boy in the bight and the crossed shouts. Adjust "Ease the gate!" at L113 to match. Do not remove or restructure the relief gate in a dialogue pass; leave that decision to the owner, and add no further detail about the gate or flood works.
