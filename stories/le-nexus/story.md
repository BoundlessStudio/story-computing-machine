I had just got my whiskey at Le Nexus when my left hand tried to pick up a woman. I’d bought the arm two doors up Pressers’ Lane, off a rack with spring clips for the wrists, such as you might hang a bicycle from. The dealer gave me a sheet of tests and ate a cheese roll while I did them. I sorted coins in the palm, picked the price sticker off his glass and put it back without a crease. At the elbow the red wires showed between two steel plates. The cap on the little finger had worn through. There was a flat place on the thumb where I’d have expected a curve. It suited me. I had already had one arm that obliged people to ask what happened to it.

The woman beside me had her blonde hair done up in a way that looked good for about the next three minutes. She was nursing a red cocktail and letting the bartender tell her about a man who’d had a coat stolen at his own wedding. Her free hand drummed its fingers in order along the zinc. That was when my hand got up. There was a courtliness to it, with that thumb held out of the way. She gave me the private grin a woman can give you while somebody is still talking to her.

I put the hand back where it had been. A tram driver who’d been standing a little back between us caught the grin and took his glass down the bar. His coat gave up the smell of wet wool in the heat coming off everybody’s shoulders. Everybody was so helpful on a Friday. I’d come in cold off the lane. I wanted one drink and a dance when they cleared the tables.

The bartender pulled his board toward him and slit a grapefruit. I got the whole bright smell of it as the strap took a pull from behind. This time the hand had got as far as her elbow. She lifted her arm and the hand came on. I pinned the steel wrist across the bar and bore down. The studs in my right cuff dug in almost as hard as the buckle at my ribs. I could keep it there. The fingers walked down the zinc one by one and had the marvelous nerve to make the little one wait for its turn. Its bare tip rang on the counter.

The woman set her glass down by the stem. She bent one finger and knocked on the zinc beside my hand. I felt it come through the counter and up the wrist I was pinning. Her knuckle hung above the bar long enough for me to think that was all, then came down again. The bartender was still talking.

Just as I was going to ask her what she’d done, the steel wrist went slack under my palm. Its thumb opened away from the fingers. Before I’d taken any weight off the wrist she’d slipped her hand into the gap as if she’d known how wide it would be. She was starting to close her fingers when she looked up at me. They stayed bent over the thumb. She had looked at me earlier, across the lip of the glass, as someone she might choose to know in a few minutes. The look she gave me now took longer, although there was less of it she wanted me to see.

“Hang on.” I still had my palm on the wrist. “Has it got you?”

She showed me her palm. “I thought somebody was being very funny at my expense. There used to be a burr on the end of your thumb. It caught the lining of my jacket until I sanded it off with an emery board. Took a whole set of them. She’d hold her hand like that forever if I let her, just under my elbow. I used to wait with my drink halfway up to see which one of us would give in.”

I took my weight off the wrist. The steel hand stayed open. I put my right thumb where she’d had her fingers. You could feel the small plane she’d made even with a callus.

“Who was she?”

“A woman called Rika, from the presses.” She picked up her drink to let the bartender’s cloth go by. “She traded it in for a lighter one a week before she left. I saw her off on the night train and she carried her suitcase in the new hand the whole way to her seat. She wanted me to see what all the money was for. I’d have bought this one if she’d asked. God knows what I would have done with it. It went to Meers with the blue sign, did it?”

“I wonder what else Meers forgot to charge me for.” I took a drink. The whiskey had got warm while I was waiting for it to warm me. Two people had found just enough room beside the ice bin to dance. They were taking turns backing out of the way of the busboy and going straight on where they’d left off. I wanted to be where they were. The first night I’d left Meers with the arm in a cardboard box, there’d been a woman dancing right up against the front window of Le Nexus. Through the glass all I could hear was her heels. I went home round by the tram sheds after that.

“What did it ask you?”

She offered me her fingers over the zinc. “For a hand. Rika and I used to start sideways at the rail, when it was still too crowded to turn. By the time I had her fingers I could haul my foot out from under a tram driver’s stool and we were off. We could do a whole dance with only the two of us knowing what the other one had said.”

She let her hand drop between us. “See where it goes when I turn.”

I put down my whiskey. She turned until we were both facing the bar. My left hand slipped behind her and shut around her waist. The red light under the rail shone between the plates where the wrist had bent. She put a little of her weight against it and I felt the strap take hold of my back. Her shoulders had scarcely moved. The curl beside my gold hoop stirred when she breathed out.

“Would you do the whole thing if I stood here?” I asked the hand. I straightened my elbow and the strap let up. She’d only just begun to lean when I got the fingers open.

“Here’s how to change.” She caught my steel hand and rapped three quick times on its back with one finger. “My knee went through a deck board once. For quite a while I had to stay up when we danced, and the minute a man came in thinking he was going to sweep me down, I’d give him that and put my own arm round his waist. Down he’d go in my arms and he’d have to wait till I stood him up again. Some of them got a taste for it. That lot at the corner can tell you.”

She had a way of delivering a sentence to me and then turning the end of it out toward everybody within reach. She lifted her glass at the tram driver. He bowed from the neck, as much as he could spare her without getting off his stool.

“Let me try it,” I said.

She let me put the hand at her waist and leaned again. I could lift a price sticker off a glass with the steel fingers. Getting the ring finger up by itself when the whole hand wanted to close was a different proposition. I got two knocks out against her ribs and the finger stuck. She waited with that little bit of herself resting on me. I opened the hand a fraction. This time I got three knocks off in a hurry. She straightened up, took my steel hand off her waist and put her own hand flat at my side.

We were still facing the bar. I could see the damp ends of her hair close to my cheek in the pink glow off the mirror behind the bottles. I wanted to know what she’d been about to say with her hand when she reached for the thumb. I’d need a better acquaintance with her before I could ask.

“Come out with me when they clear,” I said. She left her glass between mine and the stack of empties and started putting up her hair.

“Odile,” she said. She waited with the band between her teeth till I told her mine.

* * *

A horn played two notes from the bottom of its range, and a man by the mirrors finished whatever he was saying to his wife and put their chairs one on top of the other. Along the edge of the floor people sucked in their stomachs to let the dancers past. Here you could go up to a person you hadn’t seen since last winter and put a hand under her elbow, and she might ask after your mother as the two of you went by the bar. You had the length of the song before they wanted their chairs back.

Odile put a shoulder between two men who were talking across the opening. The brass buttons on a driver’s coat scraped one of my buckles when I went in after her. Once we were clear she turned around and walked backward. I had thought she’d take both my hands before we set off. She waited with one hand on her hip. I could hear the horn better here, with the speaker high up on the far wall, and feel the double stroke of the bass where the floor had worn thin. My hand was quiet. I’d been holding my left elbow too close to my ribs and I let it out.

I offered her my right hand. She put her left in it and settled her other hand lightly on my shoulder, over the lower buckle where a person could get purchase if she wanted it. The pink sign in the mirror lit one side of her face. She tipped her head to show me the stretch of boards behind her that no one had got to yet. I gave her a push with the heel of my hand and she went, grinning as if I’d put her up to something.

Odile danced at an unhandsome distance. A pocket of air stayed between her and me, so that when she stepped across me on the upswing it came as a surprise how near her thigh was. It took me four bars to believe she could be where she promised from so far away, and once I took a step long enough to see how far she’d go, we went the whole length of the rail before either of us thought to turn. A man with his back to us started to ease his way out with six drinks held against his stomach. I could feel the laugh begin in Odile before she opened her mouth. She swung us both round his back and never touched him. I kept my right hand wide for her to come back through and she came a little closer this time.

Odile bent forward laughing and put her forehead on my shoulder. I saw the shine between her shoulder blades when I put my steel hand there to steady her. There were tables at home where I could make a corner with my feet when I was waiting for the kettle to boil, and a landing where there was a beautiful run of four boards from my door to the neighbour’s, if nobody happened to be coming up the stairs. Another couple came through in a hurry and I raised my left arm to let their hands pass under it. The steel caught the blue light of the bottle shelves, and Odile looked up into the open joint. I’d brought her out there with the arm in plain sight and I wanted her to look at it for as long as she liked. I went on dancing while she did.

When the horn came round again she brought her mouth to my ear. I felt her say something before I heard any of it. “Keva.” I gave her a small pull with my right hand and she turned until we were facing the bar together. She came so close on my left that her heel clipped mine. The forearm ran up hard against my side. My steel hand slipped behind her and found the place where the leather of her vest ended at her waist. The thumb turned in.

I could feel the rivet in the strap where it crossed my back. Odile was still over her feet and the motor was getting ready to have all of her. All I had to do was set my right foot a little farther back and she would follow that hand down into a sweep it knew better than I did. She covered it with her hand and pressed the flat place with the pad of her own thumb as if she had found it in the dark. Her chin lifted. I felt the hand take the first of her weight.

I’d have liked to catch her. We had the room for it and there was a terrific look of ease about the way she started down. I could give her the whole thing she remembered. Under my right foot the board ran lengthwise, with a little play between it and the next one. I pressed my heel down where it was. As the strap tightened I edged the heel of my steel hand out from her waist, far enough to lift the ring finger while she still had the thumb.

Three quick knocks against her ribs, right below the vest. She drew breath on the last one, sharp enough that I felt her go still.

She was close enough for me to see where her zipper had caught a thread, with the hair she’d bundled up two minutes ago coming down beside her mouth. At the bar I’d thought she’d straighten up as soon as I got the taps off. There was a long, bare part of the music in which she might still lie back against my hand. Her thumb went to the edge of the flat and back, once, with the small care of a person who had gone to the trouble of filing it down. Then she shifted her weight over her own feet and took her hand away. She looked at me with her lips parted. I’d seen her smile two or three times already where anybody could see. This time I was near enough to see the corners of her mouth tremble into it.

Odile turned in against me and laid her left arm around my waist. I put my right hand on her shoulder. She took one step and waited. I still had all my weight in my heels, and I’d been using the muscles under that strap to hold my shoulder level for so long that they stayed hard even with her there. When I let myself go I felt the whole of my left side go at once. The lights over the bar had made a bent red line in the mirror. I had time to see it from halfway down and then to lose it and see Odile’s face in its place. She had a wide stance and a shoulder as firm as the rail. Her arm rose warm under my ribs until it had all of me. I let the left arm hang. The strap went loose across my back.

She brought me up slowly enough to let my heels find the floor. I kept my right hand on her shoulder. There were two short breaths and a long one under my palm, and when she’d got it all out she smiled at me from one side of her mouth. The rail was a few feet away. In the mirror I could see the red cocktail still standing beside my whiskey. The horn played its two low notes again. Odile drew her hand away from my waist and it hung between us, palm up. She’d started to turn it over to let it fall when I put my left hand in it.
