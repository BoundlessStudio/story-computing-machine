I had just got my whiskey at Le Nexus when my left hand tried to pick up a woman. I’d bought the arm two doors up Pressers’ Lane, off a rack with spring clips for the wrists, such as you might hang a bicycle from. The dealer gave me a sheet of tests and ate a cheese roll while I did them. I sorted coins in the palm, picked the price sticker off his glass and put it back without a crease. The enamel plate over the elbow came from a blue arm and a place on the steel thumb had been worn flat. It suited me. I had already had one arm that obliged people to ask what happened to it.

The woman beside me had her blonde hair done up in a way that looked good for about the next three minutes. She was nursing a red cocktail and letting the bartender tell her about a fellow who’d gone home on the wrong branch of the tram line three Fridays running. Her free hand drummed on the zinc, twice with two fingers together and then with the edge of her wedding finger. That was when my hand got up. There was a courtliness to it, with that thumb held out of the way. She gave me the private grin a woman can give you while somebody is still talking to her.

I put the hand back where it had been. The braid of the new strap caught a dark curl against my gold hoop when I tipped my head, and I had to put down the whiskey to get them apart. Meanwhile the tram driver I’d followed to the bar caught the grin and picked up his glass. I smelled wet wool as he went by. He was giving us the elbow room it had taken me three tries to get out of him. Everybody was so helpful on a Friday. I wanted enough whiskey to get warm and then a piece of the floor when they cleared it.

The bartender pulled his board toward him and slit a grapefruit. I got the whole bright smell of it as the strap took a pull from behind. This time the arm had got as far as her elbow. She was lifting it to make room. I got the heel of my right hand onto the steel wrist and leaned until the edge of a buckle dug into my ribs. I could keep it there. It had four fingers left to speak with and the marvelous nerve to make the little one wait for its turn. That was the one with a worn cap. It struck a high note on the zinc, like the end of a question.

The woman set her glass down by the stem. Two taps and then a space and then another tap went through the zinc into the heel of the hand I was holding the wrist with. The man was still talking. I couldn’t even tell if the woman’s answer used the same fingers. She had brought her hand across the wet ring left by my whiskey and was reaching for the thumb. She paused with her fingers bent. She had looked at me earlier, across the lip of the glass, as someone she might choose to know in a few minutes. The look she gave me now took longer, although there was less of it she wanted me to see.

“Leave it there,” I said.

She held her hand where it was until she saw I meant the arm, then drew it back. “I thought somebody was being very funny at my expense. There used to be a burr on the end of your thumb. It caught the lining of my jacket until I sanded it off with an emery board. Took a whole set of them. She’d hold her hand like that forever if I let her, just under my elbow. I used to wait with my drink halfway up to see which one of us would give in.”

I lifted my right palm. The steel hand stayed spread as if there were still a reason for me to pin it. I put my right thumb where hers had been going. You could feel the small plane she’d made even with a callus.

“Who was she?”

“A woman called Rika, from the presses.” She picked up her drink to let the bartender’s cloth go by. “She traded it in for a lighter one a week before she left. I saw her off on the night train and she carried her suitcase in the new hand the whole way to her seat. She wanted me to see what all the money was for. I’d have bought this one if she’d asked. God knows what I would have done with it. It went to Meers with the blue sign, did it?”

I nodded and took a drink. The whiskey had got warm while I was waiting for it to warm me. On the floor a man was blotting a spill with his sleeve. He and the woman he was dancing with got four or five steps farther along every time he bent down, and she did exactly the same thing with her feet each time she had to wait for him. I wanted to be where they were. I’d spent the past month going through the door two up from a bar I liked, and taking the long way back to avoid the window.

“What did it ask you?”

She offered me the fingers of her right hand over the zinc. “For a hand. Rika and I used to start sideways at the rail, when there wasn’t a board free anywhere. By the time I had her fingers I could haul my foot out from under a tram driver’s stool and we were off. We could do a round without either of us yelling a count. I could tell you a thing Rika used to do just to see whether I’d keep up with her, but you’re wondering if I’ve been making your hand do things since you got here.”

I was wondering both, in that order. “Could you get it to let go if it had hold of you?”

She turned toward me at that. The red band of light under the bar put a shine on the brass zipper of her vest. She took the steel thumb between a finger and her own thumb, loosely, where I could pull it out. My arm bent at the elbow. I straightened it and felt the resistance let up.

“You can get it to let go,” she said. “It’s made to work off you. You’d have the weight of whoever was on the other end as well, if you waited till I was down. We used to have a call to change before that. My right knee went through a deck board once and for a year I was the first to use it wherever we went. I used to run a beautiful little game on the men who came in thinking I was about to need saving. Let them begin to catch me, then down they went. Some of them got a taste for it. That lot at the corner can tell you.”

She had a way of delivering a sentence to me and then turning the end of it out toward everybody within reach. The tram driver looked up in time to see her point at him with the stem of her glass. He smiled and gave the glass back to the bartender with his tram keys in it, the way an old customer could ask a man to keep him in a room a little longer.

“Show me the change,” I said.

She swept a patch of zinc dry between our drinks. She tapped twice with her middle finger and once with her ring finger, all in a rush. I laid my left hand in the dry patch. I could pick a bit of paper off a coin. I could have done a whole stack of them faster with that hand than with the right. It took me three tries to get the ring finger to carry the last tap all by itself. She let my first two attempts lie there, then brought her hand down flat over mine on the third. I thought she meant well done until I felt her bear down. I did it once more, slowly, and on the last tap she took her hand off.

I found I’d got so close to her that the wet ends of her hair would have reached my cheek if she’d shaken her head. I stood back enough to have a look at her. She picked up her drink and regarded me with as much ease as she’d had before I’d put my arm between us. I wanted to know what she had started to say with her hand when she’d reached for the thumb. I’d need a better acquaintance with her before I could ask.

“Come out with me when they clear. What do I call you if I’ve only got one breath to do it in?”

“Odile will do if you start on the right note.” She got a strap of her vest out from under the band of hair at her neck, put two more twists in the band and had the whole of her face to look out of. “And you?”

“Keva.” There was enough left of both drinks to leave them where they were.

* * *

A horn played two notes from the bottom of its range, and a man by the mirrors finished whatever he was saying to his wife and put their chairs one on top of the other. Along the edge of the floor people sucked in their stomachs to let the dancers past. Here you could go up to a person you hadn’t seen since last winter and put a hand under her elbow, and she might ask after your mother as the two of you went by the bar. You had the length of the song before they wanted their chairs back.

Odile put a shoulder between two men who were talking across the opening. The brass buttons on a driver’s coat scraped one of my buckles when I went in after her. Once we were clear she turned around and walked backward. I had thought she’d take both my hands before we set off. She waited with hers down by her thighs. I could hear the horn better here, with the speaker high up on the far wall, and feel the double stroke of the bass where the floor had worn thin. My hand was quiet. I’d been holding my left elbow too close to my ribs and I let it out.

I offered her my right hand. She put her left in it and settled her other hand lightly on my shoulder, over the lower buckle where a person could get purchase if she wanted it. The pink sign in the mirror lit one side of her face. She tipped her head to show me the stretch of boards behind her that no one had got to yet. I gave her a push with the heel of my hand and she went, grinning as if I’d put her up to something.

For all her talk about the hold, Odile danced at an unhandsome distance. A pocket of air stayed between her and me, so that when she stepped across me on the upswing it came as a surprise how near her thigh was. It took me four bars to believe she could be where she promised from so far away. Once I believed it I took longer steps. She let me have the run along the rail and I took it all the way down to the stack of glasses. Farther than we meant to go. A man with his back to us started to ease his way out with six drinks held against his stomach. I could feel the laugh begin in Odile before she opened her mouth. She swung us both round his back and never touched him. I kept my right hand wide for her to come back through and she came a little closer this time.

I had missed this. There were tables at home where I could make a corner with my feet when I was waiting for the kettle to boil, and a landing where there was a beautiful run of four boards from my door to the neighbour’s, if nobody happened to be coming up the stairs. Near the mirrors my shoulder went blue where the enamel plate passed the bottle lights. I saw Odile see it. She glanced down at the plate as I raised the hand to go by another couple. Her mouth stayed open a little after the laugh. I’d brought her out there with the arm in plain sight and I wanted her to look at it for as long as she liked. I went on dancing while she did.

She brought her mouth to my ear when the music went back to the first figure. I felt her say something before I heard any of it. It was my name. I gave her a small pull with my right hand and she turned until we were facing the bar together. She came so close on my left that her heel clipped mine. The forearm ran up hard against my side. My steel hand slipped behind her and found the place where the leather of her vest ended at her waist. The thumb turned in.

I could feel the rivet in the strap where it crossed my back. Odile was still over her feet and the motor was getting ready to have all of her. All I had to do was set my right foot a little farther back and she would follow that hand down into a sweep it had already had years to learn. In the blue light I saw her eyes go from my face to the thumb. She covered it with her hand and pressed the flat place with the pad of her own thumb as if she had found it in the dark. Her chin lifted. I saw the beginning of her breath go and knew the rest of what she was expecting.

I’d have liked to catch her. We had the room for it and there was a terrific look of ease about the way she started down. I could give her the whole thing she remembered. Under my right foot the board ran lengthwise, with a little play between it and the next one. I pressed my heel down where it was. The strap went tighter across my back. I edged my wrist away from her waist while the thumb stayed where she had it. The middle finger came clear and I brought it down twice. I could feel my mouth open from the effort of keeping two fingers still and getting the ring finger up by itself. It had taken three tries to do it clean on the bar. I got the one there was time for.

She was close enough for me to see where her zipper had caught a thread, with the hair she’d bundled up two minutes ago coming down beside her mouth. She still had the thumb. At the bar I’d thought she’d straighten up as soon as I got the taps off. There was a long, bare part of the music in which she might still lie back against my hand. I watched her come over her own feet and take her hand away. Her eyes lifted from the thumb to find mine.

Odile turned in against me. The way she put her left arm around my waist left my right hand free to find her shoulder, and I felt her take one step that put her in a place I could fall toward. She was waiting while I still had my weight in my heels. The lights over the bar had made a bent red line in the mirror. I had time to see it from halfway down and then to lose it and see Odile’s face in its place. She had a wide stance and a shoulder as firm as the rail. I’d been expecting a jolt when she got my whole weight, but all I felt was the warm shape of her arm coming up under my ribs. I let the left arm hang. The strap went loose across my back.

She brought me up slowly enough to let my heels find the floor. My right hand stayed on her shoulder and I could feel that she’d worked for it from the different lengths of her breaths. Her smile came back on one side first. The rail was a few feet away. In the mirror you could see a red stemmed glass beside a spot where somebody had managed to leave a whiskey standing. The horn played its two low notes again. Odile drew her hand away from my waist and it hung between us, palm up. She’d started to turn it over to let it fall when I put my left hand in it.
