# The Return Line

Iona was tightening a knot that might someday save a stranger's life when her workbench vanished.

She landed on a shelf of stone no wider than her kitchen table. Cold wind went up her sleeves. Tav hung below her by one hand, his boots scraping at empty air.

"Hello," he said, with the startled politeness he always used when his clasp fetched her.

Iona dropped flat and caught his wrist. Her other hand found a root wedged into the rock. It held while Tav got a knee over the edge. They lay side by side until his breathing slowed. Then he sat up and took off his coat.

"Keep it," she said.

"You're in your shirtsleeves."

"I was indoors."

He laid the coat over her anyway. His homeward clasp, a copper ring set with three small knots, hung dull against his chest. When an adventurer's fall was about to kill them, the knots drew within reach the person who most wanted that adventurer home. It had done exactly what the guild promised. Tav had survived.

The guild's promise ended there. Iona and Tav still had half a mountain to climb down, and she had come without her boots.

By the time they reached the guildhouse, the soles of her feet were sore and Tav had apologized seven times. She had accepted twice. More apologies would not recover the afternoon's work or the bread she had left rising in a warm room.

At her bench, the knot she had been tightening lay open. She could see the little flaw in it now, a crossed strand that might have broken under a fall. Being taken away had spared some future adventurer that danger, at least. She untied it and began again.

Tav watched from the door. "I can stop wearing it."

She looked at the bruises coming up on his wrist, the place where she had caught him. Last winter he had broken two ribs when a bridge gave way. This was the first time the clasp had saved him since.

"You said you were taking the ridge path," she told him.

"I was. The path went with the rock."

"I'll tell you when I want you to take it off."

He stayed long enough to light the lamp over her work.

The next morning Iona was examining a new batch of clasps when a woman in a wet cloak came into the workroom. Fen was the guild's safety officer. She carried a board of incident reports and had the sort of brisk step that usually meant somebody had omitted a date.

"I need your seal on six," she said.

"For what?"

"A retrieval party. South face of the Crown."

Iona set down her needle. An injured man was sitting on a bench behind Fen, one ankle splinted. Iona knew Orren by sight. He led expeditions to the old observatory when the stars were clear. Today he stared past the rows of copper clasps as if any one of them might answer a question.

"Bela," he said. "Have they found her?"

Fen's fingers tightened on the report board. "We're assembling a party."

Orren looked at Iona. "She was making breakfast. One moment I was falling, and there she was beside me, holding on to my belt. The stone between the shelf and the path broke off after the others got me out. They couldn't cross back to her."

Iona imagined a woman carried from her kitchen to a mountainside in whatever she had happened to be wearing. She had been lucky yesterday. Tav had been close enough to help her walk down.

Fen turned a page. Under **Guild members recovered**, Orren's name was marked in green. Under **Witnesses remaining at site**, someone had written *one civilian, unconfirmed condition*.

"Why is she a witness?" Iona asked.

"She isn't a member of the party."

"The clasp made her one."

Fen did not argue. That was more frightening than if she had.

Orren tried to stand. The splint slipped on the floor. "I told them she was there."

"You did," Fen said. "The retrieval request went in before dawn. It waited behind two member casualties for approval. I took it out of the queue myself."

Iona looked at the six clasps on her bench. She had made every one. Her seal meant that each knot had the required three turns and could bear the guild's test weight. The guild's trials counted a clasp successful when its wearer lived. Nobody's form asked what happened to the person it brought. She had written three reports about being summoned herself. The first had come back marked *no member injury*. She had filed the other two in her own drawer and kept sealing clasps.

Tav wore one. That had been reason enough, until now, to do work she no longer trusted.

"I can't seal these for your party," she said.

Fen glanced at Orren. "The fastest team can leave in ten minutes if they have them."

"And if one of them slips?"

"We may have another person to bring down," Fen said. She was looking at the clasps, not at Iona. "What will you bring instead?"

Iona went to the rope rack. It took nearly an hour to gather enough line to cross a broken path and bring two people back. She laid out two coats, a water flask, and a pair of boots close to her own size. Orren said Bela's feet were smaller. Iona found another pair.

All the while she could feel the hour going by on the mountain. She could have sealed the six clasps in half that time. Fen might have taken a quick team and found Bela sooner. Iona did not know whether the extra line would matter more than the lost light. She tied it anyway, checking each length with hands that wanted to hurry.

Tav came in as she was coiling the last of it. He looked at Orren, at Fen, then at the untouched clasps.

"I'll go," he said.

"I know."

For once neither of them told the other to be careful.

The south face of the Crown was not a place for a straight line. The path doubled back under pale rock, crossed a slope of loose stones, then narrowed where the mountain dropped away. Tav led with Fen behind him. Iona carried the coats and most of the rope. She had to stop twice to ease the strain on her shoulders. The second time, Tav took a coil without asking. She let him.

They saw Bela before they reached the break. She stood on a shelf across a gap of perhaps eight paces. A fold of the mountainside had torn loose between her and the path. Below it was a long fall, though Iona tried not to look long enough to judge how long.

Bela raised one hand. She was in a thin blue house dress and had wrapped her arms in a piece of canvas. Her feet were bare.

"Orren?" she called.

"At the guildhouse," Iona called back. "His ankle's hurt. He's safe."

Bela lowered her hand to the rock beside her. For a moment that was all she did.

Fen studied the gap. The face above Bela was sound enough for an anchor. Tav had brought a small bow and a light cord. He sent an arrow over, not toward Bela but into the scree behind her. She walked to it slowly and pulled the cord free.

"Tie it around the stone above your head," Iona called. "Can you reach it?"

"I can reach it," Bela said. "Tell me what you need."

She secured the cord where Iona pointed. Together they used it to draw a heavier line across. Iona tested both anchors and found one of hers set in cracked stone. She moved it. The sun was already touching the western ridge.

"How long?" Fen asked.

"Longer if we rush it."

Tav stood beside Iona with the second line in his hands. His clasp glinted at his throat. If he fell while she was across the gap, the clasp could take her from Bela and set her beside him without warning. It had never asked whether her hands were busy saving somebody else.

He followed her glance and unclipped it.

Iona wanted, absurdly, to make him put it back. He noticed. His mouth twisted, not quite a smile.

"I know you're here," he said. "That's why I have to take it off."

He put the clasp in his pocket and wound the line once around the sound rock Fen had chosen. His hands shook. Iona had watched him step into caves full of things that hissed and scrape at locked doors with the heel of a dagger. She had not often seen him afraid of his own weight.

"If I fall," he said, "hold the rope."

"Yes."

"And if you need me to let go?"

"I'll tell you."

Iona went over the gap in a loop of rope, with Tav and Fen taking her weight from their side. She kept her eyes on Bela. The woman had left a good handhold clear and was waiting to catch the end of the return line. That small preparation steadied Iona more than the knots.

On the shelf, Iona passed her the coat first. Bela put it on without ceremony. Her fingers were stiff enough that Iona fastened it for her, then sat beside her while she worked the boots over her feet.

"What took so long?" Bela asked.

It was a fair question. Iona told her about the approval queue, and about the rope.

"An hour for rope?"

"Nearly."

"I wish you'd tied faster."

"So do I."

Bela pressed the heel of one borrowed boot against the rock. "You weren't the one waiting."

Iona had been snatched onto cold stone before. She had never sat on it from dawn until afternoon with no certainty anyone was coming. She did not ask Bela to forgive the hour.

They fitted Bela into the second loop. Bela checked where it pressed against her ribs and asked Iona to shift it higher. She took the canvas she had used for warmth and wrapped it around a sharp spur of rock where the line might rub.

"I would have gone after Orren," she said as they worked. "If someone had told me he was falling, I would have gone. I'd have brought my boots."

The return crossing was slower. Halfway over, the wind turned, pushing Bela away from the line. She caught herself, but the sudden weight pulled the rope against its anchor. Tav took the strain. Iona saw him slide a step on the grit and heard Fen call his name.

For an instant Iona saw the whole simple, cruel answer: Tav's clasp could have drawn her back to hold him. It was in his pocket now. Fen dropped to a knee beside him and took the line. Tav found his footing. Together they brought Bela over.

Tav's palms were red where the rope had run through them. He closed them before Iona could inspect them. She was too tired to insist.

At the guildhouse, Orren was waiting on the bench where they had left him. Bela stopped just inside the door. He tried to rise; she crossed the room and sat beside him instead. She took his hand, held it a moment, and then asked why he'd let the others carry him away.

He began to answer. She listened. They would need more than one conversation, Iona thought, and none of it belonged in a guild report.

Fen cleared a table for the incident book. She crossed out **Witnesses** and wrote **Persons placed in danger by guild equipment**. She gave Bela the pen.

Bela looked down at the page. "Will this bring me home any faster next time?"

"It won't," Fen said. "But I can halt a device after an unrecorded injury. Your account means I can do that today. The council will have to decide what replaces it."

Bela touched the maker's seal on one of the clasps Fen had brought. "Yours?"

"Yes," Iona said.

"You knew it could leave someone up there?"

Iona thought of the reports in her drawer. "I knew it had left me behind. I kept signing them."

"Put that in the book, too."

Bela wrote slowly. Iona did not try to read over her shoulder.

Fen posted the suspension before dusk. No homeward clasp could be issued until the guild could name a willing helper in advance and show how that person would get back. The present clasps could do neither. A knotwright from the next city might offer a new design. For now, the six unsealed clasps lay on Iona's bench, and the winter orders that would have paid her rent were stopped with them.

Tav came to help her close the workroom. He set his clasp on the table. Neither of them reached for it.

"You could have told me to take it off months ago," he said.

"I did tell you, once. Then I asked you to put it back on."

"I remember."

Iona folded the coat he had lent her yesterday. It still smelled of mountain grass. She handed it to him.

"I'm afraid of what happens to you without it," she said.

"So am I."

They carried the coils of rope outside together, past the new notice.

At the corner where their streets divided, Tav offered her coil back. Iona left it on his shoulder.

"Walk me home," she said.
