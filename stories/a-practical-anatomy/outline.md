# A Practical Anatomy

Provisional direction: a warm, dryly comic first-person veterinary case, told by an experienced small-town practitioner whose pride is largely justified. Roughly 2,500 words, confined to the clinic. The pleasure is watching good animal medicine become inventive under pressure; the chimera remains an animal with recognizable attachments and inconvenient habits.

A familiar client brings in Crumpet, her young chimera: lion head and forequarters, a goat head rising at the shoulders, goat hind legs, and a snake-headed tail. The lion coughs, the goat's belly is swelling, and the snake keeps trying to curl itself into an impossibly small knot. Each head reacts differently to examination; their owner knows how to comfort each, but cannot explain the illness. The missing collar bell is recognized only after examination reveals something that chimes inside.

The first ordinary bloat intervention goes visibly wrong: a soft tube down the goat's throat emerges from the snake tail's mouth. There is one crowded fork, where goat throat and snake gullet join above the goat's stomach. The snake swallowed the bell; it sits over the stomach opening and deflects the tube into the free tail branch instead of letting gas out. The gas-swollen stomach is squeezing the lion's breathing. There is no known safe whole-body sedative dose for this patient's shared circulation, nor time to send her away.

The treatment must follow the discovered arrangement. The veterinarian works the tube beside the bell and vents gas for the lion's breath. That relief also frees the pressure-pinned bell: it turns, and its rim catches. A straight pull is now unsafe. The two approaches discovered through the failed examination become useful: support and turn the bell through the goat's tube while retrieving it along the snake's passage. The owner's learned handling maintains the tail in a broad supported curve around a forearm rather than its defensive self-knot. Keep the lion's audible breathing present between procedural beats. One junction and one consequential setback suffice; no extra shared-nerve rule, sudden new magic, or cure by affection.

Recovery brings the heads' different appetites and habits back together. The doctor's confidence has become more particular, not been exposed as fraud. End in physical contact with the recovering creature, whose snake tail settles around the doctor's wrist while the lion and goat find their own ways to crowd in. The escaped bell can remain on the counter, now a ridiculous little thing beside the amount of trouble it caused.
