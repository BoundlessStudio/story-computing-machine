# A Practical Anatomy

Provisional direction: a warm, dryly comic first-person veterinary case, told by an experienced small-town practitioner whose pride is largely justified. Roughly 2,500 words, confined to the clinic. The pleasure is watching good animal medicine become inventive under pressure; the chimera remains an animal with recognizable attachments and inconvenient habits.

A familiar client brings in Crumpet, her young chimera: lion head and forequarters, a goat head rising at the shoulders, goat hind legs, and a snake-headed tail. The lion coughs, the goat's belly is swelling, and the snake keeps trying to curl itself into an impossibly small knot. A collar bell went missing yesterday. Each head reacts differently to examination; their owner knows how to comfort each, but cannot explain the illness.

The veterinarian first attempts an ordinary goat-bloat examination, then finds the apparently separate complaints move together. Listening and careful tracing reveal a shared, crowded arrangement: the tail's swallowing passage runs forward into the same upper abdomen as the goat's fermenting stomach, beside the lion's breathing apparatus. The snake swallowed the bell. It is wedged at that junction, trapping the goat's gas; the expanding stomach is now squeezing the lion's breath. Conventional whole-body sedation would affect the three kinds of nervous tissue differently through their shared blood. There is neither a textbook diagram nor time to send Crumpet away.

The treatment must follow the actual discovered arrangement rather than three species' standard protocols. First relieve the trapped gas to make room, keeping the lion calm and its airway safe; then use warmth, support, and a carefully guided instrument to retrieve the bell back along the snake's passage without pulling against its teeth or twisting the shared body. The owner has a consequential part in keeping the heads settled and positioned while the veterinarian improvises the sequence and changes it when a reflex goes wrong. Give the procedure a real setback and a visible anatomical cause; no speech, sudden new magic, or cure by affection.

Recovery brings the heads' different appetites and habits back together. The doctor's confidence has become more particular, not been exposed as fraud. End in physical contact with the recovering creature, whose snake tail settles around the doctor's wrist while the lion and goat find their own ways to crowd in. The escaped bell can remain on the counter, now a ridiculous little thing beside the amount of trouble it caused.
