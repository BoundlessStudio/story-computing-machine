# The Healer's Measure

Ruva saw the wrist before she saw the opening.

Vekran's practice blade was high, his right shoulder bare to a clean strike. That was the opening. The wrist beneath it was the truth: two tendons drawn tight under the skin, the thumb turned inward, one narrow line where a shallow cut would empty the hand without ruining it.

She went for the shoulder.

Three paces of sand vanished under her boots. Vekran did not retreat. He set the iron length of his blunted blade against hers, breathed out, and turned.

Pressure traveled through the blades like a kick from a mule. He had declared one turn before they began, and he spent it neatly. Ruva's own charge carried her past him. Her back foot crossed her front; the yard tipped; she landed hip-first in the sand.

Vekran stopped his blade a finger's width from her ribs.

"Halt."

Ruva lay still until the iron went away. The packed practice yard had been raked, its boundary checked, both blades inspected in her hands. Six weeks of bruises had taught her that Vekran meant every safety rule he gave her. They had not taught her how to fall without feeling personally insulted by the ground.

"Again," she said.

"What did you decline to strike?"

She sat up. "If you're looking for a graceful description of my failure, you'll have to recruit a poet."

"I recruited a healer."

There it was again: an answer shaped like a shut door. In every village along the road, children played at witchknights with broom handles. Soldiers lowered their voices over stories of warriors who could read a blow through iron and send it elsewhere. Vekran could have chosen any fighter who knew which end of a sword to hold. Instead he had walked into Ruva's treatment tent, watched her work for half a day, and offered her an apprenticeship she had spent six weeks proving she did not deserve.

He waited.

Ruva brushed sand from her sleeve. "Your wrist. Inside line. I could have stopped the hand without cutting the tendons if the blade were sharp."

"Yes. Next pass, that is your target."

"You want me to hesitate better?"

"I want you to reach the choice while someone is attacking you."

Hooves struck the road outside the yard. A rider pulled up hard enough to spray grit through the open gate. "The roadside infirmary. Medicine cart caught in an attack. The roof's going in."

Vekran was already exchanging the blunted blade for his edged one. "Ruva. Edged blade, warm cloak, healer's satchel."

She buckled on the edged apprentice blade and caught up the cloak and satchel from beside the fence. "All three?"

"All three."

The sword pulled at one hip. The satchel dragged at the other. She followed him through the gate with neither weight settled.

* * *

The medicine cart stood crooked across the infirmary courtyard, one wheel wedged against a stone trough. Bolts had punched through its canvas cover. A driver lay beside it with blood in their hair, and two attackers had the cart pinned from opposite sides while pressure groaned through the low building beyond.

Vekran touched the flat of his blade to the gatepost.

For one breath, nothing visible happened. Then his attention moved across the yard: roof, doorway, cart, hands. Ruva had learned enough of the measure to feel only a muddle through her own iron. He seemed to find every load at once.

A crossbow snapped.

Vekran stepped into the bolt. His blade met it at an angle too slight to stop anything. Breath and warmth moved from his living grip through the iron focus; the bolt changed course and buried itself in the empty water trough with a crack.

He was at the cart before the shooter had lowered the bow. An attacker cut for his neck. Vekran caught the sword close to its guard, bound it, and drove one shoulder into the wielder's chest. The same turn of his body put his free bracer under a lintel that tore loose above the driver. He sent the timber sideways into bare paving, twisted the sword from the attacker's hand, and kicked it under the cart.

One step, two threats, no wasted reach. This was why soldiers lowered their voices.

"Ruva. Take the driver. Rear route."

It sounded too much like stay out of the way.

She slid beside the driver anyway. "Can you hear me?"

The driver's eyes opened. Their pupils matched. Breathing quick, no wet rattle. The scalp wound bled freely but the skull beneath felt sound.

"I'm going to move you behind the wall. Press here. Hard." She put their hand over a folded cloth. "Ready?"

At their nod she pulled, keeping the neck straight, until the stone well curb lay between them and the crossbow.

The infirmary door bowed inward. Two walking patients stumbled into the yard, one supporting the other.

"Rear gate," Ruva called. "Stay low. Take the driver when you pass."

The crossbow attacker reached for another bolt. Vekran slapped his blade against the cart's iron rim, read the incoming pressure, and turned the next shot high over the wall. The magic took heat with it. His knuckles had gone pale. His next breath ended short.

The disarmed attacker rose behind him with a knife.

"Left rear," Ruva shouted.

Vekran pivoted before the knife reached him. He caught the wrist, folded it without breaking it, and sent the attacker face-down across the cart tongue. The crossbow shooter met him on the other side; Vekran slipped inside the stock, struck once with his sword guard, and caught the weapon before its owner hit the dust.

Ruva bound the driver's head, checked the two walking patients, and found a third person beneath the cart with a bruised leg but a strong foot pulse. She showed the mobile pair where to lift the axle, freed the trapped leg, and sent all four toward the rear wall with the cart between them and the yard. Three hurt. None bleeding to death. No red priority.

She drew her edged apprentice blade. "My route is clear. I'm coming to the door."

"Keep evacuating."

"You took me as an apprentice, not a porter with bandages."

The building groaned again. Dust streamed from the eaves.

Vekran looked at her then, not away from the doorway but somehow through the same attention. "You own the rear route until there are no red-priority patients. Report."

"None red. One head wound controlled. One leg injury, stable. Two mobile."

"Then advance under a shield-sized hold. No farther than you can retreat in three steps."

It was not reassurance. It was usable.

They crossed the threshold together. Inside, two patients crouched behind an overturned table. At the far roof post, a figure in a dark coat had one shoulder pressed against the wood and one hand clenched around an iron clasp fixed through torn clothing. Four lines of pressure ran from that point into the ceiling braces. The person held a knife in the other hand.

Vekran moved between the table and a falling beam. He planted his sword point in a crack between two floorstones, set his palm to the doorframe, and made a fixed ward from one surface to the other. The beam stopped dropping. The whole room complained around him.

The knife flashed from beside the post. Vekran shifted enough to keep it from his ribs, but the edge opened his sleeve under the upper arm.

He did not lose the ward. His jaw tightened once.

"Lane," he said.

Pressure gathered along Ruva's bracer, no wider than a shield. It gave her a path past the hanging beam.

She went in.

* * *

The knot-caster's knife had fallen after the strike. Ruva kicked it away before she knelt. Their skin had the gray cast of worsening shock, and the hand below the injured shoulder was darkening at the fingertips. Blood soaked the coat around the iron clasp, though not fast enough to explain the pulse Ruva could see jumping there.

It beat twice for every beat in the caster's throat.

"Don't touch it," they said. Their breath came shallow. "I let go, the roof comes down. Your witchknight cuts me, it comes down faster. So perhaps we discuss a door."

Behind Ruva, Vekran's hold hummed through stone and iron. "I can part the hard line through clasp and shoulder. I cannot map past it while I carry these loads."

The fastest cut would end the living source. It would also cross muscle, vessels, perhaps the joint. Ruva looked at the four braces, each drawn toward the wounded shoulder as if the caster had tied the room to their own body and pulled.

"If I open a door," she said, "you can't walk to it. Your hand is losing blood. Your breath is failing."

"Then promise I leave alive."

"I can't promise freedom. I can examine the shoulder and press the wound, or I can stay back while he prepares the cut he can make. Those are the choices we have now."

The caster's mouth tightened. "A healer traveling with a witchknight. How comforting."

"Pressure, or no touch?"

The post creaked. One of the patients behind the table made a small sound.

"Pressure," the caster said. "The clasp stays."

Ruva set clean folded cloth around the iron, not over it. "I'm touching you now."

She pressed. The caster hissed and tried to turn away.

"Still. Or tell me to stop."

They held still.

Only then did Ruva give the healing working a little of her warmth. It passed through her palms, living attention following the gross boundaries beneath them: skin, torn muscle, the hard foreign edge of the clasp. She could encourage the small vessels to close and hold damaged edges where they belonged. She could not replace what had already been lost. She could not clean the wound while the iron remained, or give the caster back the heat shock had taken.

Cold climbed into her fingers.

The false pulse changed. Not a pulse at all. A second rhythm had been laid alongside the wound, tightening whenever the caster's breath stumbled. As Ruva held the tissue steadier, the pressure in the ceiling eased by a fraction.

Vekran felt it. "Again."

"I can't do more without spending what I'll need to move."

"Then don't. Name what changed."

Ruva followed the imposed rhythm as far as her touch allowed. It did not end at the clasp. It ran beside living tissue, borrowed the easiest path around the joint, then divided toward all four roof braces.

She took her hands away. "The working is braided along the wound. If you cut through the shoulder, all four loads release together."

The ward shuddered under Vekran's hand.

"My map was incomplete," he said at once. "I can keep the roof or attempt a second working. Not both."

No excuse. No performance of certainty. The room had become a measure of how long his body could remain warm enough to choose.

Ruva pulled her cloak around the caster's uninjured side and looked for another line.

* * *

Blood had reached Vekran's cuff.

"Your arm first," Ruva said. "Thirty heartbeats and I can bind it."

"Assess it from there."

"You need stitches."

"First death?"

She hated the answer. "No. Painful. Steady loss, but the arm still bears weight. Your hand?"

"Cooling. Full feeling for now."

He had not dismissed her judgment. He had asked her to use it.

Ruva returned two fingers to the caster's shoulder. With the wound steadier, she could feel a narrow interval where the iron clasp touched the shaped pressure without crossing flesh. A finger's width at most. It shifted with each breath.

"There," she said. "Nonliving line. But it moves. I can't mark it once and trust it."

"Then you cut from the live map," Vekran said.

"I've parted chalk cord twice. Once cleanly."

"Name what you require."

"Someone qualified."

"Unavailable."

The caster gave a breath-short laugh that broke into a cough. "Your standards inspire confidence."

Ruva ignored them. Listing reasons had not changed the room. She changed tactics.

"A shield lane. A count I don't have to keep myself. Permission to abort if I lose the line."

"Yes. I drop the lane if my fingers go numb. If you lose the line, you stop. No blind cut."

"And the roof?"

"About sixty heartbeats under this load when I began. Fewer now."

The caster's hand closed over the clasp. "It stays. It's the only reason your people haven't killed me."

"It's also killing you," Ruva said. "Hold still and warn me when the false branch moves. I will try to separate it without cutting your shoulder. If you live, you go to treatment under ordinary guard. Not free. Alive."

"Try?"

"That's the true word."

The caster looked past her to Vekran. "And him?"

"Those are the terms," Vekran said. His voice had gone plain and thin. "Your companions have fled or fallen. Choose."

Dust spilled from a new crack overhead.

The caster released the clasp, though their fingers hovered beside it. "It throws a false branch on the fourth beat after each tightening. I call it."

Ruva took chalk from her satchel and settled the edged apprentice blade in her grip. Stabilize for three breaths. Trace. Shift to guard. Turn the defensive pressure into bare floor. Part the braid. If the map blurred, stop.

Narrow enough to attempt. Too narrow for courage to improve.

* * *

Ruva tucked both hands into the warm fold of her cloak until her fingertips stung.

"Do I still have your yes to touch the shoulder?"

"Yes. The clasp stays until you cut it."

"If you pull away, I stop."

She cleaned the accessible skin, replaced the blood-wet cloth, and set her palms around the wound. Her warmth moved again. One breath: tissue edges, unsteady but distinct. Two: the caster's true pulse, quick beneath her little finger. Three: the imposed rhythm sliding beside it.

"Tightening," the caster whispered. "One."

Movement sounded at the broken side door.

An attacker from the courtyard lurched through, one hand braced on the frame, a short sword in the other. Their eyes found the caster.

"Traitor," they said, and drove forward.

Ruva released the healing working and stood. Six weeks had not taught her how to win a sword fight. Six weeks had taught her one short parry and how badly her guard recovered afterward.

The sword struck her bracer hard enough to bruise through leather. She caught it late, gave ground, and saw the attacker's wrist turn beneath the guard.

Inside line. The hand, not the shoulder.

Ruva cut once. The edge opened sleeve and skin shallowly across the exact disabling line. The attacker's fingers sprang loose. Their sword hit the floor.

She kicked it through the broken doorway. The attacker clutched the useless hand, stared at her, then stumbled after their fleeing companions rather than try Vekran's ward unarmed. Ruva did not pursue.

"Position," Vekran said.

Not praise. The thing she needed.

Ruva dropped beside the caster and raised a shield-sized hold as pressure snapped outward from the working they were still struggling to sustain. Her back foot slid in blood-damp dust. The hold buckled, flinging strain into her shoulder.

She let it go.

The pressure struck the wall above them and showered grit. Forcing the hold would have thrown it through the patients—or through Ruva.

"New count," she said. Her breath shook. "I lost the footing."

She shifted half a pace to dry stone. Her arm throbbed where the bracer had taken the sword. Vekran's hands shook now, too, but his ward remained fixed.

"Ready," he said.

Ruva put her palms back to the wound.

"Still yes?"

"Yes. Hurry."

"Call the branch. Nothing else."

The caster swallowed and nodded.

"One," Vekran counted.

Ruva found the living pulse.

"Two."

She found the false rhythm alongside it.

"Three."

She drew chalk across the clasp where the narrow gap lay at that instant. Then she released the healing hold. The wound stayed bound by cloth and its own encouraged clotting, not cured, not safe for long.

Ruva shifted to guard.

The caster's trained reflex tightened the working as pain caught their breath. Pressure lashed from the clasp toward Ruva's chest. She met one bounded line with her bracer, set her weight through the dry back foot, and turned.

Force hammered into the floor. Tile split with a flat crack. Numbness rushed from her wrist to her elbow.

Moved, not erased.

"One," the caster gasped. "Two—"

Ruva brought the edged iron toward the chalk mark.

"Don't. It moved."

The false branch slid toward the living pulse.

Vekran's breath hitched. The beam above him dropped the width of a nail before his ward caught it again.

Every urgent part of Ruva wanted to finish the stroke. Faster. Stronger. Before the room chose for them.

But there was no safe line beneath her blade.

She aborted, drew back, and waited.

"Three," the caster said. Their whole body strained against the need to move. "Four. Now."

The false branch passed. Beneath it, for less than a heartbeat, healer-sense gave Ruva the boundary her eyes could not: warm living tissue here, cold clasp there, shaped pressure crossing only the iron.

She cut on that line.

The blade passed through the edge of the clasp and the braided working with a sound like cloth tearing under water. The caster cried out but the edge cleared their shoulder.

The four roof loads released unevenly.

Vekran pulled his sword from the floor and turned with his whole body. He opened the ward toward the empty courtyard. Timber, tile, and force went outward through the path he chose, bursting across the cleared paving in a roar of dust.

Then he dropped the hold before it could take the last warmth from his hands.

Part of the roof fell where the medicine cart had stood before the patients dragged it back. The inner beam settled onto the overturned table instead of the people behind it.

Vekran sank to one knee, conscious, his sword point resting on stone.

Ruva did not check whether anyone had seen her cut. She put the blade down, caught the caster before they slumped against the post, and pressed fresh cloth to the shoulder.

"Stay with me," she said. "The spell is over. The wound isn't."

* * *

By the time guards arrived from the road, Ruva had found six injuries, none immediately fatal. The driver needed watching for confusion. The patient with the bruised leg needed a splint for the journey. The caster needed warmth, fluids if they could keep them down, and proper cleaning before anyone considered removing what remained of the clasp.

Vekran needed stitches.

"Cloak first," Ruva told him.

"The caster—"

"Has mine and two warmed bricks. Give me your arm."

He did. His cut was shallow but longer than she had hoped, and his skin had the waxy chill of spent measurework. She cleaned and bound it; stitching could wait until there was better light and a roof that did not shed dust into open wounds.

The caster watched from a litter while a guard secured their uninjured wrist. Ruva checked the restraint for circulation, then checked the shoulder binding again.

"This isn't pardon," the caster said.

"No. It's transport. Keep that hand still."

They looked as if they had another taunt available. Exhaustion won. The litter went toward the road under guard.

The infirmary had lost half its roof. The medicine cart had a split axle and three bolts through its canvas. Those who could walk gathered blankets and unbroken jars from the dust. There would be repairs, statements, custody, more treatment. Nothing Ruva had done made those costs vanish.

Vekran sat against the wall with his bandaged arm inside the cloak. "Assessment."

Ruva was too tired to pretend not to know what he meant. "Of me?"

"By you."

She wanted one word: worthy or mistaken. He had never offered comfort where evidence would do, so she gave him the evidence.

"My recovery is weak. I failed the first hold because I didn't clear my footing. The turn worked and numbed my arm. The parting worked because the caster corrected my timing. I stabilized two patients." She looked at his bandage. "And one mentor who still needs stitches."

"Accurate."

"Is that why you chose me? To keep a witchknight alive after the work?"

"Partly. Not chiefly." He flexed his fingers, stopped when they trembled, and put the hand back under the cloak. "Anyone can be taught where to cut. I needed an apprentice already trained to ask what has to live through it."

Ruva glanced at the ruined room. "Then you should have said you wanted a healer. Not someone you meant to turn into a former healer."

"I thought bringing the satchel made that plain."

"It did not."

He considered this with the same seriousness he gave a bad map. "My error."

"The satchel stays in every field kit. Healing shifts count as witchknight training, not time away from it. Those are my terms."

"Agreed."

No ceremony. Ruva found that she preferred it.

Vekran nodded toward the broken tile where her turn had landed. "Your back foot was late."

"I noticed."

"Tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow you sleep. The day after, perhaps you may criticize my feet."

His mouth moved at one corner, too tired to become a smile.

When the damaged medicine cart finally rolled toward town, Ruva walked beside the patients. Her healer's satchel crossed one shoulder. Her edged apprentice sword crossed the other, cleaned and returned to its sheath.

The two weights pulled differently. After a dozen steps, Ruva stopped and shortened the sword strap until they balanced. Then she walked on.
