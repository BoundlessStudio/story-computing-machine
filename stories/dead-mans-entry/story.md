Before we left Atlantic City, I bet a man two hundred dollars that he would need my help before we got to California. He had beaten me the previous summer, so I was prepared to be helpful up to the value of two hundred dollars.

That year he'd finished ahead of me with four broken ribs and a tie-down strap holding his Suzuki's fairing on. I had spent the winter thinking about those eleven minutes, sometimes when other people were talking to me.

“You're going to break my bike,” he said.

“I want you to write down that you said that. When I stop to help you, I want you to have it ready to read aloud.”

We were behind a fish warehouse. A truck driver kept leaning on her horn, and twenty-three people who knew exactly how to get out of her way pretended they couldn't hear her. The sun was coming up over the roofs. I remember the gulls stealing the starter's breakfast, because I laughed, and I remember that I was happy. I had the Yamaha beneath me, three thousand miles ahead, and a million dollars waiting to be taken off somebody's hands. I had won it twice. Nobody had yet asked me for any of it.

I was wearing shorts, with my riding pants rolled on the saddle behind me. A man had told me I ought to get dressed. He had his own name printed down both legs, which is a thing you can do if you have the money. I pushed my amber glasses down and watched him trying to work out whether I was laughing at him.

Then the Norton came through.

I could tell you I recognized the sound. Afterward everybody wanted that part to be the sound. But the fish truck was idling and a dozen bikes were running, and my father had been dead for twenty years. I saw the silencer first, flattened underneath by a curb in Camden. Then the saddle moved on its bracket. I had held the lamp while he welded that bracket crooked.

I got off the Yamaha and knelt beside the Norton. I put my hand under the stranger's saddle before I thought to ask his permission.

The ridge was there, thick at one end, thin where Dad had run out of patience. He had said it would hold until Saturday. It was a Thursday when he said it, and the following Thursday he was dead. I had touched that weld more often in my sleep than I ever had with my fingers.

“Easy,” the rider said. “You'll get grease on yourself.”

He took off his helmet. His hair had gone white above the ears, and he had acquired the soft, pale skin of a man who had spent years indoors. The broken little finger on his right hand still stood out when he laid it on the tank.

Len Barlow had ridden with my father, worked with him, and been taken away for an armored-truck job the summer Dad died. An electrical fire in the workshop had filled Dad's upstairs room with smoke while he slept. They let me go to his funeral in cuffs, with a deputy on either side. Before Len was arrested, he had come to see me in the county jail and told me what the fire had left. There had been a saucepan I could have if I wanted it. My first question had been about the Norton.

I stood up too fast and had to put a hand on his shoulder.

“You told me this burned.”

“There's a fair bit to explain, Dell. I thought we could get some breakfast down the road.”

“You brought it here.”

“I knew you'd be here.” His hand closed over the keys. “I've heard all about you. Only woman who ever won this twice. I was hoping I might get a word with you before everybody else started telling me who you are.”

The starter wanted to know what the trouble was. He had the prize case open on his table, the money in tight bands, and the truck driver had climbed down from her cab.

“Family friend,” I told him. “Arrived on some family property.”

Len's shoulder tightened beneath my hand. For a few seconds I wanted to hurt him badly enough to miss the start. I could see the particular place where his collarbone came to the skin. Then the woman from the truck said she was calling the police, and three men who had promised to race across America at any cost began putting their gloves on.

I took my hand away.

Len said, “I'm going south out of here. I've got somebody who'll look the bike over on the way. Less police, and you don't queue for a mechanic you know. Come with me. We can talk properly.”

It would put me behind him, on his roads, at his breakfast table. I had been a passenger on that Norton often enough. I looked at my own machine, the turquoise paint rubbed pale beneath my knees and the brown saddle patched with a piece of a handbag. It had carried me across the country with a broken collarbone on my first win. On the second I had finished with a fever so high the man paying me asked which of us was going to sign the receipt.

“Give me the keys,” I said.

“That's what we need to talk about.”

The starter struck the table with the flat of his hand. The truck driver was speaking into her phone now, looking directly at him.

The two proof stops that year were Tucumcari and Saratoga Gap. You had to show up at both before the people waiting on the coast would pay you. Everything between was your business. Len could have his quiet southern roads. The quicker line went through places where I knew the jail food already.

“I'll see you in Tucumcari,” I said. “Try not to sell any more of my belongings before I get there.”

I pulled my pants on over the shorts, retied the knot of hair that was coming loose, and tucked the end of my red scarf inside my jacket. The cloth smelled of coffee and the leather polish I'd spilled on it the night before. My hands shook as I buckled the helmet. I was angry about that too.

Len watched me mount. He looked pleased with something.

The starter dropped his arm. We left in a burst of exhaust that sent the gulls over the roof, and at the first light two riders had to brake for the fish truck. The woman driving it didn't look at us. I was past her turning circle before she could straighten, the Yamaha pulling cleanly, and Len's headlight stayed in my mirror until the roads divided.

Pennsylvania put rain inside my gloves. They dried hard in Ohio, and each time I stopped I had to bend the fingers before I could pull them on. A patrol car waiting outside a service plaza cost me six minutes and a breakfast I hadn't wanted. I had enough theft convictions to fill both sides of the form, and a bench warrant I preferred to discuss after I had money for a better lawyer. The road to the next state looked very good to me.

In Illinois, the Suzuki rider needed my tire gauge. He paid me two hundreds and read his accusation aloud from the back of a receipt. I lent him the gauge and stayed to watch him use it, because I wanted to hear the bit about breaking his bike again. He finished first and was gone while I was still getting my gloves back on.

We lost a rider outside Springfield. His bike was being put on a flatbed when I passed, and he stood against a police car with his hands on the roof. I had to make myself look back at the road. That night I slept three hours with my red scarf over my eyes and the helmet against my ribs, then woke convinced I had missed the whole second day.

Across Oklahoma I kept trying out explanations for the Norton. Len had found it in a barn. He had bought a burned engine and rebuilt the rest. Someone had told him where it was while he was in prison. I gave him different amounts of credit depending on how tired I was. In every version he eventually put the keys in my hand. By the time I reached Tucumcari, I had got quite good at making that part happen.

* * *

At the Sundown Motor Court in Tucumcari, a woman with a basket of towels made me move the Yamaha three times before she was satisfied. There were yellow lines for the bikes and yellow lines for the laundry cart. I had stopped with a wheel in the wrong kind.

The mechanic at the service bay wanted my rear tire off. I wanted it back on before he had taken it off, which he said was usual. I got out of my jacket and pants and sat on a chair outside room six, in the shorts I'd started in, with my braid unraveling down one shoulder. The chair's metal arms were hot enough to hurt. Inside, a fan pushed the smell of somebody else's soap through the doorway.

Len arrived while I was pouring ice down the back of my neck, soaking the yellow-striped top beneath my open collar. He brought the Norton across the courtyard and set it on its stand where I could see it. He had put nearly two thousand miles on it since I had last touched the bracket. There were new insects on the headlight.

He saw me looking at them.

“My brother's boy went through it before I brought it out. Tires, seals, all that. It's been looked after.”

“You found it in the fire and put it back together?”

He pulled off his gloves one finger at a time. “There wasn't much putting back together. Let me get a drink. You ought to have something with a bit of salt in it. I've been eating those crackers they give you with soup.”

I stood in the doorway when he came back with two cold colas. He offered me one and waited until I took it. That annoyed me more than it should have. I'd been ready to refuse a can of something I wanted.

He leaned against the other side of the door and began.

“The fire was out by the time they let me into the yard. Your dad had already been taken away. I got a call because there were things they needed moving and nobody wanted to make a decision about them. You were still inside, and there wasn't any point asking you to look at a list of burned tools. I borrowed a trailer. Most of the building was down. The paint shed, that little corrugated place at the back, hadn't caught. Your dad had left the Norton there under a sheet. There wasn't a mark on it.”

He stopped to drink. On the far side of the court, the woman with the towels was trying to get a room door open with her elbow. A visitor held it for her and asked whether this was some kind of rally. She told him the men in the service bay would be gone by midnight.

Len said, “Arty owed me twenty-four hundred dollars for the engine work. I paid the machine shop. The money was going to come back after the summer, and then the summer didn't happen. So I put the bike on the trailer. I took it to my brother's place. There was dry storage there. My own place was up for sale by then. When the truck case came up, I told my brother to keep it until I came home. His boy used to ride it to work. It's been out on the road, Dell. It hasn't been rotting in a box.”

The can was cold enough to make my fingers ache. I rolled it down my bare brown forearm before moving it to the other hand.

“You took it before you came to the jail.”

“That was the order things happened in.”

“And you sat across from me and said the frame was melted.”

“I shouldn't have used that word. But your father owed me the money. That isn't something I've come up with now. He'd have told you himself.”

I remembered the engine cases laid out on the bench and Len bringing a bill in an envelope. Dad had put the envelope underneath a mug. When he lifted the mug later, the envelope had come up with it. He'd said he'd better pay it before he ate it by mistake.

“He owed you,” I said. “I'll give you twenty-four hundred dollars. You give me the bike.”

“It doesn't work out at that now.”

“Then give me a bill for the storage. Put your nephew's mileage on it as a credit.”

He took a longer drink. He looked down the court toward the service bay, where my Yamaha stood with its rear wheel out. I could see the empty curve between the two shocks. For the first time since leaving New Jersey, I disliked being so far from it.

“You weren't there,” Len said. “Somebody had to go down and do those jobs. I took care of things. And when you got out, you had something to start with. You got the money from the insurance, didn't you?”

“The building was rented. There was no insurance money for me.”

“Well, I wasn't told about that.” He turned the can in his hand. “I wasn't told a lot of things. I had my own trouble coming by then.”

I had thought, before he started, that he might have saved it for me. There had been twenty years in which I could have been riding it, but at least I could have stopped wondering what it looked like burned. Now I was standing close enough to smell the warm cola on his breath and trying to imagine him carrying a saucepan out of that yard for me while the Norton waited on his trailer.

“I wrote to the scrap man,” I said. “The one you gave me the address for. I asked if he'd kept the tank. I told him I'd pay him for it, and he sent a photograph of what he'd hauled out of the building. He must have thought I was crazy. I kept looking through that photograph for a piece of gold paint. I was trying to buy a bit of a motorcycle you'd already put in somebody's shed.”

Len rubbed the broken little finger against his palm.

“I thought you were past all that,” he said. “When I saw you'd won the race. You seemed to have got on with things.”

“I got on a different motorcycle.”

There was a pale wet mark on the front of his shirt where the can had rested. I watched it drying. I didn't want to cry in front of him, and I was having to think about ordinary stupid things to prevent it: his shirt, the grit inside my boots, whether the mechanic had charged me for a tire with the right rating. Dad had cut the sleeves out of his best jacket to make me my first riding gloves. The fingers were different lengths. I had left them in a cellophane property bag when I got arrested and never asked for them back. I had been sure he could make me another pair.

I drank some of the cola. It was too sweet, and I wanted the rest.

“What arrangement are you offering, Len?”

He had been waiting for me to ask it that way. His shoulders came away from the doorframe.

“Pull out here. Ride the Yamaha home, let an old man have a run at it. When I've finished, my brother's boy will bring the Norton to you. I'll pay for the transport. You've got your two wins, Dell. Nobody can take those off you. You could have this in your own garage next week.”

“You want me to watch you win a million dollars on my father's motorcycle.”

“I want us to come away with something each. I'm trying to be practical. A million dollars is a lot of money to you. It's a lot more to me at the moment.”

He was doing better than I'd expected. I could picture the black tank between my knees on the road near home, at a speed nobody would pay to watch. I could take the crooked bracket off at last and keep it in a drawer. I would wait a week while he got his applause from people who remembered a quicker, thinner Len. I was already deciding where to put the Yamaha so there would be room.

From the bay, the mechanic called that somebody had parked in front of his compressor. The man with his name printed down his pants went to move his bike. He looked at the Norton as he passed.

“That's a nice one,” he said.

“It's mine,” I told him.

Len looked at me.

“I mean to make that plain,” I said. I set my can in the doorway and walked to the Norton. The tank was warm through the palm of my hand. Two people had passed me while Len was telling his story. My tire was still not on, and I had been allowing him to take all the time he wanted.

He followed me into the court. “We can go inside if you'd rather.”

“I'd rather race you for it.”

The man in the printed pants stopped beside his bike. A rider waiting for fuel at the bay turned round too.

Len said, “I didn't come here to take your Yamaha.”

“Then you'll be doing a very familiar thing by accident. First one of us to finish the whole race gets both bikes. If someone else wins the million, we still settle between us. If neither of us gets there, we each keep what we brought.”

The man in the printed pants said, “You are putting that Yamaha in? The turquoise one?”

He'd watched me change its plugs in a motel parking lot the previous summer, after I'd promised him I could do it before his coffee cooled. He had brought me a second coffee to make sure I finished.

“It comes with a new back tire, if they ever put it on. Look after it if you get it, Len. It has a very low opinion of most people.”

The other rider had taken his phone out. Len saw the phone, then looked toward the motor court's sign, as if somebody might be waiting beside it to tell him whether this was a good idea.

“You want to make it a public thing,” he said.

“You brought it to the start.”

He shifted his weight. I thought I could see what appealed to him: my Yamaha to win, and my father's bike between me and the finish. He would get to make me chase it for another thousand miles. The rider by the bay was already talking into his phone, telling somebody on the coast to listen to this.

“First one to finish,” Len said. “Both machines. I'm not answering for it if you come off trying to save a few seconds.”

“Just answer for the keys.”

He held out his hand. His grip was dry and strong. When he released mine, he looked at the men beside the bay and gave them a little nod. He was back among people who could see him keep his word. He liked that better than I had expected him to.

I went to reclaim the Yamaha. The mechanic had put the new tire on and was putting air in it. I pulled my riding pants over my boots, watching the gauge.

“You'll have to let me do this part,” he said.

“I've paid for a very particular amount of air.”

He made me laugh. I couldn't even remember afterward what he said. I was looking at the Norton while Len tied his bag on, and laughing, and I had to sit down on my own brown saddle before I could get my gloves on.

We went west into the evening. I put three riders behind me before the light was gone, lost two of them while I stopped for fuel, and had to do it again in the dark. The extra time at Sundown kept returning to me in the shape of somebody else's taillight. In Arizona a truck threw a piece of tread across my path, and I swerved so hard the bag on my saddle shifted. I stopped to retie it with my stomach still trying to move sideways. I had known a rider whose race ended with less warning than that. They brought his helmet to the finish the year I first won.

Near Barstow I caught Len buying an egg sandwich. He offered me half, holding it out until I took it. I was still chewing when he left. The Suzuki was somewhere ahead of both of us. I slept another three hours outside Bakersfield and woke with the scarf folded in my hand. My arms ached while I was lying still; once the Yamaha was under me again, I could forget them for a few miles at a time.

The fourth morning came up beyond the last stretch of freeway. We climbed toward the proof stop in a little procession, the Suzuki ahead, Len next, me behind him, all three machines black with insects and road dirt. When I saw the Norton at a bend above me I no longer had to guess how it had got there. I wanted the man on it to look in his mirror and find me closer.

* * *

At Saratoga Gap on the fourth morning, the woman taking our numbers wore a fleece and held a cup of coffee in both hands. I wanted to put my hands round it without asking her. There was damp in the air, damp on the road, and a smell of trees that came through even the old sweat in my helmet.

The Suzuki went first. Len rolled out behind it. I was third, close enough to see the Norton pitch slightly as he changed down for the first bend. The woman at the van had told us we were the first three through. There was nobody ahead of us now except ordinary people going somewhere.

My braid had fallen down inside my jacket. Its end had been rubbing one place between my shoulder blades since Barstow. The red scarf had bunched under the helmet strap and I could feel every fold when I swallowed. I left both alone. The Yamaha was warm and willing beneath me, and the road went down among redwoods so tall I couldn't see where their tops began.

For the first few miles the three of us kept our places. I could make up ground under braking, then the bend would open and Len would have it back. He was good. I had spent a day and a half being angry at him and had started to think that ought to make him slow. He took the wet patches cleanly and never once looked over his shoulder. When he wanted to know where I was, he used the mirror.

Lower down, we caught a truck carrying stacked timber. The Suzuki closed up behind it. Len came up behind the Suzuki and I had to brake hard enough to feel the weight of my bag move against my back. The cut ends of the timber filled the road ahead. Diesel hung between the trees. We went round two bends with nowhere to get by, each of us making room and taking it away again.

The road opened to a longer view. The Suzuki's rider moved his head out, looked, and drew back. Len went past him. The Norton came alongside the truck, small and black beside its wheels, and I could see clear road beyond both of them. I followed.

The Suzuki's front wheel was beside my boot for a second. He had been deciding whether to take the opening when Len took it away from him. Now I was taking the rest. I kept looking beyond the truck's cab, where I wanted to be, while the air at its side pushed the Yamaha toward the center line. My wrist wanted to turn farther than the engine could answer. Then the cab fell behind and there was space to move back in. Len was already setting himself for the next curve.

The truck was between us and the Suzuki. I could stop thinking about eleven minutes.

North of Felton the sun came through in pieces. A patch of road would shine ahead of us, then go dark under the trees before we reached it. Len had the middle of our lane. I moved toward the right as the road began to bend that way, and he turned in before I could draw alongside. By the time I reached his back wheel, he had left me a strip of broken edge and dirt. I slowed and went back behind him.

He stood the Norton up sooner than I expected. The early turn had carried him toward the center line at the exit. I had left myself too far back to use it.

On the next right bend I showed him my wheel again. His helmet moved a fraction, the mirror catching me. He closed the inside before I got there. This time I stayed nearer, and I saw him have to ease the throttle to keep from running over the line when the bend tightened. I could hear the change in his engine through the noise of my own.

Dad used to sit farther forward on that saddle. On long rides I had rested my chin between his shoulder blades until he complained I was wearing a hole in him. Len's bag was tied over the place where I had sat. It bounced once, and the loose end of a strap flicked at the bracket.

I looked past him. Another right, the far end visible between the trunks, with the road tightening before it straightened. There was no car coming the other way. I could see the strip I had to stay inside.

I brought the Yamaha toward his right again. His head moved. He began his turn early to shut me out, and I let him go without following him into it. I stayed farther left, on our side of the line, while the Norton leaned away from me. For a few yards his back wheel was getting farther ahead. I had to wait through that. My hands wanted to hurry the turn, and I held them where they were until I could put the Yamaha onto the road opening beneath his machine.

Then I turned.

He was already coming out wide. I went through on his right, my front wheel past his back wheel, the engines rising together. I could see the gold letters beside my knee. Len looked down and shifted the Norton inward.

The hit caught my left leg above the kneecap. My teeth met. I felt the whole weight of the Yamaha in my arms, as if somebody had handed it to me to carry, and for a second I couldn't tell how much of my leg was still on the peg. His black tank filled the edge of my sight. I had an absurdly clear view of a fresh scratch through the gold, bright metal showing underneath. I had spent a month of my eighteenth year trying to buy a piece of that paint.

There was road in front of me. I kept my eyes on it. The Yamaha shook once through the bars, my left boot found the peg again, and I stayed on the throttle until the Norton was behind my elbow. The bend eased. I got the bike upright with my knee swollen against the inside of my pants and the back of my neck wet with sweat.

I wanted to look down. I couldn't afford it yet.

Len stayed close all the way into Felton. In the slower traffic I had to put my left foot down, and the leg trembled so badly I thought it would fold. A van driver waved me out ahead of him. I took the space and kept going. When I had to change gear, I lifted my whole thigh rather than bend the knee, and the pain made me taste the coffee I hadn't had at Saratoga Gap.

Santa Cruz put cars between us. I saw Len's headlight at a light, two cars back, and lost it again when a bus pulled out. I couldn't make the cars vanish. I watched for their indicators, for a head moving toward a mirror, for the little room that would open if I waited instead of putting myself where somebody was about to turn. By the time I reached Highway 1, I had begun hoping that the traffic had held him.

Then I saw black in the mirror.

On the open coast the wind came at us from the side. The ocean was there beyond the fields, too bright to look at. My amber glasses had slipped down my nose and the helmet pressed them in place where they had stopped. I flattened myself over the turquoise tank. The brown saddle had a split under my right thigh that I could have found blindfolded; my leg had been rubbing it for three days. I put my weight there and made myself small.

The Norton came closer. I could hear it now, in the gaps between the wind hitting my helmet. Len stayed behind me for a stretch, getting the shelter of my machine, and when the road straightened he moved out to my left.

I asked the Yamaha for the rest. It had been giving it to me for some time.

Len's front wheel drew level with mine. His eyes were forward. He was finished trying to make me hesitate; he wanted the line first. I could see the low buildings ahead, the abandoned works beyond them, and the break in the fence on our right where the finish people had hung red cloth. Past the entrance, the flag was in the dirt lot. We had to turn in to reach it.

He gained half a wheel. He was farther from the entrance than I was. I held the right side of the road and waited for the place where I had to slow, with my left leg throbbing so hard I could feel it against the tank. Len was beside me now. He could have the straight if he could keep it.

I braked. The Yamaha's weight came forward and I turned right into the lot, feeling the new rear tire shift on the loose surface. I put my good foot out without touching it down. The flag was still ahead. Len had to take a wider turn from my left; I heard his engine come up behind me as I straightened.

A man stepped backward with the flag. I went past him first. Then I had to stop.

My left knee folded when I put the foot down. I caught the Yamaha leaning into me and shouted for somebody to take the bar. A woman came off the counting table's bench and held it while I got my weight onto the other leg. Len passed the flag behind us and coasted to a stop with both feet down. For a while all I could hear was the engines and my own breathing.

The woman helped me lower the Yamaha onto its stand. She looked from my leg to my face.

“Do you want a medic?”

“Get one. And keep the man with the case where I can see him.”

He was coming over already. The case had changed hands since the fish yard; the new man was younger and kept saying my name as if I might dispute it. He put it on the table and made room for me to sit. The ocean was loud beyond the road. A gust took a sheet of paper off the table, and he put his boot on it before the next gust could.

Len shut the Norton off. He sat on it with the keys in his hand. The man with the case looked at him, then at his phone, which had been lying beside the money.

“We've got the bike bet too,” he said.

Len swung a leg off. He had to pause with his hand on the saddle before he could stand straight. There was a red mark on his jaw where the helmet strap had been. He looked at the waiting phone, and at the woman holding my jacket, and finally at me. I could have asked for the keys. I let the man by the case wait for them.

“I pay what I owe,” Len said.

He put the keys on the table. He wanted everyone to see his hand come away empty.

The man opened the case. The money was in the same tight bands it had worn in Atlantic City. I asked him to break one of them and give me twenty-four hundred dollars. He counted out twenty-four notes.

I set the money beside the keys.

“For the engine work,” I told Len. “You can write that one paid.”

He picked up the notes and squared the edges against his palm. I had thought he might refuse them. I was glad he didn't; I would have had to sit there looking at them.

“So we're settled,” he said. “I thought we could get this worked out.”

“You paid the bet,” I said. “I'll tell people that when they ask.”

He waited for more. We were still waiting for the medic when the woman came back with a first-aid bag. She wanted me to undo my boot, and I put my hands on the table so she wouldn't see how much I wanted to stop her. Len took his helmet from the Norton. By the time she had the boot loose, he had gone to sit on a piece of concrete beyond the fence.

After she had wrapped the knee, she and the man with the money helped me get the Norton beside the Yamaha. There was a scratch through the gold on the right of the tank. It needed mending, and the crooked seat bracket still needed doing properly. I borrowed the paint marker from the flag man and put my race number on the black tank, above the scratch, while the woman steadied it for me. Then I sat back on my own brown saddle, both sets of keys in my hand. They were still counting out the money. Each time the wind took a corner of a note off the table, I watched until somebody put it back.
