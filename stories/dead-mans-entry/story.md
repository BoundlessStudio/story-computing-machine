Before we left Atlantic City, I bet Dennis Fisk two hundred dollars that he would need my help before we got to California. The previous summer he'd beaten me by eleven minutes while riding with four broken ribs and a tie-down strap holding his Suzuki's fairing on. I was prepared to be helpful up to the value of two hundred dollars.

I had spent the winter thinking about those eleven minutes, sometimes when other people were talking to me.

“You planning to break my bike first?” he said.

“I want you to write down that you said that. When I stop to help you, I want you to have it ready to read aloud.”

We were behind a fish warehouse at Gardner's Basin, with cold air coming through the doors that smelled of fish and bleach. A truck driver kept leaning on her horn, and twenty-three people who knew exactly how to get out of her way pretended they couldn't hear her. The first sun was on her refrigerated trailer, leaving us in its shadow. I remember the gulls stealing the starter's breakfast, because I laughed, and I remember that I was happy. I had the Yamaha beneath me, three thousand miles ahead, and a million dollars waiting to be taken off somebody's hands. I had won it twice. Nobody had yet asked me for any of it.

I was wearing shorts, with my riding pants rolled on the saddle behind me. A man had told me I ought to get dressed. He had his own name printed down both legs, which is a thing you can do if you have the money. I pushed my amber glasses down and watched him trying to work out whether I was laughing at him.

Then the Norton Commando came through.

I could tell you I recognized the sound. Afterward everybody wanted that part to be the sound. But the fish truck was idling and a dozen bikes were running, and my father had been dead for twenty years. I saw the muffler first, flattened underneath by a curb in Camden. Then the saddle moved on its bracket. I had held the lamp while he welded that bracket crooked.

I got off the Yamaha and knelt beside the Norton. I put my hand under the stranger's saddle before I thought to ask his permission.

The ridge was there, thick at one end, thin where Dad had run out of patience. He had said it would hold until Saturday. It was a Thursday when he said it, and the following Thursday he was dead. I had touched that weld more often in my sleep than I ever had with my fingers.

“Easy,” the rider said. “You'll get grease on yourself.”

He took off his helmet, showing white hair above the ears. He started to smile when he saw me looking at his hand. The broken little finger on his right hand still stood out when he laid it on the tank.

Len Barlow had ridden with my father and worked with him. The summer Dad died, an electrical fire filled his room above the workshop with smoke while he slept. They let me out of county for the funeral in cuffs. When I leaned over the coffin, one of the deputies lifted the chain so it wouldn't catch on the wood. Len visited the jail afterward, before his own arrest for the armored-truck job. He had a list from the yard and read it to me through the visiting-room phone. There had been a saucepan I could have if I wanted it. My first question had been about the Norton. “The frame melted, Dell,” he'd said. “There wasn't anything to save.”

I stood up too fast and had to put a hand on his shoulder.

“You told me this burned.”

“Keep your voice down, Dell.”

“You brought it here.”

“I knew you'd be here.” His hand closed over the keys. “I've heard all about you. The collarbone, then you went back and won again. Only woman who's ever managed two. Your father would have been proud.”

The starter wanted to know what the trouble was. He had the prize case open on his table, the money in tight bands, and the truck driver had climbed down from her cab.

“Family friend,” I told him. “Arrived on some family property.”

Len's shoulder tightened beneath my hand. I wanted to hurt him badly enough to miss the start. I could see the particular place where his collarbone came to the skin. Then the woman from the truck said she was calling the police, and three men who had promised to race across America at any cost began putting their gloves on.

I took my hand away.

Len said, “I'm going south out of here. I've got a man who'll take this straight onto the lift, and he's got the parts waiting for me. Fewer cops, too. Come with me. We could eat while he works.”

It would put me behind him, on his roads, at his breakfast table. I had been a passenger on that Norton often enough. I looked at my own Yamaha, a '77 XS650 with the turquoise paint rubbed pale beneath my knees and the brown saddle patched with a piece of a handbag. It had carried me across the country with a broken collarbone on my first win. On the second I had finished with a fever so high the man paying me asked which of us was going to sign the receipt.

“Give me the keys,” I said.

“You want the bike, you'll have to hear me out first.”

The starter struck the table with the flat of his hand. The truck driver was speaking into her phone now, looking directly at him.

The two proof stops that year were Tucumcari and Saratoga Gap. You had to show up at both before the people waiting on the coast would pay you. Everything between was your business. Len could have his quiet southern roads. The quicker line went through places where I knew the jail food already.

“I'll see you in Tucumcari,” I said. “Try not to sell any more of my belongings before I get there.”

I pulled my pants on over the shorts, retied the knot of hair that was coming loose, and tucked the end of my red scarf inside my jacket. The cloth smelled of coffee and the leather polish I'd spilled on it the night before. My hands shook as I buckled the helmet. I was angry about that too.

The starter dropped his arm. We left in a burst of exhaust that sent the gulls over the roof, and at the first traffic signal two riders braked for the fish truck. The woman driving it didn't look at us. I got past while she was still making the turn, the Yamaha pulling cleanly, and Len's headlight stayed in my mirror until the roads divided.

Pennsylvania put rain inside my gloves. They dried hard in Ohio, and each time I stopped I had to bend the fingers before I could pull them on. A patrol car waiting outside a service plaza cost me six minutes and a breakfast I hadn't wanted. I had enough theft convictions to fill both sides of the form, and a bench warrant I preferred to discuss after I had money for a better lawyer.

At nine that night, outside Effingham, I found Dennis on one knee beside the air hose at an I-70 truck stop. He looked up when I stopped at the pump. The hose had a gauge with a cracked face, and he had been arguing with it long enough for his helmet to leave a ring of sweat on the concrete.

“You got a gauge?” he asked.

“You got two hundred dollars?”

“I didn't bet you two hundred dollars you'd own a tool. I can use the other pump when those guys are done.” He nodded at two trucks waiting beyond the canopy.

I put the nozzle back and started pulling my gloves on.

“I'll give you two hundred to take it out and shut up,” he said, getting his wallet.

“And the thing you wrote down,” I said.

He unfolded the receipt he'd stuffed behind the bills. “You planning to break my bike first?”

I got the gauge out. He had paid. I stood there holding my gloves instead of putting them on.

“Whose Norton was that at the start?” he asked.

“My father's.”

He checked the pressure, looked at the gauge, then did it again. “You buying it back?”

“I'm getting it back. I held the lamp while Dad welded that seat bracket. It still has the crooked weld, and the guy riding it came to see me in jail after Dad died. He told me there wasn't anything left of it.”

“Get your money first. You put another two thousand miles on a bike like that, you'll need it.”

I opened my mouth to explain that I wasn't buying a restoration project, but he had already given me back the gauge. By the time I had put it away and got my gloves on, he was pulling out. The two hundred-dollar bills were still folded in my pocket; I hadn't had time to smooth them.

We lost a rider outside Springfield. His bike was being put on a flatbed when I passed, and he stood against a police car with his hands on the roof. I had to make myself look back at the road. That night I slept three hours with my red scarf over my eyes and the helmet against my ribs, then woke convinced I had missed the whole second day.

Across Oklahoma I kept trying out explanations for the Norton. Len had found it in a barn. He had bought a burned engine and rebuilt the rest. Someone had told him where it was while he was in prison. I gave him different amounts of credit depending on how tired I was. In every version he eventually put the keys in my hand. By the time I reached Tucumcari, I had got quite good at making that part happen.

* * *

At the Sundown Motor Court in Tucumcari, the evening sun was still on the courtyard when a woman with a basket of towels made me move the Yamaha three times before she was satisfied. There were yellow lines for the bikes and yellow lines for the laundry cart. I had stopped with a wheel in the wrong kind.

The mechanic at the service bay wanted my rear tire off. I wanted it back on before he had taken it off, which he said was usual. I got out of my jacket and pants and sat on a chair outside room six, in the shorts I'd started in, with my braid unraveling down one shoulder. The chair's metal arms were hot enough to hurt. Inside, a fan pushed the smell of somebody else's soap through the doorway.

Len arrived while I was pouring ice down the back of my neck. I pulled the striped top away from my skin to let a piece fall through, and it landed between my boots. He brought the Norton across the courtyard and set it on its stand where I could see it. He had put nearly two thousand miles on it since I had last touched the bracket. There were new insects on the headlight.

He saw me looking at them.

“My brother's boy went through it before I brought it out. Tires, seals, all that. It's been looked after.”

“You rebuilt it. I'd like you to tell me how much work that took.”

He pulled off his gloves one finger at a time. “There wasn't much putting back together. Let me get a drink. You ought to have something with a bit of salt in it. I've been eating those crackers they give you with soup.”

I stood in the doorway when he came back with two cold colas. He offered me one and waited until I took it. I'd been ready to refuse a can of something I wanted.

He leaned against the other side of the door and began.

“The fire was out by the time they let me into the yard. Your dad had already been taken away. I got a call because there were things they needed moving and nobody wanted to make a decision about them. You were still inside, and there wasn't any point asking you to look at a list of burned tools. I borrowed a trailer. Most of the building was down. The paint shed, that little corrugated place at the back, hadn't caught. Your dad had left the Norton there under a sheet. There wasn't a mark on it.”

He stopped to drink. On the far side of the court, the woman with the towels was trying to get a room door open with her elbow. A visitor held it for her and asked whether this was some kind of rally. She told him the men in the service bay would be gone by midnight.

Len said, “Arty owed me twenty-four hundred dollars for the engine work. I paid the machine shop. The money was going to come back after the summer, and then the summer didn't happen. So I put the bike on the trailer and took it to my brother's place, where there was dry storage, and my own place was up for sale by then anyway. When the truck case came up, I told my brother to keep it until I came home. His boy used to ride it to work. It's been out on the road, Dell. It hasn't been rotting in a box.”

The can was cold enough to make my fingers ache. I rolled it down my forearm, and the water left a stripe of brown skin through the gray road dust.

“You took it before you came to the jail.”

“That was the order things happened in.”

“And you sat across from me and said the frame was melted.”

“I shouldn't have used that word. But your father owed me the money. That isn't something I've come up with now. He'd have told you himself.”

I remembered the engine cases laid out on the bench and Len bringing a bill in an envelope. Dad had put the envelope underneath a mug. When he lifted the mug later, the envelope had come up with it. He'd said he'd better pay it before he ate it by mistake.

“He owed you,” I said. “I'll give you twenty-four hundred dollars. You give me the bike.”

“It doesn't work out at that now.”

“Then give me a bill for the storage. Put your nephew's mileage on it as a credit.”

He took a longer drink. He looked down the court toward the service bay, where my Yamaha stood with its rear wheel out. I could see the empty curve between the two shocks. For the first time since leaving New Jersey, I disliked being so far from it.

“You weren't there,” Len said. “Somebody had to go down and do those jobs. I took care of things. And when you got out, you had something to start with. You got the money from the insurance, didn't you?”

“The building was rented. There was no insurance money for me.”

“Well, I wasn't told about that.” He turned the can in his hand. “I wasn't told a lot of things. I had my own trouble coming by then.”

The version I'd liked best in Oklahoma had Len rebuilding it. I had pictured him putting burned parts on a workbench, finding what could be saved, doing the work for me. I'd been ready to thank him for that. Now I was standing close enough to smell the warm cola on his breath and trying to imagine him carrying a saucepan out of that yard for me while the Norton waited on his trailer.

“I wrote to the scrap man,” I said. “The one you gave me the address for. I asked if he'd kept the tank. I told him I'd pay him for it, and he sent a photograph of what he'd hauled out of the building. He must have thought I was crazy. I kept looking through that photograph for a piece of gold paint. I was trying to buy a bit of a motorcycle you'd already put in somebody's shed.”

Len rubbed the broken little finger against his palm.

“I thought you were past all that,” he said. “When I saw you'd won the race. You seemed to have got on with things.”

“I got on a different motorcycle.”

There was a pale wet mark on the front of his shirt where the can had rested. I watched it drying. I didn't want to cry in front of him, and I was having to think about ordinary stupid things to prevent it: his shirt, the grit inside my boots, whether the mechanic had charged me for a tire with the right rating. Dad had cut the sleeves out of his best jacket to make me my first riding gloves. The fingers were different lengths. I had left them in a cellophane property bag when I got arrested and never asked for them back. I had been sure he could make me another pair.

I drank some of the cola. It was too sweet, and I wanted the rest.

“What arrangement are you offering, Len?”

His shoulders came away from the doorframe.

“Pull out here. Ride the Yamaha home, let an old man have a run at it. When I've finished, my brother's boy will bring the Norton to you. I'll pay for the transport. You've got your two wins, Dell. Nobody can take those off you. You could have this in your own garage next week.”

“You want me to watch you win a million dollars on my father's motorcycle.”

“I want us to come away with something each. I'm trying to be practical. A million dollars is a lot of money to you. It's a lot more to me at the moment.”

I could picture the black tank between my knees on the road near home, at a speed nobody would pay to watch. I could take the crooked bracket off at last and keep it in a drawer. I would wait a week while he got his applause from people who remembered a quicker, thinner Len. I was already deciding where to put the Yamaha so there would be room.

From the bay, the mechanic called that somebody had parked in front of his compressor. The man whose pants said TERRY went to move his bike. He looked at the Norton as he passed.

“That's a nice one,” he said.

“It's mine,” I told him.

Len looked at me.

I set my can in the doorway and walked to the Norton. The tank was warm through the palm of my hand. Two people had passed me while Len was telling his story. My tire was still not on, and I had been allowing him to take all the time he wanted.

He followed me into the court. “We can go inside if you'd rather.”

“I'd rather race you for it.”

Terry stopped beside his bike. A rider waiting for fuel at the bay turned around too.

Len said, “I didn't come here to take your Yamaha.”

“Then you'll be doing a very familiar thing by accident. First one of us to finish the whole race gets both bikes, even if somebody else takes the million. If neither of us gets there, we each keep what we brought.”

Terry said, “You're putting the Yamaha in?”

He'd watched me change its plugs in a motel parking lot the previous summer, after I'd promised him I could do it before his coffee cooled. He had brought me a second coffee to make sure I finished.

“It comes with a new back tire, if they ever put it on. Look after it if you get it, Len. It has a very low opinion of most people.”

The other rider had taken his phone out. Len saw the phone, then looked toward the motor court's sign, as if somebody might be waiting beside it to tell him whether this was a good idea.

“You want to make it a public thing,” he said.

“You brought it to the start.”

He shifted his weight. I thought I could see what appealed to him: my Yamaha to win, and my father's bike between me and the finish. He would get to make me chase it for another thousand miles. The rider by the bay was already talking into his phone, telling somebody on the coast to listen to this.

“First one to finish,” Len said. “Both machines. I'm not answering for it if you come off trying to save a few seconds.”

“Just answer for the keys.”

He held out his hand. His grip was dry and strong. Before he let go he looked toward the phone and gave its owner a nod.

I went to reclaim the Yamaha. The mechanic had put the new tire on and was putting air in it. I pulled my riding pants over my boots, watching the gauge.

“You'll have to let me do this part,” he said.

“I've paid for a particular amount of air.”

He made me laugh. I was looking at the Norton while Len tied his bag on, and laughing, and I had to sit down on my own brown saddle before I could get my gloves on.

We went west into the evening. I put three riders behind me before the light was gone, lost two of them while I stopped for fuel, and had to do it again in the dark. The extra time at Sundown kept returning to me in the shape of somebody else's taillight. In Arizona a truck threw a piece of tread across my path, and I swerved so hard the bag on my saddle shifted. I stopped to retie it with my stomach still trying to move sideways. A rider had died hitting a piece of truck tire the year I first won. They brought his helmet to the finish.

Near Barstow I caught Len buying an egg sandwich. He offered me half, holding it out until I took it. I was still chewing when he left. Dennis was somewhere ahead of both of us. I slept another three hours outside Bakersfield and woke with the scarf folded in my hand. My arms ached while I was lying still; once the Yamaha was under me again, I could forget them for a few miles at a time.

We climbed toward the last proof stop in a little procession, the Suzuki ahead, Len next, me behind him, all three machines black with insects and road dirt. When I saw the Norton at a bend above me I no longer had to guess how it had got there. I wanted the man on it to look in his mirror and find me closer.

* * *

At Saratoga Gap on the fourth morning, the woman taking our numbers stood beside an open van, wearing a fleece and holding a cup of coffee in both hands. I wanted to put my hands around it without asking her. There was damp in the air, damp on the road, and a sharp smell of crushed bay leaves that came through even the old sweat in my helmet.

Dennis went first on the Suzuki, Len rolled out behind him, and I followed close enough to see the Norton pitch as he downshifted into the first bend. The woman at the van had told us we were the first three through. There was nobody ahead of us now except ordinary people going somewhere.

My braid had fallen down inside my jacket. Its end had been rubbing one place between my shoulder blades since Barstow. The red scarf had bunched under the helmet strap and I could feel every fold when I swallowed. I left both alone. The Yamaha's buzz came through my knees as Highway 9 carried us down among the redwoods. Wet needles lay pressed flat in the shadows at the edge of the asphalt.

For the first few miles the three of us kept our places. I could make up ground under braking, then the bend would open and Len would have it back. He was good. I had spent a day and a half being angry at him and had started to think that ought to make him slow. He took the wet patches cleanly and never once looked over his shoulder. When he wanted to know where I was, he used the mirror.

Lower down, we caught a truck carrying stacked timber. The Suzuki closed up behind it. Len came up behind the Suzuki and I braked hard enough to feel the weight of my bag move against my back. The cut ends of the timber filled the road ahead. Diesel hung between the trees. We went around two bends with nowhere to get by, each of us making room and taking it away again.

The road opened to a longer view. Dennis moved his head out, looked, and drew back. Len went past him. The Norton came alongside the truck, small and black beside its wheels, and I could see clear road beyond both of them. I followed.

The Suzuki's front wheel was beside my boot for a second. He had been deciding whether to take the opening when Len took it away from him. Now I was taking the rest. I kept looking beyond the truck's cab, where I wanted to be, while the air at its side pushed the Yamaha toward the center line. My wrist wanted to turn farther than the engine could answer. Then the cab fell behind and there was space to move back in. Len was already setting himself for the next curve.

The truck was between us and the Suzuki. I could stop thinking about eleven minutes.

North of Felton the sun came through in pieces. A patch of road would shine ahead of us, then go dark under the trees before we reached it. Len had the middle of our lane. I moved toward the right as the road began to bend that way, and he turned in before I could draw alongside. By the time I reached his back wheel, he had left me a strip of broken edge and dirt. I slowed and went back behind him. At the exit his back wheel went wide toward the center line. I was too far behind to put mine in the space he left, so on the next right bend I showed him my wheel again and stayed nearer. His helmet moved a fraction, the mirror catching me. He closed the inside before I got there. This time I saw him ease the throttle to keep from running over the line when the bend tightened; I could hear the change in his engine through the noise of my own.

Dad used to sit farther forward on that saddle. On long rides I had rested my chin between his shoulder blades until he complained I was wearing a hole in him. Len's bag was tied over the place where I had sat. It bounced once, and the loose end of a strap flicked at the bracket.

I looked past him. Another right, the far end visible between the trunks, with the road tightening before it straightened. There was no car coming the other way. I could see the strip I had to stay inside.

I brought the Yamaha toward his right again. His head moved. He began his turn early to shut me out, and I let him go without following him into it. I stayed farther left, on our side of the line, while the Norton leaned away from me. For a few yards his back wheel was getting farther ahead. I had to wait through that. My hands wanted to hurry the turn, and I held them where they were until I could put the Yamaha onto the road opening beneath his machine.

Then I turned.

He was already coming out wide. I went through on his right, my front wheel past his back wheel, the engines rising together. I could see the gold letters beside my knee. Len looked down at my wheel, then brought the Norton back toward me.

Now I could hear the Norton over everything, its exhaust cracking beside my left boot.

The hit caught my left leg above the kneecap. My teeth met. I felt the whole weight of the Yamaha in my arms, as if somebody had handed it to me to carry, and for a second I couldn't tell how much of my leg was still on the peg. His black tank filled the edge of my sight. I had an absurdly clear view of a fresh scratch through the gold, bright metal showing underneath. I had spent a month of my eighteenth year trying to buy a piece of that paint.

There was road in front of me. I kept my eyes on it. The Yamaha shook once through the bars, my left boot found the peg again, and I stayed on the throttle until the Norton was behind my elbow. The bend eased. I got the bike upright with my knee swollen against the inside of my pants and the back of my neck wet with sweat.

I wanted to look down. I couldn't afford it yet.

Len stayed close all the way into Felton. In the slower traffic I had to put my left foot down, and the leg trembled so badly I thought it would fold. A van driver waved me out ahead of him. I took the space and kept going. When I had to change gear, I lifted my whole thigh rather than bend the knee, and the pain made me taste the coffee I hadn't had at Saratoga Gap.

At a red light in Santa Cruz, Len was two cars back. I could see his helmet above a car roof. I watched for the light to change, for turn signals, for a space to get away. When the light went green I got moving in first and tried to hook the next gear. My leg stopped halfway through the lift. The engine went free and loud, and the Yamaha stopped pulling.

Len came up on my left while I was trying again. I had to haul the leg from the hip to finish the shift. The gear caught hard. A car beside us kept him from drawing level, but at the next light he was behind my elbow. I watched his front wheel instead of his face.

We reached Highway 1 with him right behind me, the ocean showing between the fields to our left. The wind smelled of salt and was flattening a dark track through the artichoke leaves toward the road. My amber glasses had slid down my nose and the helmet held them there. I flattened myself over the turquoise tank. The brown saddle had a split under my right thigh that I could have found blindfolded; my leg had been rubbing it for three days. I put my weight there and made myself small.

The Norton came closer. I could hear it now, in the gaps between the wind hitting my helmet. Len stayed behind me for a stretch, getting the shelter of my machine, and when the road straightened he moved out to my left.

I asked the Yamaha for the rest. It had been giving it to me for some time.

Len's front wheel drew level with mine. His eyes were forward. I could see the low buildings of Davenport ahead, the idle cement plant beyond them, and the break in the fence on our right where the finish people had hung red cloth. Past the entrance, the flag was in the dirt lot. We had to turn in to reach it.

He gained half a wheel. He was farther from the entrance than I was. I held the right side of the road and tried to brace my left knee against the tank. The leg jerked away. Len was beside me now, and the break in the fence was coming toward us. I pulled my left thigh inward from the hip until the knee pressed against the tank again.

I braked, and the Yamaha's weight drove my knee against the tank, but I held it there as I turned into the lot, blinking until the flag came clear beyond the red cloth, staying bent around the tank while the new rear tire shifted on loose dirt and the bike came straight. Len had taken the wider turn from my left. I heard his engine rise behind me.

A man stepped backward with the flag. I went past him first. Then I stopped.

My left knee folded when I put the foot down. I caught the Yamaha leaning into me and shouted for somebody to take the bar. A woman came off the counting table's bench and held it while I got my weight onto the other leg. Len passed the flag behind us and coasted to a stop with both feet down. I was still trying to find the helmet buckle when he came over.

“Put your weight here, Dell,” he said, taking my elbow. “Don't stand on that one.”

“Let go of me,” I said.

I pulled my arm away. The woman had to catch the back of my jacket. We nearly tipped the Yamaha before she got it onto its stand.

I got the helmet off and dropped it onto the saddle. The scarf had tightened around my throat; I pulled it loose and left it hanging from one hand.

Len held his hand out, palm up. The broken little finger stood away from the rest.

“I'm trying to get you somewhere you can sit down.”

“You hit me and I had to keep riding because I still wanted to beat you. I couldn't find the peg. I thought I was going under your bike, and then I could feel something moving inside my knee. I didn't know what I was doing to it. Don't stand there acting like you're the one who got me here.”

He looked at the woman. She had my arm over her shoulder now. His hand came down.

“I saw you. I thought you'd lift when I came over. There was room behind me, and I wasn't just going to give you the race. I've come a long way for this money too. You don't have to make it sound like I was trying to kill you.”

I had wanted to hurt him in the fish yard. I had pictured it quite clearly. I was trying to remember that while he stood in front of me, but my leg had begun to shake against the woman's leg and I couldn't make it stop.

The woman asked me if I could turn toward the bench. I tried to move with her and took a breath before I could do it. Len stepped forward, then stopped when I put my hand up.

“You got here first,” he said. “I'm not disputing it. You want both bikes, you've won them. That's what we agreed.”

“Then get the keys. Put them where I can reach them.”

He turned back toward the Norton. The woman got me onto the bench and asked whether I wanted a medic.

“Get one,” I said. “And keep the man with the case where I can see him.”

While she called for the medic, the man with the prize case came over, younger than the starter in Atlantic City and saying my name as if I might dispute it. He set the case on the table. The ocean was loud beyond the road. A gust took a sheet of paper off the table, and he put his boot on it before the next gust could.

Len returned with the keys. The man at the table put his phone beside the case and watched him.

“I pay my bets,” Len said.

He set the keys on the table, leaving his hand beside them. I picked them up. He was watching my face, and I wanted the woman back with her bag and something to hold my leg still. I wanted him far enough away that I could move without his hand coming out.

“Open the case,” I told the man. “I want the first twenty-four hundred loose.”

The money was in the same tight bands it had worn in Atlantic City. He broke one and counted out twenty-four bills. I pushed them toward Len.

“That's the engine work. That bike was never yours.”

“I'm not asking you for that now.”

“You said it was owed. I don't want to hear it again.”

He picked up the bills and squared the edges against his palm. I watched until he put them away. The woman came back from her car with a first-aid bag and knelt in front of me; she needed my boot off, and we got it loose between us while Len stood at the end of the bench.

“I kept the Norton running,” he said. “If I hadn't, there wouldn't be anything to give you.”

I gripped the table while the woman moved my leg. I looked up at him when I could.

“Take your money, Len. Let her do this.”

He took his helmet and went to sit on a piece of concrete beyond the fence. The man beside me started counting the rest of my million. Dennis rode past the flag while the woman was working on my boot, and stayed straddling the Suzuki after the engine was off.

After the woman had wrapped the knee, they helped me bring the Norton alongside the Yamaha. I hooked my hand under its saddle to steady it, and the thick end of the weld pressed into my fingers, warm from the ride. Dad had pushed my hair out of the light with the back of his wrist, leaving grease on my forehead. I remembered rubbing it off with the hand that was supposed to keep the lamp still, and him catching the lamp before I dropped it. The woman had the bike balanced now. I kept my hand under the saddle until she asked me to let go. Then I borrowed the flag man's marker and put my number on the black tank, above the fresh scratch. I sat back on my own brown saddle, both sets of keys in my hand. They were still counting out the money. Each time the wind took a corner of a note off the table, I watched until somebody put it back.
