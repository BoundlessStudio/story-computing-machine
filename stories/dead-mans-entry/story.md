## Opening 1

The man with the million dollars under his card table wouldn't open the case again, so Della put her boot on it.

“We've counted it,” he said.

“You counted it. I was buying coffee.” She lowered her amber glasses and gave him the patient look that had cost several men money. “We're standing behind a fish wholesaler at five in the morning. You haven't got a permit for a raffle. I think asking to see the prize is a reasonable precaution.”

Around them, motorcycles were being coaxed awake. Someone had brought a machine that sounded as if it were eating its own cutlery; someone else was already helping him with it. Twenty-three starters this summer. Della knew most of their engines better than she knew their faces, and she knew a good deal about the faces. The woman in the refrigerated truck wanted them out of her loading lane.

The starter shoved Della's boot aside, hauled the case onto the table, and opened it. She leaned forward on her turquoise tank, enjoying the creak of the brown saddle beneath her. There was pleasure in having all that road still to do. Her hands hadn't swollen yet. Her hair, braided and piled on her head, hadn't spent three days trying to saw through the collar of her jacket. California was full of people who had no idea she was coming to take their money.

A black motorcycle rolled between her Yamaha and the table.

For a moment she resented the interruption. Then the rider put a boot down, shifting his weight, and she saw the back of the saddle lift a little on its bracket.

She got off the Yamaha. Coffee tipped over in her hand and ran hot across her knuckles; she set the cup on the money without looking at the starter. The black tank carried gold lettering. The left silencer had a flat place underneath where a high curb had caught it.

She crouched beside the stranger's knee and reached beneath his saddle. Her thumb found the crooked ridge of a weld she had held a lamp for twenty years ago. Her father's bike was supposed to have burned with him.

## Opening 2

Before we left Atlantic City, I bet a man two hundred dollars that he would need my help before we got to California. He had beaten me the previous summer, so I was prepared to be helpful up to the value of two hundred dollars.

That year he'd finished ahead of me with four broken ribs and a tie-down strap holding his fairing on. I had spent the winter thinking about those eleven minutes, sometimes when other people were talking to me.

“You're going to break my bike,” he said.

“I want you to write down that you said that. When I stop to help you, I want you to have it ready to read aloud.”

We were behind a fish warehouse. A truck driver kept leaning on her horn, and twenty-three people who knew exactly how to get out of her way pretended they couldn't hear her. The sun was coming up over the roofs. I remember the gulls stealing the starter's breakfast, because I laughed, and I remember that I was happy. I had the Yamaha beneath me, three thousand miles ahead, and a million dollars waiting to be taken off somebody's hands. I had won it twice. Nobody had yet asked me for any of it.

I was wearing shorts, with my riding trousers rolled on the saddle behind me. A man had told me I ought to get dressed. He had his own name printed down both legs, which is a thing you can do if you have the money. I pushed my glasses down and watched him trying to work out whether I was laughing at him.

Then the Norton came through.

I could tell you I recognised the sound. Afterward everybody wanted that part to be the sound. But the fish truck was idling and a dozen bikes were running, and my father had been dead for twenty years. I saw the silencer first, flattened underneath by a curb in Camden. Then the saddle moved on its bracket. I had held the lamp while he welded that bracket crooked.

I put my hand under the stranger's saddle before I thought to ask his permission.
