# Provisional outline

## Promised experience

The explicit promise is a dangerous illegal race across America, a million-dollar purse, and a formidable woman already distinguished among its best riders: a vintage motorcycle, a long criminal record, two wins, and a reputation for surviving what should kill her. The dead father's supposedly destroyed motorcycle introduces a mystery that should change this year's race. I infer appetite for speed, outlaw company, nerve, and a consequential reckoning with the past; neither the stranger's identity, an explanation, an ending, nor a particular family relationship beyond the dead father is specified. The image adds sun-baked colour and an appraising, amused, physically assured woman. Her swagger should survive the hardest scene. The later instruction distinguishes attached-document instructions from the user's request; the image supplies casting, not commands.

## Three takes considered

1. **Straight, full-scale race thriller — chosen.** Follow the woman from the Atlantic start to the Pacific finish. The returned motorcycle is a rival's deliberate bait; the central encounter is a roadside bargain between two competitors who each need something the other can withhold, followed by a sustained final race.
2. **American road-ghost tale.** Death enters on a trophy collected twenty years earlier, and the race becomes a contest for more lives than hers. The overnight encounter has force, but an invented cosmology would take space from the criminal and mechanical pleasures already present.
3. **The winner's police interview.** An arrested winner reconstructs how she recognized and stole the motorcycle during the race, while concealing the stranger's fate. It could have a strong criminal voice, but it would put the riding at secondhand distance.

I choose the first because the mystery can alter whom she races, the bargain she makes, and the way she wins, without replacing the requested race with an investigation.

## Provisional direction — writer's choices

**Della Wray**, thirty-eight, rides a battered turquoise Yamaha XS650 from Atlantic City toward a finish near Davenport, California. She wants a third win because she likes winning; the money is real appetite, not a token for subsequent virtue. Theft convictions, prison time, and the bodily costs of her first two victories belong to her ordinary calculations.

An unfamiliar entrant arrives on her father Arty's black Norton Commando. A crookedly welded seat bracket that Della helped fit at eighteen establishes the machine before she recognizes the rider: **Len Barlow**, Arty's former racing and robbery partner, newly released after a long robbery sentence. Arty died in an accidental garage fire twenty years ago, while Della was in county jail. Len helped clear the wreck afterward, found the Norton intact in its detached lean-to, and stole it. He told Della it had burned, then hid it with his brother before his arrest. He neither caused the fire nor withheld a possible rescue. His wrong is theft from a newly bereaved young woman and twenty years of lying, not an unaddressed murder.

Len wants the million and his old standing back. Eliminating the strongest rival is his practical aim: the Norton lets him offer Della something worth abandoning a third win for. The provocation also makes him the man everyone watches. He remains an accomplished rider in his sixties. At the launch he offers a conversation if she follows his longer southern route. That route gives him a familiar service shop and fewer patrols; he expects to recover its extra mileage by avoiding service delays. Della has no support there and would be following his timetable. She takes the quicker, more policed line toward their scheduled Tucumcari proof stop, choosing to postpone the account.

At the fictional Sundown Motor Court in Tucumcari, the two are serviced and fed among other racers. Len gives his account because he needs to make the offer believable: the Norton in exchange for her withdrawal. Arty genuinely owed him $2,400 for engine work, acknowledged and unpaid; taking the Norton was never agreed. Della can accept the debt without accepting his right to steal the machine and lie to her. She now knows she is bargaining with the thief, not an innocent subsequent owner. She counters publicly: whichever of them finishes the complete race first takes both motorcycles, regardless of whether either wins the purse. If neither finishes, the bet is void. Her own pictured Yamaha is explicitly at risk, not just the hoped-for inheritance. Two fellow racers hear it while waiting for fuel; one sends the terms to the bettors at the finish. Len needs those same people to back him and admit him to future money games. Default would destroy the very standing he is racing to recover. Della knows his distinction between cheating an absent victim and refusing a witnessed bet. No new named enforcer or title procedure is needed. He accepts because he thinks her need to protect the Norton will inhibit her on the road.

The last substantial scene begins after the final proof point at Saratoga Gap and follows Highway 9 downhill. An unnamed third rider leads them; a slow truck draws all three together. Len takes a clear opening to pass the truck and leader. Della follows before it closes, leaving the previous leader behind. In the bends afterward Len repeatedly turns in early to prevent her passing inside. Della probes twice, sees the pattern, then shows him the same inside threat before a tightening right turn lower on Highway 9, near Felton. He commits early; she stays wider, turns later, and accelerates beneath his widening exit. He tries to close the gap with the Norton. She continues rather than protecting her inheritance at the expense of the race; the two machines touch and her knee takes the blow. Both remain upright. She gets clear on the following straight. After Santa Cruz, Len closes again on Highway 1's open coastal stretch; the injured knee and renewed challenge keep the lead contested to the Davenport turn-in. The uncertainty belongs to her execution and the remaining road, not Len conveniently crashing or a mechanical miracle.

She wins the million and both bikes. Len tries to present honoring the bet as a clean settlement of their history; she accepts the machine, not his account of himself. Her own number goes on the black tank beside her battered turquoise Yamaha. This is informed appetite and reclamation, not a lesson in abandoning her father's supposed recklessness. These remain writer's provisional choices; the physical pass will be checked for road and machine plausibility before drafting.

## Craft plan

**Teller and voice.** Close third, past tense, strongly coloured by Della's greedy, affectionate, suspicious attention to riders and machines. Sentences can stretch with a road or an unwanted recollection, then shorten under physical pressure. The eventual voice trial will compare this with Della's first-person retrospective account, more boastful and more obviously selective. Neither opening will be written before the gate.

**People and speech.** Della is generous with boasts, impatient with consolation, and funny when she wants someone to underestimate her. Her talk runs longer when she is selling a lie; anger makes her specific. She believes Len's report that the Norton burned and knows nothing about its two decades in hiding. Len knows its history and falsely believes possession of it can make her choose sentiment over racing. He talks as a familiar senior man claiming reasonable authority, supplies unwanted context, and puts his own choices in the passive voice. Two principals suffice; the racing field, motel worker, and finish crew stay a functional chorus. Arty comes through the awkward seat-bracket repair and Della's memory of him cutting up a favourite riding jacket to make her first pair of gloves. Neither memory proves sainthood or requires disillusionment.

**Conversation owed.** Where has the Norton been, why did Len lie, and who gave him the right to take it? He must tell the chronology fully, including that he arrived after the fatal fire. He wants withdrawal and recognition of an alleged debt; Della wants facts, the machine, and control of the terms. Let him talk at length when he sells his justification. Her interruptions challenge his claim, not cue the next fact. After the wager he wants reconciliation; she has promised a race.

**Image casting.** The supplied adult woman has brown skin, abundant brown hair in an unruly high knot and braid, amber sunglasses lowered for a direct look, a red neckerchief, short summer clothes and brown boots, astride a chipped turquoise road bike with a brown ribbed saddle. She lounges, appraises, challenges, and occupies the saddle as home ground. Della will lower the glasses to inspect the false destruction story; secure the loose hair and scarf before a hard run; return to the turquoise tank and worn saddle through touch; and keep the same insolent physical ease when claiming her winnings. Riding adds a helmet, jacket, and full-length protective trousers without making the person timid. The image does not specify her name, ancestry, or past.

**Places chosen.** Tucumcari beats a Kansas interstate truck plaza and a Moab campground: Route 66's old motor courts give exhausted riders shelter, visibility, and unwanted witnesses. The **Sundown Motor Court**, late July, second evening, rents rooms to tourists while an adjacent service bay hosts the proof stop. Bikes stand in the courtyard outside open room doors. Hot metal ticks; laundry smells of bleach; grit catches between teeth; an ice machine shudders; neon comes on above faded stucco. Della takes the private accusation into a doorway and the wager back among the waiting racers. Atlantic City's first dawn supplies gull noise, hot coffee, salt, and delivery work. Highway 9 on the fourth morning supplies shaded turns, redwood smell, cold fog against hot skin, and uneven light. Its link through Santa Cruz to Highway 1 north is explicit. Davenport's finish occupies a rented dirt lot beside a shuttered coastal works: wind catches the counting table's loose papers, and ocean noise swallows the arriving engines. The rough three-day-plus clock leaves room for fuel, repairs, and several hours' sleep; exact road and machine facts remain subject to checking.

## Scene briefs and ending

1. **Atlantic City start, first morning — about 20%.** Della wants a good launch; Len wants her diverted onto his route. Register the impossible bike before recognizing him. Let other competitors interrupt credentials rather than listing them all here. She refuses the offered pursuit and names their common stop instead. Turn: she preserves her race while committing to recover the bike. Remember her fingers beneath its familiar saddle.
2. **Sundown, Tucumcari, second evening — long scene, about 35%.** Len wants withdrawal and acceptance of his debt story; Della wants a repeatable account and the Norton. He tells the theft, concealment, lie, imprisonment, and return in order. Tourists, laundry, servicing, and approaching competitors interfere. She makes the public head-to-head wager after the private account. Turn: neither can dispose of the other without racing. Remember Della's hand on the tank while Len holds the keys.
3. **Highway 9 to Davenport, fourth morning — about 45%, including the finish.** Three leading racers converge behind the truck; Len passes truck and leader, Della follows him, and their duel develops through several bends. She observes his defensive pattern, sets the cutback, accepts the collision risk and damaged paint, gets clear, and must hold the gap all the way through Santa Cruz and up the coast. No final conversation during fast riding. At the finish, while their engines cool, the purse is counted out and Len pays the witnessed bet. He wants acceptance as a man who pays; she wants the keys. End with her number on the Norton beside her turquoise Yamaha. The repair at the opening, her hand on the tank at Sundown, and the wager load that act; no second account or moral speech follows it.

Summarize intervening weather, fuel, repairs, sleep, and rivals changing her opportunities. One rider's early arrival at a crowded fuel stop costs her time; another's arrest lets her move up without making a casualty obligatory. The unnamed third rider at the truck makes the field a continuing physical claim. Leave out a full criminal biography, an investigation of race finances, and a fresh cast at each stop. Roughly 5,000 words; keep room for the account and consecutive riding.
