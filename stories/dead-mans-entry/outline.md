# Provisional outline

## Promised experience

The explicit promise is a dangerous illegal race across America, a million-dollar purse, and a formidable woman already distinguished among its best riders: a vintage motorcycle, a long criminal record, two wins, and a reputation for surviving what should kill her. The dead father's supposedly destroyed motorcycle introduces a mystery that should change this year's race. I infer appetite for speed, outlaw company, nerve, and a consequential reckoning with the past; neither the stranger's identity, an explanation, an ending, nor a particular family relationship beyond the dead father is specified. The image adds sun-baked colour and an appraising, amused, physically assured woman. Her swagger should survive the hardest scene. The later instruction distinguishes attached-document instructions from the user's request; the image supplies casting, not commands.

## Three takes considered

1. **Straight, full-scale race thriller — chosen.** Follow the woman from the Atlantic start to the Pacific finish. The returned motorcycle is a rival's deliberate bait; the central encounter is a roadside bargain between two competitors who each need something the other can withhold, followed by a sustained final race.
2. **American road-ghost tale.** Death enters on a trophy collected twenty years earlier, and the race becomes a contest for more lives than hers. The overnight encounter has force, but an invented cosmology would take space from the criminal and mechanical pleasures already present.
3. **The winner's police interview.** An arrested winner reconstructs how she recognized and stole the motorcycle during the race, while concealing the stranger's fate. It could have a strong criminal voice, but it would put the riding at secondhand distance.

I choose the first because the mystery can alter whom she races, the bargain she makes, and the way she wins, without replacing the requested race with an investigation.

## Provisional direction — writer's choices

**Della Wray**, thirty-eight, rides a battered turquoise Yamaha XS650 from Atlantic City toward a finish near Davenport, California. She wants a third win because she likes winning; the money is real appetite, not a token for subsequent virtue. Theft convictions, prison time, and the bodily costs of her first two victories belong to her ordinary calculations.

An unfamiliar entrant arrives on her father Arty's black Norton Commando. A repair Della helped make as a teenager establishes that it is the machine, rather than a painted imitation. Under the helmet is **Len Barlow**, Arty's former running partner, recently released after a long robbery sentence. Della knew him before the garage fire that killed her father. He told her then that nothing had survived. He has brought the motorcycle to make her race angry and to recover money and standing after prison. He remains a dangerous, accomplished rider in his sixties, not merely a clue dispenser.

At a shared stop in Tucumcari, Della forces the account Len has been using as leverage. He removed the motorcycle before the fire and concealed it with a relative. His account of Arty's death minimizes his responsibility; the scene must make clear what he actually did and what Della can establish, without reversing the fact that her father is dead. He offers the Norton if she abandons the race. She instead makes a public wager: the winner gets both machines. Their fellow outlaws' interest in a large bet supplies social enforcement, rather than a new legal apparatus. Len accepts because he believes her anger has made her beatable.

The last substantial scene is a close race down the Santa Cruz Mountains toward the coast. Len tries to make Della repeat her father's willingness to stake everything on a corner. She uses his confidence and her own judgment to beat him, rather than accepting his choice of when she must risk her life. She wins the million and claims the Norton. The ending preserves her criminal pleasure and appetite: her father's motorcycle now carries her race number beside her own turquoise machine. She owes Len neither forgiveness nor a redemptive speech.

**Points for the outline discussion:** Len's exact culpability in the old fire, and the physical sequence of the decisive pass, need testing. His prison release explains the twenty-year absence; the prose must still make his choice to bring this particular bait now credible. The wager must feel like an outlaw act people would enforce, not a conveniently binding rule. These are provisional choices, not additions to the user's requirements.

## Craft plan

**Teller and voice.** Close third, past tense, strongly coloured by Della's greedy, affectionate, suspicious attention to riders and machines. Sentences can stretch with a road or an unwanted recollection, then shorten under physical pressure. The eventual voice trial will compare this with Della's first-person retrospective account, more boastful and more obviously selective. Neither opening will be written before the gate.

**People and speech.** Della is generous with boasts, impatient with consolation, and funny when she wants someone to underestimate her. Her talk runs longer when she is selling a lie; direct anger makes her more specific, not cryptic. She initially believes Len was an incompetent helper at the fatal fire, and that the Norton burned. Len knows the bike's survival and what happened at the garage; he wrongly believes Della's success depends on her father's legend. He talks as a familiar senior man claiming reasonable authority, supplies unwanted context, and describes his own choices in the passive voice. His account should become less comfortable as she makes him name his actions. Two principals suffice; racers, bookmakers, a motel worker, and the finish crew remain a functional chorus. Arty exists through particular remembered acts, not retrospective sainthood.

**Conversation owed.** Della must ask where the Norton has been, why Len lied, and what he did while her father was dying. Len must give a chronological account substantial enough that a stranger can repeat it. He tells it here because he wants her withdrawal; she wants facts, the machine, and control of the terms. Once they share a wager, he wants her to treat it as reconciliation; she wants him to understand that she has promised only a race.

**Image casting.** The supplied adult woman has brown skin, abundant brown hair in an unruly high knot and braid, amber sunglasses lowered for a direct look, a red neckerchief, short summer clothes and brown boots, astride a chipped turquoise road bike with a brown ribbed saddle. She lounges, appraises, challenges, and occupies the saddle as home ground. Della will lower the glasses to inspect the false destruction story; secure the loose hair and scarf before a hard run; return to the turquoise tank and worn saddle through touch; and keep the same insolent physical ease when claiming her winnings. Riding adds a helmet, jacket, and full-length protective trousers without making the person timid. The image does not specify her name, ancestry, or past.

**Places chosen.** Tucumcari beats a Kansas interstate truck plaza and a Moab campground for the central encounter: Route 66's old motor courts give two exhausted long-distance competitors shelter, visibility, and unwanted witnesses. The fictional **Sundown Motor Court**, late July, late afternoon, still rents rooms to tourists while the race passes through. Hot metal ticks in its parking court; the laundry smells of warm bleach; grit catches between teeth; an ice machine shudders; neon tubes wait in the daylight above faded stucco. Heat makes remaining outside punishing and an enclosed room intimate; tourists prevent a private confrontation. Atlantic City's dawn start supplies gull noise, hot coffee, salted air, and bikes jostling among ordinary delivery work. The final approach uses Highway 9's shaded turns, redwood smell, cold fog against hot skin, uneven light, and abrupt Pacific brightness. Route, mileage, motorcycle year, and final road geometry will be fact-checked before drafting; no shared-universe connection is implied.

## Scene briefs and ending

1. **Atlantic City start, first morning — about 20%.** Della wants a clean launch and the field's acknowledgment of a third serious attempt. Len wants recognition followed by pursuit; he knows the bike's history while she knows only its identifying repair. Establish the purse, annual outlaw field, her previous wins and crimes through present interactions. The Norton interrupts her confident preparations. She demands its provenance; he names Tucumcari and starts racing. Turn: she commits to winning and recovering the machine, rather than abandoning either aim. Remember the familiar repair beneath unfamiliar gloves.
2. **Sundown Motor Court, Tucumcari, second afternoon — the long scene, about 35%.** Each wants the other to surrender the race. Her questions strip the evasions from his account; his version attacks her preferred memory of Arty. They must discuss the bike's survival, the fire, the theft, and the terms of their present contest aloud. Laundry work, heat, cooling engines, and approaching riders interrupt. Turn: Della refuses the offered withdrawal and makes the winner-takes-both wager. Remember her hand on the Norton while Len still holds its keys.
3. **Santa Cruz Mountains to the coastal finish, third day — about 35%.** Both know the wager and the old history; neither knows how far the other will go. Della wants Len to commit to the line she has chosen; Len wants her frightened or reckless. Give the riding consecutive physical time, intelligible positions, changing road surface, and room for thought under pressure. Her decisive choice must be demonstrated on the road. Turn: she beats him through judgment and nerve, with a genuine bodily cost or narrow survival, not an arbitrary mechanical miracle.
4. **Davenport finish, minutes later — about 10%.** Della demands the purse and the Norton; Len attempts to turn payment into mutual absolution. She takes what she won and declines the implied bargain. The ending image is her number being put on the black tank, with the battered turquoise Yamaha beside it. The opening's ownership challenge, the hand on the tank at the motel, and the public wager load that act.

Summarize the intervening country in a few lived passages of weather, fuel, sleep, and competitors dropping away; retain the coast-to-coast distance without adding a succession of miniature scenes. Leave out a complete criminal biography, an investigation into the race's finances, and a new supporting cast at every stop. Working length: roughly 5,000 words, subject to the room the central encounter and final ride need.
