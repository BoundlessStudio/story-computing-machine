# Provisional direction

First-person, roughly 3,000 words, within the waterfall's single rising minute. The dark-haired speaker, in a coral gown, lost her kingdom at seven, thirty years before this centenary. She has spent almost all her remembered life preparing a warning to the queen before the siege. The palace balcony survived and has been repaired under a successor country. Her mother was a loving, imperfect queen, not a concealed monster.

Her daughter has come with her. The prepared warning could stop the decision that brought the army to the capital, but changing thirty years changes encounters and births. The speaker met the girl's father in exile. He is alive and waiting below, refusing to help undo their shared life. She has promised that she would find him again in any history; she has no way to keep that promise.

At the first upward movement she is already releasing her rehearsed warning. The arriving letter interrupts her hand: "Whatever you do, let it burn." She may refuse it; a warning of a possible history is not a decree. Sending is simply releasing a folded, addressed letter during the minute, to a chosen person and past moment. The original warning stays usable.

The speaker reaches to enlist her daughter's second message: she could tell her father where to find the younger princess after history changes. Instead the girl reveals a private letter asking the queen to escape and find her child. She wants the grandmother from the stories, not only a happy mother. This is a plausible smaller rescue. The speaker starts to forbid it, then stops, recognizing the dutiful role she has been teaching the girl. The message belongs to her. Memories retain the queen's homely pleasures and unfinished care. A living queen would search for her daughter and change the exile in which the father was met. The speaker admits that finding him again was a hope she cannot guarantee. The girl challenges the withdrawn promise and asks for silence to decide. She ultimately keeps the letter, giving up her imagined grandmother; her reasoning and reaction remain her own.

The speaker also chooses against restoration, without proving every alternative impossible. She writes the cruel six words and sends them to her own hand at the minute's beginning: they bought this encounter by interrupting her reflex. The warning's origin is visible and consumes only her message, not the girl's. The falls resume; the queen remains dead. Keep the dead specific and the daughter's response unresolved; no embrace as moral acquittal. Some preparation and memory precede the minute so its brief exchanges and writing physically fit.

Open pressure: does the mother's admission actually let the girl choose, or merely steer her toward the preferred result?
