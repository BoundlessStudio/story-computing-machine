# A Life of Her Own

The request's explicit center is a renowned, accomplished Lady Knight and someone's disbelief that she could want to be a housewife. “Just” invites the pleasure of an answer that overturns the speaker's scale of value. I infer room for warmth, wit, and a sincere domestic desire that does not cancel her prowess; these are interpretations, not specified motives or tone. The speaker, marriage, children, reason for her choice, scope of adventure, and ending remain open. The anticipated payoff is learning what this particular formidable woman wants and seeing that want govern her life.

Provisional direction: close third person with Lady Ameline, an undefeated knight who still enjoys riding and swordplay. After a victorious peace, she is offered the marshalship. She has been married to Piers, a bookbinder, for five years but has spent scarcely a month at home in total. Her wish is to keep their house and finally live their marriage. This relationship and history are my choices. She is neither incapacitated nor secretly undertaking another great mission; the peace is secure, other able officers exist, and her pension is already hers. The conflict is between a genuinely attractive public future and the ordinary shared life she has repeatedly postponed, under pressure from a former commander who sincerely admires her.

- At the palace's farewell reception, Chancellor Berolt offers the marshal's baton and speaks the prompt's disbelief when Ameline says what she intends to do. Her victories are matters of shared, concrete memory: Berolt was present when she took a fortified gate and brought their trapped army out. She takes pleasure in her record and in the ambitious offer. The scene gives her something real to refuse rather than making military life worthless.
- Before giving her final answer, she goes to the small house she and Piers bought while she was away. They have letters and affection but little practice living together. She helps prepare their first unhurried supper; a discovery that he dislikes a food she has lovingly sent him for years becomes a funny, affectionate disagreement. They begin learning the person the letters left out. Housekeeping is a literal desired occupation and contribution, not a disguise for commanding an estate or saving the kingdom. Piers has his own work and preferences; he neither grants her leave nor begs her to stay.
- Berolt brings the appointment to the house before its acceptance deadline. Ameline can still take it. Piers leaves the answer to her. She refuses clearly, returns the baton, and accepts that she will miss comrades and some adventures. She keeps her sword and her accomplishments. Berolt need not be converted to her tastes in order to respect her decision.
- After he leaves, Piers initially speaks as though they must fit everything into another brief leave. Ameline makes an ordinary plan for the following week; he realizes she will actually be there. Their physical ease and delight, rather than a final argument about duty or a perfect meal, supply the ending. The last attention belongs to the marriage she has chosen to live, including the prospect of uneventful days.

The prose may find a better sequence or scale. Protect the knight's self-direction, her real enjoyment of her former life, the specific attraction of this husband and home, and a domestic ending that is an affirmative choice rather than a consolation prize.
