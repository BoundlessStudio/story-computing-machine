# A Life of Her Own

The request's explicit center is a renowned, accomplished Lady Knight and someone's disbelief that she could want to be a housewife. “Just” invites the pleasure of an answer that overturns the speaker's scale of value. I infer room for warmth, wit, and a sincere domestic desire that does not cancel her prowess; these are interpretations, not specified motives or tone. The speaker, marriage, children, reason for her choice, scope of adventure, and ending remain open. The anticipated payoff is learning what this particular formidable woman wants and seeing that want govern her life.

Provisional direction: close third person with Lady Ameline, an undefeated knight who still enjoys riding and swordplay. After a victorious peace, she is offered the marshalship. She has been married to Piers, a bookbinder, for five years but has spent scarcely a month at home in total. Her wish is to keep their house and finally live their marriage. This relationship and history are my choices. She is neither incapacitated nor secretly undertaking another great mission; the peace is secure, other able officers exist, and her pension is already hers. The conflict is between a genuinely attractive public future and the ordinary shared life she has repeatedly postponed, under pressure from a former commander who sincerely admires her.

- At the palace's farewell reception, Chancellor Berolt offers the marshal's baton and speaks the prompt's disbelief when Ameline says what she intends to do. Her victories are matters of shared, concrete memory: Berolt was present when she took a fortified gate and brought their trapped army out. The first peacetime riding circuit makes the offer vivid: she pictures herself riding with old companions beneath the northern peaks, with the authority to choose their way. She wants that too. Her domestic intention is sincere; her formal answer is due that evening.
- She goes to the small house she and Piers bought while she was away. They have letters and affection but little practice living together. Preparing supper, she discovers that he dislikes a food she has lovingly sent him for years. Their funny, affectionate disagreement opens a reciprocal gap: she too has presented her best self in letters and brief visits. She wants the unedited days and discoveries that cannot be compressed into another leave. While Piers is occupied with his work, her pleasure in choosing and preparing their home has its own scene weight. Housekeeping is a literal desired occupation and contribution, not disguised command. He has his own work and preferences; he neither grants her leave nor begs her to stay.
- Berolt brings the appointment before its acceptance deadline. Ameline can still take it. She refuses clearly, returns the baton, and accepts that she will miss comrades and some adventures. The answer now rests on the particular, still-unfamiliar life she has encountered at the house, not a repetition of an abstract preference. She keeps her sword and her accomplishments. Berolt need not share her tastes in order to respect her decision.
- After he leaves, Ameline takes pleasure in making a plan for the following week, an expanse of time that belongs to her. Piers has been speaking as though they must fit everything into another brief leave; now he realizes she will actually be there. Their mutual delight and unhurried physical ease supply the ending, with her satisfaction carrying the final viewpoint. The marriage still contains plenty they do not know about one another. No final argument about duty or perfect meal settles it for them.

The prose may find a better sequence or scale. Protect the knight's self-direction, her real enjoyment of her former life, the specific attraction of this husband and home, and a domestic ending that is an affirmative choice rather than a consolation prize.
