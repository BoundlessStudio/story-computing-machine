# Party of Four Hundred

Somebody had tied a man to one of Zella Stovall’s good chairs. There were six of those at the Last Turn Fairground, all steel, and she’d spent half the afternoon filing the feet flat so a man could rock back a little without going all the way over. It was almost midnight and the heat of the day was still in the tar. The pan of scallion oil had just begun to spit when she put her head out of the window for a look at the knot.

“He’s a prisoner,” said the man who had brought him.

“He can be a prisoner on his own damn time. Give him his hands if you want me to give him supper.”

The two crews had each taken a driver off the other’s road. The woman with the crimson crest watched the man across from her undo the knot. As soon as he did, she unclipped the chain around her prisoner’s wrists. Her own driver came over rubbing his hands and she slid a truck key on a blue ribbon into his palm. “Your rig’s by the ring toss,” she told him. “Get something hot in you first.”

The two drivers drew up their chairs, which was the point of having the exchange here, within fifty feet of the Midnight Noodle Truck. You could have brought them out onto the dead rides and saved yourself the price of dinner. Then you’d have to eat with one hand on your gun, which was a shitty way to get an egg down your throat.

She lifted the basket of noodles and struck it twice against the rim to drain. The man who washed dishes for his supper passed her two bowls, his big thumbs wrinkled white from the suds. At the bottom of each bowl was a grinning skull in the teeth of a gear. Zella had put the emblem on the side of the truck by flashlight, filling in one tooth at a time, and now it was on everybody’s jackets and bags.

One of the hill boys had sewn it onto his jacket and told her he could ride to the end of the north road on her name. She’d pointed to the red lettering beside the menu. *No fighting within fifty feet of the truck.* You could wear that patch all the way out into the hills and people would still shoot at you if they meant to.

She put the eggs in last and slid the bowls across the ledge. Both drivers put their faces down to the steam. In the pause before anybody wanted anything else, she shook four brass ride tokens out of the envelope Raddock had left that afternoon. He paid ahead when he needed a table with the South Gate men, who’d been shooting holes in his salt trucks since spring. She chalked their names in for tomorrow’s supper and clipped the envelope of tokens behind the board beside the window. One of the gate men usually wanted a second helping of whatever had salt in it. That was the one who’d shot up the most trucks.

She switched on the red ramen sign. The light reached the first row of lanterns. Beyond that, the braces of the Ferris wheel went black against the moon.

When Zella turned back to the window, Saffy had hooked one cream high-top behind a packing crate and drawn it up for a stool. The ear of a bedraggled prize bear stuck out of the sack at her feet. Her goggles were pushed into her black fringe. When she wanted credit or had one of Zella’s saucepans in her pockets she wore the exact grin she was wearing now.

“Two bowls,” Saffy said. “You can charge me for both before I tell you what I’ve brought.”

Whatever it was she’d already set it up on the crate. It was yellow, scraped gray at the corners, and had a single blue eye in a wide black visor. Zella put a bowl down at the window while she thought what to say about keeping toys off the tables. The machine followed the bowl, then slowly brought its eye back to her face. It rolled back to give Saffy room and the crate creaked.

“There’s been some talk about the bears,” Saffy said. She leaned her freckled nose over her noodles. “I’ll have you know I climbed through that prize booth window with a grain sack and every intention of taking bears. The mice have had most of the fur and you can still sell the eyes. Anyway, I was up to my shoulder in them when Dayrate asked if I’d consider a request for assistance without an estimate of the sum involved. I just about came out of my socks. I asked what kind of assistance and it said it wanted my company at dinner. In that voice, like a man explaining a warranty. I figured a voice like that was good for a bowl on credit.”

Zella checked both ends of the counter. The woman with the red crest was asking for chili oil. A goggled mercenary in the good chair nearest the counter was using a tin bowl for an ashtray. “Who told you it was Dayrate?” Zella asked.

The machine spoke in precisely the slow, expensive voice Saffy had described. “I introduced myself at the prize booth. I would have done so here, too, once I’d paid, since I am told you are more likely to help a paying customer.”

Zella had once filled tins for nineteen hundred soldiers on Dayrate’s count. On cold mornings she’d stood out in the yard listening to that steady voice over a speaker on a barrel. She’d cursed how long it took to get to the number while the cold came up through the soles of her boots. It felt strange to have to lean in to hear it now. Dayrate had provisioned the army right up to the end of the war, when the reserve ammunition went into bunkers only it knew how to find. The bounty on it was the highest in the wastes. The truck could sell out every night for the rest of Zella’s life and she wouldn’t take in that much.

“You told it I’d help,” she said.

“I told it you’d serve it. There were hunters up and down the midway, looking in every booth. I was climbing back out the window when Dayrate asked me if you took late orders. One of the blue-pennant boys turned round and said he knew that voice from the army mess. He was on his radio telling everybody Dayrate was in a yellow shell before my feet hit the ground. I could hear engines starting up on both sides of the wheel. So I came straight here. When they show up I want you to tell them we’re at your table. That’s the bit I promised.”

“My table, Saffy. You could have fucking asked.”

Saffy took her chopsticks out of the bowl and sat very straight. “I’m asking you now. I want them to see that you’re the one who set the bowl down in front of it.”

Zella brought the second bowl over to the crate herself. She could see a crooked print of her own gear and skull on the yellow shell. The machine tipped forward and the steam fogged its visor. She was reaching with the corner of her cloth when it said, “Please leave it there.” She took her hand away. It leaned closer, as if trying to get the fog all the way to the edges.

The bowls on the ledge began to tick against one another.

Beyond the red shade of the lanterns a line of blue pennants sprang up in a wash of white headlamps. “Here. At the truck,” somebody called. Then she heard the same cry from across the midway and more engines came in until she could hear them on every side. Wheels came to a stop one by one at the old brass queue studs she’d dug out of the cracked tar and set back fifty feet from the truck. Zella could still have gone round the counter and put a bowl into the hands of anyone sitting under a lantern. Beyond those lanterns she could hardly see a patch of ground that somebody hadn’t parked on or lit up to shoot across.

“Dayrate,” said a voice from the midway. “We’d like a word.”

* * *

The voice had come from a loudspeaker on a gun carriage. A column of rogue machines was walking guns down the Ferris wheel road on feet as fine as knitting needles. When the first carriage stopped at the brass studs there was a second noise, a single clack that went back up the road as all those feet planted themselves. From the other side of the wheel the gangs kept coming on their bikes. They packed in so close that the ones at the front put their feet down inside the studs while their tires stayed outside. Zella had been tasting the broth for salt all night. Now she could taste the gasoline in it.

Inside the lanterns the drivers at the exchange had grown intent on their soup. Saffy sucked a noodle into her mouth in stages, with a small lift of the chin when she came to a kink in it. She had a gear and skull patched over one shoulder of her jacket, the same yellow as Dayrate’s shell. Zella could look at the two of them and almost believe they’d come to supper together every night of their lives.

The mercenary with the goggle strap dug into his forehead got a boot on the truck’s running board. There was a pale stripe in his beard where he’d wiped his mouth. Zella knew the bare-metal wagons coming down the west road behind him. They worked for whichever side had put down the money that week. He tucked a thumb into his belt and looked at his watch. “I can get you half the bounty if I hand it over still talking,” he told Zella. “You’ve got three minutes to decide. Look out there. Everybody has a different idea of intact.”

“Get off the fucking truck. I serve speeches sitting down.”

He looked back at his good chair. The driver with the marks of chain links on both wrists had dragged it over to where he could lean his elbows on the table, and was moving a slice of egg with great care towards his mouth. The mercenary fetched himself a folding stool. Zella put a bowl in front of him before he could think what to do with his hands. The ladle sank a little deeper than she’d meant into the pot and gave him all the good bits of pork that had been settling to the bottom. She’d have wanted those herself towards morning.

One of the rogue machines came walking up with a squat turret in the cradle of its arms. Its legs made a regular light scraping noise like the baskets against one another when there was a breeze through the truck. It set the turret down on the empty stool beside Saffy’s crate. Steel rang against steel. A spoon handle jumped against the rim of a bowl.

“Seat reserved,” said the machine. “My column will send somebody to sit in it.”

Zella bent over to see if the turret had spoons or anything living attached. There was a brass thread showing where it had been taken off its mount. She tapped the bucket they used for scrap with the back of her ladle. “No saving seats with a turret. That can wait in the bucket with the bent forks. If it gets hungry I’ll bring it something.”

The red-crested woman held up an empty carton for Zella to see. “Clear that gun away and I’ll put in an order to go.”

Zella let the ladle ring down against the bucket once more. The machine picked up the turret. The bucket sank into the tar when it set the thing down. Then it went straight back to the stool and lowered itself onto it, gathering all its narrow legs carefully to one side. “My unit can speak for the rest,” it said. “One seat. One bowl to hold.” It held out one of the long arms it had carried the turret with and Zella put a bowl of steaming broth into its hand.

The machine tilted its head towards the crate. “My column wants the ammunition to stay underground. We would like to discuss what that would cost.”

The red-crested woman fished a radio out of her studded belt. “Kill those engines, boys. You can hear yourselves spend money from here.” One by one the bikes under the blue pennants fell quiet. The woman slid a bounty leaflet out from under her bowl. The paper had gone soft at one corner. There was an old photograph of Dayrate printed in the middle of it, nearly a hundred black cases locked together. Beside the leaflet she set one of the noodle truck’s folding cartons.

“I’ll pay good metal now for a pickup,” she said. “Use the box and put it over the sill. I’ll get one of the boys to carry it on his lap and the merc can give his dirty money to somebody else.”

Saffy put two gloved fingers on the crate by Dayrate’s wheel. The eye shifted over to her hand. It was the first time since dinner had started that Dayrate had looked away from the steam altogether. Zella dipped the ladle in the pot, just enough to wet it, and stuffed it into the carton. The bottom gave as the paper buckled. The stamped gear shone wet.

“No boxing a diner. That takeout costs you the price of a carton, too. I’ve got to buy those.”

“I’ve seen better scrap than that going for free,” said the woman.

“I charge for the box. You can have the hole.”

From the way the woman settled her shoulders, Zella thought she might be getting up. She stayed in the chair. She shook a few coins out of a pocket and set them out on the leaflet, covering the old picture with the money.

The bell on the mercenary’s watch went off. He used one knuckle to stop it and went straight back to his bowl. Zella told him his three minutes were up and asked whether he was done with the pork. He lifted the entire bowl with both hands.

That was when the land rig pulled in across the north road with three white handprints down its flank. The smaller warlords in Raddock’s convoy left their engines running. Raddock climbed down. His coat trailed the whole way from the line. The third hand, which grew low at his ribs, had a good firm grip on the front of it so he’d be able to let the other two hang loose.

“I’ll have the usual, Zella.” He rolled up three sleeves for it. “Put the girl’s on mine, too, and the crate can come out with my order. It belongs with an army. There’s plenty of room for it in mine.”

She set down his shoyu ramen and the lowest hand came straight out to receive it. “All those hands and I still only see one mouth. Everybody puts in their own order.”

There was a tremble in the crate as Dayrate straightened up. Its eye reached the height of Raddock’s lowest wrist. “I would like to finish the bowl I already have,” it said. “If you wish to make me an offer of employment after that, I would like to be able to leave while you are still making it.”

“I get asked to leave respectable establishments all the time,” Saffy said. She tried to pull up a last noodle and sent a spot of broth onto the crate. “It’s actually really nice being somewhere we can do it the other way round.”

Raddock considered Dayrate over the rim of his bowl. He knew how to hold a mouthful of soup a while so it would do him some good. When he lowered the bowl, his third hand set it down on the pale ring at his end of the table. He ate there twice a week.

“I can afford to offer you a place and ask you to think about it,” he said to the machine. “But I’ve got a man who takes my salt south, and every month he shows me a new hole in his truck. You could make people a lot less inclined to shoot at that man. That’s the kind of employment I have in mind. There isn’t a thing on my roads you’d want for, up to and including a cook.” He lifted his eyes to the service window. “It’s an old army mind, Zella. How many bowls did you make because it told you to?”

Zella reached behind her for a cloth. Under her collar the end of a pin had been making a groove where she’d fixed her hair that afternoon. She took it out. If he’d asked her about soup when it was just the two of them she’d have told him how to make the particular one he liked. She’d brought him an extra egg the first night he’d sat in that chair. He’d eaten it in careful halves, twenty minutes apart, when he was still in a coat he knew he could sleep in.

“I cooked in a canning shed once,” she said. “There was a sergeant on the doorstep turning away anybody who couldn’t tell him which officer they served. After a while all you could hear up there was feet on the damn roof. A girl dropped through a hole over the sorter and put her hand down on the rim of the stockpot to catch herself. The sergeant came in with his rifle when he heard her scream. I put her on a bench with a bowl and told him he could talk to me if he wanted anybody off that bench. For the next ten minutes she sat with her feet over the end and her face in the steam. Didn’t want to look up and see who was still there. The recruiting posters said democracy. The chaplain would’ve sworn it was God. Every new machine we got was supposed to make everybody civilized. I still had to stand by that poor kid so she could have one fucking meal without being shot at. Look where you’re sitting, Raddock. That’s as much civilization as I’m asking you for tonight.”

Raddock put his elbows down. “Well, I can wait longer than this fellow,” he said. The mercenary raised his face from his bowl. “Any one of us can wait, if we have a mind to. There are a great many things you can do fifty feet away from a truck. It’ll all be perfectly civil while your friend finishes its soup.”

He laid a hand on the back of each of the empty chairs nearest him and put the third on the rim of his bowl. The driver with the key on the blue ribbon got up to look for his truck. There ought to have been the blinking light he’d put on the roof to find it in the dark. Zella couldn’t see so much as a spoke on the ring toss wheel. Raddock’s rig had the generators to light a whole town and enough room aboard for his people to sleep while they waited.

“How many of yours are you saving seats for?” she asked Raddock.

He said, “How many seats have you got?”

Zella smiled at the nerve of him, keeping two chairs for more of his men when he’d already taken the road. She tucked her loose hair into the top of her apron, where it wouldn’t tickle her neck. “You’ve all come together for the same thing, so there’s some good news for you. I’m putting you down as one party.” She leaned out to see around the edge of the truck. The north road was a row of headlamps with no space between them. “Saffy. Take a look from the ticket step and tell me if any road is still open. Stay inside the studs. I want you here to tell me yourself.”

Saffy had been twirling a chopstick back and forth over the lip of Dayrate’s bowl to see if it could get the eye to follow. She set it down and pulled the goggles over her eyes. She went up the ticket step sideways, with a palm against the doorframe, and leaned out past the wall. She came back at a walk too, although Zella could see from the high red stripes of her socks how straight she was keeping her knees.

“The prize alley’s full too,” she said, getting up close enough to speak to Zella alone. “I came in that way. I might as well have put a sign up for them. There’s a row of blue pennants in there now. Her boys. From the step, look past the booth with the duck painted on it. You can count them yourself from up there.”

Zella went out the side door with the ladle in one hand and the cloth in the other. From the end of the counter she could see the faces at all six tables.

“That’s one party of four hundred and change,” she said. “Here’s the house rule for a party that big. The whole damn lot of you goes hungry if any one of you blocks the door. I want a clear midway and all the roads open.” She knocked her ladle against the counter at the woman with the red crest. “And I want to see every one of your bikes come out of the prize alley and go with the rest.”

The woman brought the radio up from beside her soup. She kept her finger beside the switch. Raddock had tipped back in one of the good chairs. Zella watched the whole of his weight settle safely onto the filed feet.

“You’ll be sitting up a long time waiting for me to pull that rig out of the way so the girl and her friend can go for a walk,” he said. “We could see how long all those pots go on making steam for.”

She took the envelope from behind the board and shook the four tokens out onto the ledge. With the cloth she wiped off a pork order and a reminder that a man was coming to fix the latch. Raddock had a hand out for the tokens. When she put the cloth to the South Gate names, his hand stayed open on the sill. Tomorrow’s supper went in one stroke.

Zella pulled down on the shutter strap till the painted slats covered the light from the pots. The lanterns strung above the tables still burned, each with her skull in a gear on it. Dayrate turned on the crate and looked up at her in their light.

“If I go with Raddock, the rest will follow me,” it said. “You’ll be able to serve the people who come tomorrow.”

For a second she could almost hear that voice over the yard loudspeaker. It could tell you in that tone how many tins to set out even when part of your crew had gone away and no one would tell you how many were coming back.

“Would you go with him if you could take any road you liked?” she asked.

Dayrate looked down at its bowl. Its visor had cleared enough that she could see the eye again. “I’d like to be here a while longer,” it said. “I thought the steam would be gone by now.”

Zella set her hip against the corner of the truck. “Your bowl’s yours,” she said. “Anybody says you owe them more than the price of supper can come and show me the bill.”

She ought to go back inside and put out the burners. The broth at the end of the night was always scorched because she got busy and left it on high. She could still smell the scallions coming up over the sourness from where heat had got at the bone. Usually at the end of service the people who’d been sitting further off would come sit as near the light as they could get. She wanted to go on seeing their faces move up into the light. The driver with the rope marks had put his feet back under his chair. If Zella handed a diner over tonight, next time he came she’d have to watch him try to eat with his gun in his hand. She kept her hand on the shutter. The soup could burn through to the steel for all she was going to do about it.

Raddock turned a token over on the sill. “Those men from the gate won’t sit down with me anywhere else.” He looked from the bare patch of board to the row of headlights where his rig stood. “I pull out on my own, my road’s the only one Dayrate can use. Somebody on one of those bikes will be waiting up there for it before I’ve even got into second gear.”

“You can have the table back when there’s a way for everybody to leave it. If you all give the order here, where everybody can hear, I’ll give you this.” She raised the ladle. “All of you move on the same signal. You can watch one another go. Anybody wants to come and make an offer to a diner after that, they’ll find a place to sit and ask the way I expect a person to ask.”

Over by the line an engine puttered, then caught. Saffy went back to her crate and pushed the goggles up into her hair. A clean oval had been pressed into the dust round each of her eyes.

Raddock kept two hands flat on the table while he picked up the bowl with his third. He drank what remained. The ladle hung in the steam until he put the bowl down. Then he took a radio out of a pocket in his coat. “You’re going to open the north road,” he said into it, in a voice as plain as the one he’d used to ask for the usual. “That means the lot of you. Hold your wheels until you see her swing the ladle.”

The red-crested woman still had her finger beside her switch. When she leaned out to look towards the ring toss, an inked blue swallow came into the lantern light on her shoulder. Her driver stood waiting with the ribbon wound round his wrist. Her finger came down. “Boys behind the duck booth,” she said into the radio. “Lights on. Come up to the mouth of the alley where you can see the ticket step. I want everybody here to be able to count you. You can send me the bill for the gas. When the ladle comes down, you clear out with the rest. I’ve got a man going to his truck.”

The rogue machine set its bowl down and stood. “Ferris wheel road,” it called in its loudspeaker voice. “Entire column to vacate. On signal from the cook.” Then it took its bowl and sat back down facing Dayrate.

From where the mercenary sat, he had a straight view from the crate to his wagons. So did the crews on every gun carriage across the midway. Zella watched him look from the yellow shell to the line of guns. He ran his thumb across the face of his watch, then took out his radio and told his wagons to leave the west road on Zella’s signal.

Once Zella had stepped around the truck and up onto the old ticket step she could see how much work she’d done by that window to make those fifty feet look bigger than they were. The red crates and the tables made it look like someplace you could get lost in for an hour. Seen from the step, they occupied less ground than the land rig needed to turn.

Zella could see the head of every road from the step. The bikes had come around the duck booth into the mouth of the alley. She counted seven lamps, with every rider in view, and the nearest rider raised a glove towards her. The ticket step hummed through the soles of her shoes. The metal in the ladle caught a slice of light from the nearest lantern when she brought it up. She waited till it shone and swung it down.

* * *

All the light went sideways at once. Raddock’s rig backed away with three sets of wheels turning and showed her the roofs behind it as they moved. Across the midway the mercenaries pulled out, each wagon slow under enough tin and iron to make a second fairground. The rogue machines took their walking guns out the Ferris wheel road with a noise like a hundred wrenches dropped on a shop floor. The last of the bikes came out of the alley two at a time. Zella stayed on the step till she could see the red light at the back of every one of them. The dust hung there after they’d gone, showing the width of every road by the time it took the far lanterns to come back into view.

The bowls of the two drivers were still on the table when they walked in opposite directions past the brass studs. The one with the key on the blue ribbon came back for the boots he’d left under the table. He sat in the same chair to lace them. He took his time over both knots.

She opened the shutter. The red-crested woman picked up the coins from her leaflet, counted out what she owed for the carton and left it by her bowl. She called to Zella to save the six o’clock breakfast for the boys she’d kept up all night.

“You can be first in line with the rest of the world at six,” Zella told her.

The woman picked up her chopsticks and dipped them back into the soup.

By the time Zella had got a new basket of noodles down into the pot, Dayrate had made it off the crate and out to the line. It was barely high enough to see above a wheel rut. Saffy went after it as far as the brass studs. She had a dark spot of broth on one cuff of her yellow jacket.

“I could walk with you as far as the prize booths,” she called. “I mean, you can make up your mind all the way out there if you like.”

Dayrate rolled over the studs and went about as far again before it stopped. With the fairground dark on that side, it had the whole breadth of the road to itself. You could follow the one blue eye as it took in the gutted prize booths and raised its gaze to the Ferris wheel.

Then it turned around. Coming back towards the truck it went slowly enough to choose a path among the cups and coins the wheels had shaken out of the cracks in the tar. Saffy dragged the crate into a fresh patch of light. Her eyes narrowed as she grinned and made room at her side. Zella reached for the ladle. Dayrate came straight to the crate. “I would like one more bowl, please,” it said, and tilted its face up to where the steam would be.
