Somebody had tied a man to one of Zella Stovall’s good chairs. There were six of those at the Last Turn Fairground, all steel, and she’d spent half the afternoon filing the feet flat so a man could rock back a little without going all the way over.

“He’s a prisoner,” said the man who had brought him.

“Course he’s a prisoner. A prisoner would be the only son of a bitch to put up with rope digging into him like that. Give him his hands if you want me to give him supper.”

Across the table a woman with crimson hair shaved to the scalp on both sides watched the man with the rope work the knot. She had brought a prisoner too. There was a length of chain between his wrists, with a spring clip they must have borrowed off a dog lead. When she saw the rope come loose, she undid the clip. Then the two prisoners drew up their chairs, which was the point of having the exchange here, within fifty feet of the Midnight Noodle Truck. You could have brought them out onto the dead rides and saved yourself the price of dinner. Then you’d have to eat with one hand on your gun, which, as Zella well remembered, was a shitty way to get an egg down your throat.

She lifted the basket of noodles and struck it twice against the rim to drain. The exhausted man who came in to wash dishes for his supper squeezed past her with a stack of bowls. Inside each bowl was the same grinning skull set in the teeth of a gear that Zella had stenciled on the truck when she bought it. She’d had to fill in the teeth one at a time by flashlight. Now they were on everybody’s jackets and bags. She sometimes had to point from the gear on a customer’s jacket to the red lettering beside the menu. *No fighting within fifty feet of the truck.* You could wear that patch all the way out into the hills and people would still shoot at you if they meant to.

She put the eggs in last and slid the bowls across the ledge. Both drivers put their faces down to the steam. It was still hot enough at midnight to bring the tar up from the cracks in the midway, but you could smell the scallions in the broth right across to the empty ticket office. The braces of the Ferris wheel went black against the moon just as the red ramen sign came on.

When Zella turned back to the window, Saffy had hooked one cream high-top behind a packing crate and drawn it up for a stool. Her goggles were pushed into her black fringe. When she wanted credit or had one of Zella’s saucepans in her pockets she wore the exact grin she was wearing now.

“Two bowls?” Saffy said. “You can charge me for both before I tell you what I’ve brought.”

Whatever it was she’d already set it up on the crate. It was yellow, scraped gray at the corners, and had a single blue eye in a wide black visor. Zella put a bowl down at the window while she thought what to say about keeping toys off the tables. The little thing followed the bowl, then slowly brought its eye back to her face. The crate creaked as it rolled back to give Saffy room.

“There’s been some talk about the bears,” Saffy said. She leaned her freckled nose over her noodles. “I’ll have you know I went into that prize booth with a grain sack and every intention of taking bears. The plastic eyes fetch a little. You have to start with the eyes because of the mice. Anyway, I was up to my shoulder in them when Dayrate asked if I’d consider a request for assistance without an estimate of the sum involved. I just about came out of my socks. I asked what kind of assistance and it said it wanted my company at dinner. In that voice, like a man explaining a warranty. I did think you’d want to hear it.”

Zella checked both ends of the counter. The woman with the red crest was asking for chili oil. A goggled mercenary in the good chair nearest the counter was using a tin bowl for an ashtray. “Who told you it was Dayrate?” Zella asked.

The little machine spoke in precisely the slow, expensive voice Saffy had described. “I introduced myself at the prize booth. I would have done so here, too, once I’d paid, since I am told you are more likely to help a paying customer.”

Zella had once filled tins for nineteen hundred soldiers on Dayrate’s count. It had provisioned the army right up to the end of the war. The reserve ammunition was still there, buried in bunkers only Dayrate knew how to find. They’d put the highest bounty in the wastes on the little machine. It would have been plenty if all you wanted to do was buy Last Turn and bring every light on the Ferris wheel back to life.

“You told it I’d help,” she said.

“I told it you’d serve it. I knew about the search. I expected we’d have a bit more time before I needed anything else from you. The boys with the blue pennants were still trying to get their bikes started when they saw me get out the window. You’d think they’d keep them in better nick, the amount they ride around after people.”

Zella brought the second bowl over to the crate herself. Dayrate tipped forward to trace the plume of steam, and warmth put a film of moisture all the way across its visor. “Please leave it there,” it said. Zella would have wiped the visor for it if it were a kid’s glasses, the way she could now see it was plainly enjoying having them fogged.

The bowls on the ledge began to tick against one another. Beyond the red shade of the lanterns a line of blue pennants sprang up in a wash of white headlamps. More engines followed until she could hear them on every side. Wheels came to a stop one by one at the old brass queue studs she’d set into the paving fifty feet out from the truck. Zella could still have gone round the counter and put a bowl into the hands of anyone sitting under a lantern. Beyond those lanterns she could hardly see a patch of dirt that somebody hadn’t parked on or lit up to shoot across.

“Dayrate,” said a voice from the midway. “We’d like a word.”

* * *

The sound had come from a loudspeaker on a gun carriage. The rogue machines had come up along the old Ferris wheel road, walking their largest guns on feet as fine as knitting needles. At the head of the north road a land rig plugged the gap beside the wheel. Zella recognized Raddock’s three white handprints down one flank and the uneven chug of all the small rigs behind it. The gangs had painted their outfits by crews and brought anything with two wheels. That left the bare-metal wagons on the fourth road, with fuel cans strapped high to go wherever they liked after this, assuming anybody could pay the mercenaries to go. Zella had been tasting the broth for salt all night. Now she could taste the gasoline in it.

Inside the lanterns the drivers at the exchange had grown intent on their soup. Saffy sucked a noodle into her mouth in stages, with a small lift of the chin when she came to a kink in it. She had a gear and skull patched over one shoulder of her yellow jacket, close enough in color to the little machine that Zella could look at the two of them and almost believe they’d come to supper together every night of their lives.

The mercenary with the goggle strap dug into his forehead got a boot on the truck’s running board. He cleared his throat the way men did when they’d tipped somebody to listen. “I can get you half the bounty on a clean delivery,” he told Zella. “The people out there are prepared to give you three minutes to accept. Afterwards there’s considerable uncertainty about the going rate.”

“Get off the fucking truck. I serve speeches sitting down.”

He looked back at his good chair. The driver with the marks of chain links on both wrists had dragged it over to where he could lean his elbows on the table, and was moving a slice of egg with great care towards his mouth. The mercenary fetched himself a folding stool. Zella put a bowl in front of him before he could think what to do with his hands. The ladle sank a little deeper than she’d meant into the pot and gave him all the good bits of pork that had been settling to the bottom. She’d have wanted those herself towards morning.

One of the rogue machines came walking up with a squat turret in the cradle of its arms. Its legs made a regular light scraping noise like the baskets against one another when there was a breeze through the truck. It set the turret down on the empty stool beside Saffy’s crate with a sound that stopped conversation across all six tables.

“Seat reserved,” said the machine. “You are to serve whichever of our units comes to collect it.”

Zella bent over to see if the turret had spoons or anything living attached. There was a brass thread showing where it had been taken off its mount. She tapped the bucket they used for scrap with the back of her ladle. “No saving seats with a turret. That can wait in the bucket with the bent forks. If it gets hungry I’ll bring it something.”

She let the ladle ring down against the bucket once more. The machine picked up the turret. The bucket sank into the tar when it set the thing down. Then it fetched a stool it could lower itself onto, gathering all its narrow legs carefully to one side. “My unit can speak for the rest,” it said. On the table beside Dayrate a rough ring of soup had dried onto the wood. Zella asked the machine whether it wanted a bowl that close as well. It held out one of the long arms it had carried the turret with and waited.

The red-crested woman fished a radio out of her studded belt. “Things are moving very nicely, boys,” she told it. Somebody answered high and tinny over the sound of the engines beneath the blue pennants. The woman slid a bounty leaflet out from under her bowl. The paper had gone soft at one corner. There was an old photograph of Dayrate printed in the middle of it, nearly a hundred black cases locked together, as much heat pouring out of them as you could get in a boxcar. Beside the leaflet she set one of the noodle truck’s folding cartons.

“I’ll pay good metal now for a pickup,” she said. “Use the box and put it over the sill. I’ll get one of the boys to carry it on his lap and the merc can give his dirty money to somebody else.”

Saffy put two gloved fingers on the crate by Dayrate’s wheel. The eye shifted over to her hand. It was the first time since dinner had started that the little machine had looked away from the steam altogether. Zella dipped the ladle in the pot, just enough to wet it, and stuffed it into the carton. The bottom gave as the paper buckled. The stamped gear shone wet.

“No boxing a diner. That takeout costs you the price of a carton, too. I’ve got to buy those.”

“I didn’t pick up a damn thing,” said the woman. From the way she settled her shoulders, Zella thought she might be getting up to prove she could. She stayed in the chair. After a while she shook a few coins out of a pocket and set them out on the leaflet, covering the old picture with the money.

Raddock came down from the land rig last. His coat trailed the whole way from the line. The third hand, which grew low at his ribs, had a good firm grip on the front of it so he’d be able to let the other two hang loose. Anybody who wanted the land rig out of the north road had to ask that man. Even his allies ate from little tin plates at the back of the thing while it sat where it liked.

“I’ll have the usual, Zella.” He rolled up three sleeves for it. “Put the girl’s on mine, too, and the crate can come out with my order. It belongs with an army. There’s plenty of room for it in mine.”

She put a bowl down and the lowest hand came straight out to receive it. “Everybody here puts in their own order. I’ve even got the sense to ask a man who can knock over three bowls whether he’d like one or three.”

There was a tremble in the crate as Dayrate straightened up. Its eye reached the height of Raddock’s lowest wrist. “I would like to finish the bowl I already have,” it said. “If you wish to make me an offer of employment after that, I would like to be able to leave while you are still making it.”

“We get asked to leave respectable establishments all the time,” Saffy said. She tried to pull up a last noodle and sent a spot of broth onto the crate. “It’s actually really nice being somewhere we can do it the other way round.”

Raddock ate a little and considered Dayrate. He knew how to hold a mouthful of soup a while so it would do him some good. When he took the bowl down he found the proper end of the table with his lower hand, in the casual way of someone who came here twice a week.

“I can afford to offer you a place and ask you to think about it,” he said to the machine. “But I've got a man who takes my salt south, and every month he shows me a new hole in his truck. You could make people a lot less inclined to shoot at that man. That's the kind of employment I have in mind. There isn’t a thing on my roads you’d want for, up to and including a cook.” He lifted his eyes to the service window. “It's an old army mind, Zella. How many bowls did you make because it told you to?”

Zella reached behind her for a cloth. Under her collar the end of a pin had been making a groove where she’d fixed her hair that afternoon. She took it out. If he’d asked her about soup when it was just the two of them she’d have told him how to make the particular one he liked. She’d brought him an extra egg the first night he’d sat in that chair. He’d eaten it in careful halves, twenty minutes apart, when he was still in a coat he knew he could sleep in. He had apparently taken that for something that could be owed and collected.

“I cooked in a canning shed once,” she said. “They’d sent a sergeant to keep everybody off the doorstep who hadn’t stood up and said they were ours. After a while all you could hear up there was feet on the damn roof. A girl came down through a hole over the sorter and burned her hand catching the pot. I put her on a bench with a bowl. For the next ten minutes she sat with her feet over the end and her face in the steam. Didn’t want to look up and see somebody who could tell her to go. We had democracy and religion and all the bloody technology you could get on a train. I still had to stand by her till she’d finished. I’ll take a place a person can sit down to eat without getting shot. That’ll do me for civilization.”

Raddock put his elbows down. “Well, I can wait longer than this fellow,” he said. The mercenary raised his face from his bowl. “Any one of us can wait, if we have a mind to. There are a great many things you can do fifty feet away from a truck. It’ll all be perfectly civil while your friend finishes its soup.”

He laid a hand on the back of each of the empty chairs nearest him and put the third on the rim of his bowl. In the light out in the midway Zella could see the boy who’d been a prisoner in a chair stand up and look for his truck. There ought to have been the little blinking light he’d put on the roof to find it in the dark. Zella knew where he parked, by the old ring toss, and she couldn’t see so much as a spoke on the ring toss wheel. At the end of the north road a land rig from which nobody had ever needed to walk home could wait for all her regulars to finish.

“How many of yours are you saving seats for?” she asked Raddock.

He said, “How many seats have you got?”

Zella found herself smiling. She tucked her hair loose into the top of her apron, where it wouldn’t tickle her neck. “You’ve all come together for the same thing, so there's some good news for you. I'm putting you down as one party.” She leaned out to see around the edge of the truck. As far as she could see every engine on the north road was standing where it had come to rest. “Saffy. Take a look from the ticket step and tell me if any road is still open. Keep on the studs. I want you here to tell me yourself.”

Saffy had been twirling a chopstick back and forth over the lip of Dayrate’s bowl to see if it could get the eye to follow. She set it down now and pulled the goggles over her eyes. A white beam swept the ground ahead of her as she walked to the ticket step and raised a hand to see underneath it. She came back at a walk too, although Zella could see from the high red stripes of her socks how straight she was keeping her knees.

“The prize alley,” she said, getting up close enough to speak to Zella alone. “I thought it’d be the way out. There’s a row of blue pennants in there, too. Her boys. You’ll miss them completely if you’re only watching the roads. Go up on the step and look past the booth with the duck painted on it. You can count them yourself from up there.”

Zella took her ladle round to the end of the counter. Here she could see all the claimants from the waist up. There was a little register beside the ledge with a chalkboard behind it where she wrote things a customer might want to remember. Tomorrow’s supper was on it, a seat for Raddock and a seat for the men he needed permission from to get those salt trucks down past the south gate. Raddock had paid ahead for the whole table when they’d agreed to come. Men who’d been shelling his trucks at the beginning of the month would roll in and ask which broth she’d put on as though they sat at his table every morning of their lives.

“That’s one party of four hundred and change,” she said. “Here's the house rule for a party that big. The whole damn lot of you goes hungry if any one of you blocks the door. I want a clear midway and all the roads open. That includes your boys in the prize alley.” She knocked her ladle against the counter at the woman with the red crest. “You tell them I want to see their lights all the way out, too.”

The woman brought the radio up from beside her soup. She kept her finger beside the switch. Raddock had tipped back in one of the good chairs. Zella watched the whole of his weight settle safely onto the filed feet.

“You'll be sitting up in there a long time waiting for us to move a great big machine because a girl wants to go for a walk,” he said. “We could see how long all those pots go on making steam for.”

She wiped the chalkboard with the cloth. Tomorrow’s supper went in one stroke. There was a smudged reminder about a pork order and a man who was coming by to fix the latch, so she wiped those off, too. She took the deposit from the till and set it where Raddock could see what she was returning. Then she took hold of the strap and brought the shutter down till the painted slats covered the light from the pots. The light in which they all sat came now from the hanging lanterns with her skull in a gear on each one. Dayrate had turned on the crate to look up at her, with its blue eye wide and bright in the dark space where she generally kept the soup bowls.

She ought to go back inside and put out the burners. The broth at the end of the night was always a little burned because she got busy and left it on high. She could still smell the scallions coming up over the sourness from where heat had got at the bone. Usually at the end of service the people who’d been sitting further off would come sit as near the light as they could get. She wanted to go on seeing their faces move up into the light. Now the driver with the rope marks who’d been looking for his truck had put his feet back under his chair. She couldn’t get any of them out of there while the engines stayed in the roads. She had no idea how she would get anyone to risk coming for a meal after tonight if she let this go the way Raddock wanted. She kept her hand on the shutter. The soup could burn through to the steel for all she was going to do about it.

Raddock turned a coin over on the sill. “The men from the south gate will be here for supper tomorrow. They come here to hear me out. Put them anywhere else with me and we'd have to send the whole damn meal by radio.” His eyes were on the patch of chalk where it had been written. “If I move my rig right now, every one of these people will tip into that road behind me.”

“You can have the table back when there's a way for everybody to leave it. If you all give the order here, where everybody can hear, I’ll give you this.” She raised the ladle. “All of you move on the same signal. You can watch one another go. Anybody wants to come and make an offer to a diner after that, they'll find a place to sit and ask the way I expect a person to ask.”

Over by the line an engine puttered, then caught. Saffy went back to her crate and pushed the goggles up into her hair. A clean oval had been pressed into the dust round each of her eyes. She rested a hand on the edge of the crate, close beside Dayrate, and they waited.

Raddock kept two hands flat on the table while he picked up the bowl with his third. He drank what remained. The ladle hung in the steam until he put the bowl down. Then he took a little radio out of a pocket in his coat. “You’re going to open the north road,” he said into it, in a voice as plain as the one he’d used to ask for the usual. “That means the lot of you. Hold your wheels until you see her swing the ladle.” The woman with the red crest pulled her own radio out of her belt and told her people to do the same. The rogue machine stood to speak into the night in its speaker voice. The mercenary, who had started the night naming a short time for everybody else to make up their minds, made his own people the last of them.

Once Zella had stepped around the truck and up onto the old ticket step she could see how much work she’d done by that window to make those fifty feet look bigger than they were. The red crates and the tables made it look like someplace you could get lost in for an hour. Seen from the step, they occupied a patch of dirt smaller than the patch the land rig needed to turn. She could see the alley Saffy had shown her, too, a thin slot of blue where gaps in the tarps lit up as the bikes revved. She stood where all four roads could see her. The metal in the ladle caught a slice of light from the nearest lantern when she brought it up. She waited till it shone and swung it down.

* * *

All the light went sideways at once. Raddock’s rig backed away with three sets of wheels turning and showed her the roofs behind it as they moved. Across the midway the mercenaries pulled out, each wagon slow under enough tin and iron to make a second fairground. The rogue machines took their walking guns out the Ferris wheel road with a noise like a hundred wrenches dropped on a shop floor. The last of the bikes came out of the alley two at a time. Zella stayed on the step till she could see the red light at the back of every one of them. The dust hung there after they’d gone, showing the width of every road by the time it took the far lanterns to come back into view.

The bowls of the two drivers were still on the table when they walked in opposite directions past the brass studs. One of them had taken his shoes off under the table. He came back for them and went out a second time. Zella had a good long look at him on his way. She remembered the damn chair less and less the further he got from it.

She opened the shutter. The red-crested woman picked up the coins from her leaflet, counted out what she owed for the carton and left it by her bowl. She called to Zella to save the six o’clock breakfast for the boys she’d kept up all night. Zella said she could be first in line with the rest of the world at six. The woman picked up her chopsticks and dipped them back into the soup.

By the time Zella had got a new basket of noodles down into the pot, Dayrate had made it off the crate and out to the line. It was barely high enough to see above a wheel rut. Saffy went after it as far as the brass studs, the cuffs of her jacket damp from the one spot of broth she’d tried to wipe off the crate with her sleeve.

“I could walk with you as far as the prize booths,” she called. “I mean, you can make up your mind all the way out there if you like.”

Dayrate rolled over the studs and went about as far again before it stopped. With the fairground dark on that side, it had the whole breadth of the road to itself. You could follow the one blue eye as it took in the gutted prize booths and raised its gaze to the Ferris wheel.

Then it turned around. Coming back towards the truck it went slowly enough to choose a path among the cups and coins the wheels had shaken out of the hard dirt. Saffy dragged the crate into a fresh patch of light and made room at her side. Dayrate came straight to her. “I would like one more bowl, please,” it said, and tilted its face up to wait.
