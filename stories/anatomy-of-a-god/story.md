# The Anatomy of a God

The bolt struck Avarrax beneath the ribs.

It should have killed a man. It punched through the white silk at his side, spun him half around on the temple steps, and drove a gasp from the hundreds packed into the court. Then Avarrax straightened.

Sun-colored light moved beneath his skin.

Edras saw it because he was a field medic, because he had spent half his life noticing what bodies tried to hide, and because he had watched that exact ripple before. It gathered in a hard knot below Avarrax's breastbone, divided toward his right shoulder and left hip, then returned to the wound. The bolt slid out as flesh closed behind it. Avarrax caught the bloody shaft without looking and snapped it between his fingers.

The crowd recoiled as one body.

On the execution platform, seven bound villagers watched the blood vanish from their conqueror's robe. One of them, a gray-haired potter with a split lip, had been struggling against her ropes. Now she went still.

Avarrax lifted the broken bolt. He was taller than any of his guards, broad beneath a mantle sewn with hammered gold. Light rimmed his shaved head and shone from his eyes. Whether the radiance came from his body or from some trick hidden in the temple roof, Edras could not tell. He could tell that the blood on Avarrax's fingers was real.

"One among you mistakes concealment for courage," Avarrax said. His voice filled the court without effort. "Therefore, all will learn the price of striking at a god. Begin with the prisoners."

The guards beside the platform lowered their hooked spears.

Edras pulled against the hand clamped on his shoulder. The guard behind him tightened his grip and forced him down until one knee hit the paving stones. Edras's medical satchel lay open nearby, its contents scattered for inspection: linen rolls, two wooden splints, three thin brace plates, a coil of waxed strap, needle, thread. They had taken his knives and medicines. They had laughed at the rest.

"This one first?" the guard asked.

Avarrax looked down at Edras. "The foreign physician has been offered mercy twice. He treated rebels who rejected my peace, then refused to name those who sheltered them." He descended one step. "Kneel willingly now, healer, or share their sentence."

Edras looked past him at the prisoners. The youngest was trembling so hard the platform rope quivered. The potter met Edras's eyes and gave one small shake of her head. Don't bargain for us, it seemed to say. Or perhaps only: Don't give him what he wants.

Behind Avarrax, the last trace of the healing ripple faded at his hip.

Edras remembered Miora on a narrow operating table, her fingers crushing his while her own body fought the surgeons.

She had been nineteen. A fall had broken her leg badly, but before the bone could be set, her gift had sealed it crooked. Every attempt to correct it provoked another burst of repair. Miora had listened to three surgeons tell her how dangerous the next attempt would be. Then she had looked at Edras and said, "If I change my mind, you stop them. Until then, you help them finish."

They had fitted dull plates over the three places where her healing gathered, not to damage them but to make each pulse circle harmlessly while the surgeons worked. Edras had counted the timing aloud. Miora had sworn at him for counting too slowly and made him begin again.

He remembered the sequence. He remembered something more important: the choice had been hers.

The guard shoved between Edras's shoulder blades. "Answer him."

Edras let his gaze travel over Avarrax as though assessing a patient. "Your wound is going to reopen."

Silence reached the far edge of the court in widening rings.

Avarrax touched his unmarked side. "Is that your final prayer?"

"Professional opinion. The lower return dragged. Either you're tired, or the trick is failing."

The light in Avarrax's eyes sharpened. His guard struck Edras across the mouth.

Pain flared. Edras tasted blood, but he had seen what he needed. At the impact, Avarrax's body had answered a threat that never touched him: the knot below his ribs brightened, the shoulder flickered, then the hip. Too fast to count by numbers. Not too fast to count by heartbeats.

Miora's pattern.

Not proof that they were the same kind of being. Not proof of anything beyond the path his flesh had just taken. But flesh was enough for the next few minutes.

Avarrax raised one hand, stopping the guard from striking again. "Explain my death to me, physician."

"I didn't say death. I said you're not invulnerable."

A murmur stirred under the colonnade. Avarrax heard it. That was the danger of ruling by spectacle: every boast required an encore.

"You saw an assassin's weapon fail," he said. "You saw my body cast it out."

"I've seen tavern performers swallow fire. I don't worship them either."

The guard's fingers dug into Edras's shoulder, awaiting permission.

Edras nodded toward the heavy execution frame behind the prisoners. Two upright posts supported a crossbeam, with ropes running through iron rings to a stone counterweight. The condemned were meant to be hauled into view one at a time. "Give me one attempt. No guards holding my arms. No priest whispering about miracles. Let me use that frame and what remains in my kit. If I fail, I'll fasten the prisoners to it myself. Then I'll kneel."

The potter's stare turned furious. Good. Fury meant she had not surrendered.

One of Avarrax's officers leaned close. "Radiant One, there is no need."

"There is every need." Avarrax smiled at the watching crowd. "Faith untested is only fear wearing ceremonial clothes."

Edras almost laughed. Avarrax had named his own reign and mistaken it for wisdom.

"Release him," Avarrax ordered. "Leave him his sticks and string."

The guard stepped back.

Edras rose slowly, gathered his splints, plates, and straps, and carried them to the frame. He did not look at the prisoners again. If his plan showed in his face, he wanted Avarrax watching the wrong face.

"Stand against the post," he said.

Several people in the crowd made small, disbelieving sounds.

Avarrax spread his arms. "Do you command your gods so casually where you come from?"

"Only the ones who volunteer as patients."

Avarrax stepped onto the platform and set his back against the right post. The wood was thick enough to survive a struggling body and old enough to have drunk years of rain. Edras looped the first strap around Avarrax's chest, threading one brace plate beneath it. He placed it over the fading knot of warmth he had seen below the ribs.

Avarrax watched his hands. "Your pulse is unsteady."

"I've had a long morning."

"You are afraid."

"That's what an unsteady pulse generally means."

The second plate went against the shoulder path, held between a short splint and the frame. The third lay along the return near the hip. Ordinary wood. Ordinary metal. Ordinary straps. With Miora, their purpose had been to hold a body in an exact posture while its own repair drove against the interruption and folded back on itself. The surgeons had adjusted them with calm hands in a clean room.

Edras had a stained execution frame and an audience waiting to see someone die.

He ran the free ends of all three straps through separate rings. The first he tied to the main line above Avarrax's head. The second he hooked to the platform rail. The third he wound around the counterweight's release peg.

"Is this your attack?" Avarrax flexed. The chest strap creaked. "A harness?"

"I haven't started."

Edras took the remaining splint in both hands and drove its end toward Avarrax's throat.

Avarrax caught it. Wood cracked in his fist. He backhanded Edras hard enough to throw him across the platform.

Edras landed on one shoulder and rolled. Around the court, the murmur broke into frightened laughter. Avarrax tore through the chest strap and stepped away from the post, dragging the other two loose.

For one sick instant Edras thought he had misjudged the strength of the waxed weave.

Then he saw the torn chest strap still looped through the upper ring, exactly where he needed it. The break had given him slack enough to tie its end to the counterweight line.

He crawled toward his satchel.

Avarrax followed at a leisurely pace. "This is the science your country sets against divinity?"

"Mostly we use it for sprained ankles."

Edras snatched the coil, rose, and rushed him. Avarrax seized the front of his coat. Edras wrapped the loose strap around Avarrax's forearm and let himself be lifted. His boots left the boards. He kicked the rail, swung sideways, and dropped the strap over the second iron ring.

Avarrax flung him down. This time Edras's head struck wood. The world flashed white.

He heard the youngest prisoner cry out. He heard the scrape of a spear as a guard shifted closer. Then Avarrax hauled him upright by the collar.

"Enough," Avarrax said.

Edras blinked until the frame stopped doubling. The three plates had slipped but not fallen. The straps crossed the repair paths. Above them, lines ran from ring to rail to counterweight in a crude triangle.

He needed the pulse.

"You're right," Edras said. "No one will believe sticks beat a god. Give them another miracle first."

Avarrax's smile vanished.

"The bolt wound happened too quickly," Edras continued. "Half the court didn't see it. Cut your palm. Let them watch you heal. Then kill me."

The officer below the platform said, "Radiant One, end this."

But Avarrax was looking at the crowd. Hundreds of faces had lifted toward him. Edras could almost feel the question move among them. Why refuse?

Avarrax released Edras. From his belt he drew a thin ceremonial blade the guards had not considered a weapon because it belonged to him. He drew its edge across his palm. A bright line opened.

"Witness," he said.

Light gathered below his ribs.

Edras pulled the upper line.

The first plate tightened. The healing glow struck it and divided. Edras stamped the hooked line off the rail. The second strap snapped taut across the shoulder path. Avarrax jerked, surprise breaking his composure for the first time.

One heartbeat.

Edras kicked the release peg free.

The stone counterweight dropped. The third strap cinched, catching the returning ripple. Golden light raced beneath Avarrax's skin, reached the first plate again, and circled.

His cut palm remained open.

Some of the radiance around him guttered. Not all. Edras could not tell whether the dimming came from the stalled repair, shock, or rage, and he had no time to decide.

Avarrax stared at his hand. "You actually managed to counteract my regeneration?"

Edras braced both hands on the line. "I don't know what your deal is, but where I am from guys like you aren't rare, hell, my stepsister is one of you. We know how to deal with your anatomy."

"What are you talking about!? I am a GOD!"

"You have the ego of one, that's for sure."

Avarrax roared and lunged.

The frame shuddered. One upright split near its base. Edras hauled on the line, but the counterweight bounced against the paving below, giving Avarrax half a stride of slack. That was enough. He caught Edras by the throat and lifted him.

His strength had not vanished with his healing. Neither had the light in his eyes.

Edras clawed at Avarrax's wrist. The court darkened at the edges. He had proved a limit and mistaken proof for victory.

"Guards," Avarrax said, his voice ragged but still vast. "Kill the condemned. Kill anyone who moves."

No spear fell.

The officers looked at one another. A young guard lowered his weapon first. Another backed away from the platform.

The potter moved.

She threw herself sideways, caught the counterweight rope in her bound hands, and pulled. The other prisoners understood before the crowd did. Three of them seized the line with her. Their combined weight dragged the stone clear of the ground.

The straps snapped tight.

Avarrax staggered backward. His grip opened. Edras fell, coughing, and rolled beneath the rail.

The youngest prisoner hooked one ankle around the cracked upright and dragged it toward the socket. The potter shouted, "Pull!"

People rushed from the edge of the court.

Not everyone. Many fled. Some crouched behind pillars. But a dozen villagers reached the platform and laid hands on the line. A mason looped it around the sound post. A woman with flour on her sleeves climbed up and cut the prisoners' bonds on the ceremonial blade Avarrax had dropped. Two guards turned their spears crosswise and used the shafts to brace the splitting frame.

Avarrax fought them. Wood groaned. His shoulder tore free of one strap, and the plate clattered across the boards. Golden light surged toward the gap, but the other two paths held it circling. He reached for Edras.

The potter caught the loose chest strap and flung it to him.

Edras wound it around the broken upright, passed it through the upper ring, and pulled until the first plate seated again. Avarrax's radiance flared, throwing hard shadows across the temple wall.

"I gave you peace!" he shouted.

"You gave us graves," the potter said.

With the frame stabilized, the crowd forced Avarrax back against the post. They bound his wrists with execution rope, added chains from the platform, and locked the counterweight line around a pillar base. The conqueror strained against them, but each struggle only drew more hands.

At last he stopped.

The cut on his palm still had not closed. A single bead of blood slid to his wrist.

The potter picked up a fallen spear.

Edras saw the grip she took, both hands low on the shaft. He stepped between her and Avarrax.

"Move," she said.

"No."

"He was going to hang my son."

The youngest prisoner stood behind her, rubbing his freed wrists. So that was her son. He looked from the spear to Avarrax and said nothing.

"I know what he meant to do," Edras said. "But the suspension is temporary. If you run that through him, I don't know what his body will do while the repair is caught. He may die. He may tear himself apart trying not to."

"And if we let him heal?"

"Then you judge him with clear heads and enough chains. Not in the same breath you escaped him."

The spear point did not lower.

Edras kept his hands at his sides. "He called fear faith. Don't let rage become justice because it also wants a better name."

The potter looked at her son. He reached past her and pushed the spearhead toward the floor.

After a long moment, she let it drop.

The court began to change shape. Guards removed their gold sashes. Villagers freed the remaining prisoners and closed the outer gates. Someone found heavier chains in the temple storehouse. The officer who had advised Avarrax to end the contest tried to slip away, but his own soldiers stopped him and took his sword.

Edras knelt beside the frame to inspect the straps.

"Careful," he told the four people who had gathered to learn. He pointed to each plate without naming a place on the body. "These marks show the order for him. Him alone. Keep the tension even. If his hand begins to close or the light gathers in one place, increase the counterweight by a little, not a lot. When you release him for water, washing, or judgment, loosen the plates in reverse, one at a time. Then let the repair finish before you suspend it again."

The flour-sleeved woman frowned. "You said people like him weren't rare where you came from."

"People who heal strangely aren't rare. That doesn't mean they heal the same way." Edras looked up at Avarrax. "I used this because I watched his body and recognized one pattern. Try it on someone else and you could kill them."

"Your stepsister," the young guard said. "Is she dangerous?"

Edras thought of Miora relearning stairs, rejecting every offered arm until the day she chose to take his. He thought of her laughing when he admitted that her swearing had ruined his count.

"She runs a bakery," he said. "Her greatest crime is charging too much for winter pears."

The guard flushed.

Edras removed a page from his field ledger. He drew the frame, the three straps, the counterweight, and the release order. He did not draw a body. Beside each plate he copied the tiny notch pattern he had cut into the metal, enough to keep Avarrax safely confined and no more.

"Who rules now?" someone asked.

No one answered immediately. The potter glanced at her son, then at the guards without sashes and the villagers coiling unused rope.

"No one alone," she said. "Not tonight. Tomorrow we choose a council. We collect testimony. We find every cell he kept. Then we decide how a man answers for calling himself our god."

Avarrax lifted his head. Even bound, even dimmed, he made the old court seem too small for him.

"I am not a man," he said.

Edras closed his satchel. "Maybe not. Your anatomy doesn't decide what you deserve. Neither does your title."

By sunset, the prisoners had become jailers and the worshipers had become witnesses. They carried the wounded out first. Edras checked each one at the foot of the platform, binding a burned hand, setting two fingers, cleaning the potter's split lip. No one asked him to heal Avarrax, and Avarrax asked for nothing.

When the court was nearly empty, Edras sat on the lowest temple step with his ledger balanced on his knee. He began a letter.

*Miora,* he wrote, *today the thing that once kept you safe helped me keep seven strangers alive.*

Behind him, chains shifted.

"Kneel," Avarrax commanded.

The potter walked past carrying a lantern. The young guard followed with a bucket of water. Villagers crossed the court in twos and threes, speaking of locks, witnesses, food, and morning.

No one knelt.

Edras bent over the page and kept writing.
