# A Turn at Tallow Gate

At the top of the watchtower Catrin stepped out of her shoes. She climbed into a gap in the battlements and rested her heels on the ledge, on grey stone the weather had hollowed out at the rim. The cold went straight through her stockings. At the palace she had made it to the end of the dance before admitting the shoes were too small, and the knowledge had been an agreeable secret all the way down through the streets of Vendrel. Now she could spread her toes.

Across the city, the first lantern Catrin had seen come alight in her family’s castle had been in the high round tower with the silver weathercock. She had been crossing the old horse fair at the time. Now she could see the whole of the hill, with the lanterns in the open galleries and a tall yellow row of windows along the hall. The castle was more beautiful from here than it was from anywhere inside it. You could see why they had built the galleries where they had, and gone to all the trouble of hauling the stone up the hill to have towers as well. She could almost see the low door at the far end of the hall where she’d put on her old cloak and let herself out while everyone turned to admire the painted lanterns brought up from the guilds.

The moon hung high enough to clear the roofs when the first rocket went up. It broke in a red wheel and shook out sparks the green of apple skin. Catrin followed one red spark down, and when it faded her eye found another that went on travelling long after she thought it was bound to go out. She was smiling by the time it did. She’d brought the thick cloak so that when the gold fan went up at the end she could sit here and watch the whole of it, all the way to the last thread burning over the roofs.

The streets had their own light. There were thousands of lanterns, strung along the housefronts and gathered in little clumps where the lanes opened and families had brought their lights into the road. Down by the horse fair the lanterns swung one by one across the mouths of the alleys as people went home or found a better place to stand. Under Tallow Gate, a candle merchant had turned his emptied box on its side to set out cups for his friends. The lids came off the sweet sellers’ tins and a smell of hot ginger rose in the cold.

At the foot of the watchtower a woman with a white braid had stood a girl on the candlemaker’s bench to hang up a paper bird. She guided the girl’s hand past two iron hooks in the wall to a crooked third one, which she found as quickly as she might have found the latch on her own door. Catrin had seen a bird in that place other years, its wire tail bent so far to one side it seemed to be steering. It turned on the hook and made a little light, just big enough to put the same blue on both their faces.

Then the fiddler started up again. Catrin’s toes moved against the stone for the first three notes. Her mother had tapped the count on the inside of her wrist when they danced that evening, so lightly that anyone looking on would have missed it. She’d done the same when Catrin was little and learning the measure. Back then Catrin thought the company kept such beautiful time because they could all hear her mother’s taps. One year a lapdog ran out under Catrin’s skirt and she lifted a foot over it, and sixteen pairs of dancers did the same while the dog went peacefully to sleep on the queen’s train. Somebody might still be teaching it.

Below, the woman had just set the girl down when they took hands to dance. The tune was Catrin’s right up to the turn, where the fiddler drew his bow back and gave the note another beat. The girl turned as soon as the woman’s hand went up. One foot slipped out sideways and the woman took a great stamping step to catch up with her. Round she went too, with her free arm out, and the next pair had to go wide around them. Catrin heard the woman laugh. There was a catch at the end of it as if she’d remembered some reason to stop, and then the laugh came again, even louder, and a man who’d had to move his cup called out for them to do it twice. They did.

They passed under the arch and came out where Catrin could see them again, the girl still holding up her arm as if it were she who had given the turn to the whole street. On the nights when Catrin danced, people waited for the heir to show them when the last step was finished. Even a request for another tune could send a man all the way to the kitchens to find a fiddler who’d only just sat down. She had never quite got used to how quickly people came back and said how glad they were to have been asked. She wondered how many would put out a hand to the princess if they could just as well go off and do something else.

From this height she could see the open hand of a dancer who’d lost his partner, and the little sideways rush somebody made to join him. As Catrin watched them she found herself moving her own hand, trying to guess what she might have done in time to catch the girl who turned too soon. Two rockets went up on the far side of the castle. She heard them burst and kept watching the street until the girl disappeared among the bigger people. She wanted to have that hand put into hers, and to see what her feet would do when nobody in the line knew enough about her to follow them.

The street looked a very long way down as she drew her heels off the ledge. There would be time to come back for the gold. She put her shoes on and felt her way back to the stair.

* * *

The stair at Tallow Gate turned inside the thickness of the wall. For the last few steps Catrin could hear the drum perfectly and the fiddle hardly at all. She came out beside a woman warming both hands on a cup, into a press of people who had seen the same rockets she had. Their talk was all of how far the red had spread. A boy showed her with his arms, without taking any particular interest in whether she’d been looking. She had to turn sideways to let him pass.

She looked for the girl with the lantern. Down here there was a basket hung on a nail at just the height to catch a loose strand of Catrin’s hair, and the bench where the girl had hung the lantern was in use again. People perched along it with parcels on their knees. She could see the blue bird from below, with a tiny spot of light coming through where the paper had parted at a wing. Under it stood a girl about her own age, cutting the last of a sheet of gingerbread with the side of a spoon. A long black plait hung over one shoulder. The girl steadied one end of the board on a barrel and propped the other across Catrin’s forearm.

“Hold that end a minute, will you? The last one’s always got a corner missing. There’s always somebody who needs an extra mouthful from the first row.” She pushed till the piece broke and held out the one with the ragged edge. “Try that. The corners are where all the good of it goes. My sister likes a piece that’s square, and I can get her to think anything’s better if it has the right edges on it.”

The gingerbread was so dark it looked burnt. Catrin bit through the hard crust and found an unexpectedly soft middle that stuck to the roof of her mouth. The girl watched till she saw Catrin’s eyebrows go up. Then she took back the board and slid it onto the bench just as somebody near the arch called out for Bess to bring her feet if she was bringing them. Bess picked a crumb off her apron and put it in her mouth.

“Listen to him. He’s only got there first because he’s been next to the fiddle since supper.” She had got one apron string loose and was pulling at the other. “I thought I’d have a go at taking them round myself. You can get a good turn at the far end if they give you the room. My sister says I go too far out, and yet she’s had her go twice. I’d like her to see it done my way while she’s still got her eyes open. Could you dance in those shoes?”

Catrin glanced down. Below her cloak the ends of the buckles shone. She had been tapping a heel on a bit of raised paving.

“I think I could,” she said.

Bess took the apron off over her head and flung it onto the board, which turned out to be enough to make two or three people look up. “You were doing it while you ate. Here, leave that foot where it is. We’ll start with the other one.”

They joined the tail of the line. Bess’s hand was warm, with a grain of sugar in the fold of one knuckle. The fiddler was up on a shallow ledge opposite the stair and his bow caught the light from the lanterns as it went up and down. Catrin was so glad to hear that tune again that for the first few steps she forgot to look down. There was a great deal more ground to cover between two notes than there was in the hall. She stretched a leg to catch Bess at the next stone, and Bess, seeing she could keep up, took a bigger step herself. They grinned at one another. Then Bess lifted their hands, and Catrin turned in place where the court would have turned, on the note the fiddler was still holding.

Her toe caught on the edge of a sett. She flung out her hand and took hold of a man’s sleeve. By the time she had got round she had lost Bess’s hand and arrived at the precise position from which a lady at court would have dropped a curtsey. She very nearly dropped it. The man took his arm back, held his cup of ale up to see how much was left, and gave a low whistle.

“Come round here,” Bess said. “Len, you can stand where you like, but if you stand there you’ll be drinking your ale out of your collar next time.” She was laughing as she put out her hand again. Catrin could already see the place where their turn ought to have gone. Two more pairs went by before the fiddler drew out the note. The dancers kept their hands up through it. She could have done it now. She was sure she could.

“I’ve kept you long enough,” Catrin said. She gave Bess her hand as she said it. It was what she always did after she’d had somebody dance with her. Even people whose faces had grown quite pink from the trouble of keeping up stood where she’d left them with their hands raised, smiling and waiting for her to choose whether to do it again.

“Oh, all right.” Bess put Catrin’s hand down gently. She stepped past her to call to a girl coming the other way and was carried off with the line. Her sleeve disappeared behind somebody’s lantern, and then Catrin caught sight of the end of the plait, swinging away again.

Catrin walked back past the bench to the stair. Somebody had turned over the gingerbread paper, and the clean white side shone under the blue bird. The man whose sleeve she’d taken was telling his friends how near he’d come to a bath. At the mouth of the stair she put a hand on the stone and started up. There was time to get to the top and settle herself in her place. The next rocket went up with a sound like cloth tearing, and through the first slit in the wall she saw a little bit of white scatter and go out. It would be fine to have the sky whole again, with room to watch the gold go down over every separate roof. She could take those shoes off and stay as long as she liked.

Her fingers were still curved from having held out her hand. She flattened them on the wall. If Bess had called after her she could have gone back with a smile and said, oh, very well, if you’re quite sure. She’d said that so many times that she knew how lightly to touch the word *quite*. She could almost feel how easy it would be. Bess had believed her without the smallest difficulty. And Catrin had been walking away in those wretched shoes all that time waiting to be contradicted.

Someone below the stair began beating time on the bench while the fiddler tuned up. She could still taste the ginger at the back of her teeth. She thought of standing in front of Bess and asking plainly for one more turn. Bess might look her straight in the face and say she’d found somebody. It was extraordinary how much easier it would be to go all the way home and ask for an entire room to dance. She took her hand off the wall. There were only three steps back down to the place where she could be seen.

Bess was at the head of the line. A boy was holding his hand out to her while she folded her plait up against her neck and pinned it there. When Catrin came near, Bess pushed the pin home and gave Catrin her attention. Catrin had to look her in the eye for as long as it took to say the whole thing.

“I’d like to dance with you again. Will you?”

Bess looked down the line. A pair at the tail were still arguing over whose place they had taken. “I’ve got the front this time. My sister put those two there on purpose because they stop at every shop, and if she thinks I’ll wait while they get something to eat she can come down and lead them herself.” She turned back to Catrin and held out her hand. “Come on. I want to take it all the way round.”

The first steps went quickly, with the bow running close behind them. Bess called to her sister to put down her gingerbread and look, and a woman on the bench drew her feet up out of the way and leaned forward, chewing and smiling. Catrin could hear the last pairs setting off by the shuffle over the raised stone where she’d caught her shoe. She was close enough to Bess now to feel the way her arm lifted just before she took a long step, and once, when Catrin missed it, Bess waited a little and took a shorter one to get them even again.

The turn came at the bend in the gate passage, where the stone gathered up all the street noise. Bess raised her hand. The first of the gold went off so hard that Catrin felt it against her chest, and for two steps she could hear nothing of the fiddle. Her foot wanted to come down in the old place. She waited through the beat she’d heard from the tower, her arm loose enough for Bess to take the turn as far as she wanted. Bess went wide, almost to the wall. Catrin remembered the woman’s great stamping step and put a foot out after her. The pull on her arm surprised her. She found her footing and the weight of Bess’s hand brought her round.

Then they were out from under the arch. Catrin could see a few strands of the gold fan where it came down beyond the roofline. Their swing turned her face towards the old tower just as it lit up, right to the hollow at the edge of the battlements where she’d sat. Bess’s laugh came warm against her ear. The pairs behind them came out one after another and caught the light, and when Bess turned again Catrin raised their hands and went with her.
