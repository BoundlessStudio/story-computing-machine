# A Turn at Tallow Gate

At the top of Tallow Gate’s watchtower, Catrin stepped out of her shoes. She climbed into a gap in the battlements and rested her heels on the ledge, on grey stone the weather had hollowed out at the rim. The cold went straight through her stockings. At the palace she had made it to the end of the dance before admitting the shoes were too small, and the knowledge had been an agreeable secret all the way down through the streets of Vendrel. Now she could spread her toes.

Across Vendrel the towers of her family’s castle rose clear of the other roofs. Every year for the Festival of Lights they set lanterns in the open galleries and in the narrow windows of the round tower, right up under the silver weathercock. The castle was more beautiful from here than it was from anywhere inside it. At the far end of the hall was the low door where she’d put on her old cloak and let herself out while the coppersmiths showed the company a lantern in the shape of a pike.

The moon hung high enough to clear the roofs when the first rocket went up. It broke in a red wheel and shook out sparks the green of apple skin. Catrin followed one red spark down, and when it faded her eye found another that went on travelling long after she thought it was bound to go out. She was smiling by the time it did. She’d brought the thick cloak so that when the gold fan went up at the end she could sit here and watch the whole of it, all the way to the last thread burning over the roofs.

Thousands of lanterns lined the housefronts and gathered in little clumps where the lanes opened and families had brought their lights into the road. Down by the horse fair the lanterns swung one by one across the mouths of the alleys as people went home or found a better place to stand. Beside the arch, a candle merchant had turned his emptied box on its side to set out cups for his friends. The lids came off the sweet sellers’ tins and a smell of hot ginger rose in the cold.

At the foot of the watchtower a woman with a white braid had stood a little girl on the candlemaker’s bench to hang up a blue lantern in the shape of a bird. She guided the child’s hand past two iron hooks in the wall to a crooked third one, which she found as quickly as she might have found the latch on her own door. Catrin had seen the bird there other years, its wire tail bent at the same angle under the new paper. It turned on the hook and made a little light, just big enough to put the same blue on both their faces.

Then the fiddler started up again. Catrin’s toes moved against the stone for the first three notes. Her mother had tapped the count on the inside of her wrist when they danced that evening, so lightly that anyone looking on would have missed it. She’d done the same when Catrin was little and learning the measure. Back then Catrin thought the company kept such beautiful time because they could all hear her mother’s taps. One year a lapdog ran out under Catrin’s skirt and she lifted a foot over it. By the time the dog went peacefully to sleep on the queen’s train, sixteen pairs of dancers had added the step to the measure. Somebody might still be teaching it.

Below, the woman set the child down and took her hand for the dance. At the palace everyone turned as soon as the fiddler struck the long note. When it came, the woman lifted their hands and stood ready for the end of the note. The child was already under her arm. One small foot slid out sideways and the woman took a great stamping step to catch up with her. Round she went too, with her free arm out, and the next pair had to go wide around them. Catrin heard the woman laugh. There was a catch at the end of it as if she’d remembered some reason to stop, and then the laugh came again, even louder, and a man who’d had to move his cup called out for them to do it twice. They did.

They passed under the arch and came out where Catrin could see them again, the child still holding up her arm as if it were she who had given the turn to the whole street. At the palace that evening Catrin had told a woman pink from the effort of keeping up to go and get her breath. The woman had stood smiling, her hand still out, waiting for the heir to put hers down.

From this height she could see the open hand of a dancer who’d lost his partner, and the little sideways rush somebody made to join him. As Catrin watched them she found herself moving her own hand, trying to guess what she might have done in time to catch the child who turned too soon. Two rockets went up on the far side of the castle. She heard them burst and kept watching the street until the child disappeared among the bigger people. She wanted to have that hand put into hers, and to see what her feet would do when nobody in the line knew enough about her to follow them.

The street looked a very long way down as she drew her heels off the ledge. There would be time to come back for the gold. She put her shoes on and felt her way back to the stair.

* * *

The stair at Tallow Gate turned inside the thickness of the wall. For the last few steps Catrin could hear the drum perfectly and the fiddle hardly at all. She came out beside a woman warming both hands on a cup, into a press of people who had seen the same rockets she had. Their talk was all of how far the red had spread. A boy showed her with his arms, without taking any particular interest in whether she’d been looking. She had to turn sideways to let him pass.

The child had gone. Catrin found the candlemaker’s bench again by the blue bird hanging above it. From below she could see a dark rib of wire in the lantern where a patch on its wing had come loose. People perched along the bench with parcels on their knees. At one end a gingerbread seller about Catrin’s age was dividing what was left on her board with the side of a spoon, her long black plait over one shoulder. She steadied one end of the board on a barrel and propped the other across Catrin’s forearm.

“Hold that end a minute, will you? The last one’s always got a corner missing. There’s always somebody who needs an extra mouthful from the first row.” She pushed till the piece broke and held out the one with the ragged edge. “Try that. The corners are where all the good of it goes. My sister likes a piece that’s square, and I can get her to think anything’s better if it has the right edges on it.”

The gingerbread was so dark it looked burnt. Catrin bit through the hard crust and found an unexpectedly soft middle that stuck to the roof of her mouth. The girl watched till she saw Catrin’s eyebrows go up. Then she slid the board onto the bench, where a woman reached past her for the square piece without looking up. Somebody near the arch called out for Bess to bring her feet if she was bringing them. Bess picked a crumb off her apron and put it in her mouth.

“Listen to him. He’s only got there first because he’s been next to the fiddle since supper.” She had got one apron string loose and was pulling at the other. “I thought I’d have a go at taking them round myself. You can get a good turn at the far end if they give you the room. My sister says I go too far out, and yet she’s had her go twice. I’d like her to see it done my way while she’s still got her eyes open. Could you dance in those shoes?”

Catrin glanced down. She had been tapping the toe of one thin court shoe on a bit of raised paving.

“I think I could,” she said.

Bess took the apron off over her head and flung it onto the board. “You were doing it while you ate. Here, leave that foot where it is. We’ll start with the other one.”

They joined the line. Bess’s hand was warm, with a grain of sugar in the fold of one knuckle. Catrin took a long step to catch up and Bess, feeling her do it, took a longer one herself. They grinned at one another. The fiddler struck the long note and Catrin was already turning. Her toe caught on the edge of a sett. She flung out a hand and got hold of a man’s sleeve. By the time she got round she was in just the position to drop a curtsey. She very nearly dropped it. The man held his cup of ale up to see how much was left.

“Come round here,” Bess said. “Len, you can stand where you like, but if you stand there you’ll be drinking your ale out of your collar next time.” She was laughing as she put out her hand again.

“I’ve kept you long enough,” Catrin said. She gave Bess her hand as she said it.

“Oh, all right.” Bess put Catrin’s hand down gently. She stepped past her to call to a girl coming the other way and was carried off with the line. Her sleeve disappeared behind somebody’s lantern, and then Catrin caught sight of the end of the plait, swinging away again.

Catrin walked back past the bench to the stair. Somebody had turned over the gingerbread paper, and the clean white side shone under the blue bird. The man whose sleeve she’d taken was telling his friends how near he’d come to a bath. At the mouth of the stair she put a hand on the stone and started up. There was time to get to the top and settle herself in her place. The next rocket went up with a sound like cloth tearing, and through the first slit in the wall she saw a little bit of white scatter and go out. It would be fine to have the sky whole again, with room to watch the gold go down over every separate roof. She could take those shoes off and stay as long as she liked.

Her fingers were still curved from having held out her hand. She flattened them on the wall. If Bess had called after her she could have gone back with a smile and said, oh, very well, if you’re quite sure. She’d said that so many times that she knew how lightly to touch the word *quite*. She could almost feel how easy it would be. Bess had believed her without the smallest difficulty. And Catrin had been walking away in those wretched shoes all that time waiting to be contradicted.

Someone below the stair began beating time on the bench while the fiddler tuned up. She could still taste the ginger at the back of her teeth. She thought of standing in front of Bess and asking plainly for one more turn. Bess might look her straight in the face and say she’d found somebody. It was extraordinary how much easier it would be to go all the way home and ask for an entire room to dance. She took her hand off the wall. There were only three steps back down to the place where she could be seen.

Bess was at the head of the line. A boy was holding his hand out to her while she folded her plait up against her neck and pinned it there. When Catrin came near, Bess pushed the pin home and gave Catrin her attention. Catrin had to look her in the eye for as long as it took to say the whole thing.

“I’d like to dance with you again. Will you?”

Bess glanced at the boy’s hand, then at his face. Catrin felt her own hands hanging and made herself leave them there.

“Find me for the next one, will you?” Bess said to the boy. Then she held out her hand to Catrin. “I’ve got the front this time. If my sister wants to say I go too wide, she might as well have something to point at.”

The first steps went quickly. Bess called to her sister to put down her gingerbread and look, and the woman on the bench drew her feet up out of the way and leaned forward, chewing and smiling. Catrin felt Bess’s arm lift just before she took a long step. Once, when Catrin missed it, Bess waited and took a shorter one to get them even again.

At the bend in the gate passage, Bess’s arm lifted. Catrin heard the start of the long note and then the gold went off, so hard she felt it against her chest. The fiddle was gone. Her foot wanted to come down in the old place. Through the sole of the shoe she stood on she could feel a groove in the stone. She kept her other heel up until the note would have ended. Then Bess turned under their hands.

Bess went wide. The pull on Catrin’s arm surprised her. The place where she would have to put her foot seemed a long way off. She remembered the old woman’s boot coming down hard on the stones. Catrin took that same step and felt the strain go out of Bess’s fingers. They could go farther. The music came back to her from the other side of the stone.

As they reached the mouth of the gate, gold threads hung above the roof tiles opposite. Catrin tipped her head back for the rest of the fan. The arch cut off the view. She was still trying to see when their swing took her out from under the stone and turned her face towards the old tower, lit right up to the hollow at the edge of the battlements where she’d sat. Bess’s laugh came warm against her ear. The pairs behind them came out one after another and caught the light, and when Bess turned again Catrin raised their hands and went with her.
