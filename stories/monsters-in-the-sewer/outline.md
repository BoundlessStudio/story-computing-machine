# Visitors Must Knock

## Promised experience

The explicit request puts an entire kobold clan in the modern era by unexplained teleportation and makes “there are monsters in the sewer” literally true a couple of years later. The distinctive inferred pleasure is discovering what a displaced fantasy clan has made of a modern undercity: recognizably kobold ingenuity, family life, acquisitiveness, and danger encountering contemporary habits and technology. A wry adventure seems invited by the collision and the final phrase, but humor, a sympathetic viewpoint, a particular relationship, and any scale of conflict are writer choices, not requirements. No central relationship, teleportation mechanism, moral, or ending is specified. The anticipated payoff is a sewer encounter whose course depends on both the whole clan and its two years of adaptation.

## Provisional direction

About 3,000–4,000 words, close third through **Tikka**, an adult kobold scout and trap-maker. The clan numbers eighty-seven, from small hatchlings to elders. Its chief, **Grandmother Vetch**, is Tikka’s grandmother; their affectionate argument concerns whether the clan can keep surviving by remaining a rumor. These names, kinship, and tensions are inventions. Nobody can reopen the one unexplained crossing, and the story requires no continuing spellcasting.

The clan lives in dry disused galleries off a working sewer beneath an unnamed contemporary city. It has spent two years learning the city from below: collecting discarded devices, trading small valuables aboveground, learning human language and habits, and rebuilding a communal home. Its old traps survive beside wired doorbells, bicycle parts, phones, and borrowed movie dialogue. Neither period is simply superior: a camera defeats darkness, while a human-shaped capture plan misunderstands an entire clan that can climb, squeeze, cooperate, and think.

A private animal-capture crew, following a viral sewer video, descends with small camera drones, nets, and empty specimen crates. It intends to take living kobolds to a paying collector. The clan must choose between another flight that scatters it and a defense that makes its existence undeniable. The dramatic center remains the kobolds’ collective choices and cleverness, with Tikka coordinating rather than a human granting their rescue or legitimacy. The ending rewards their continued possession of a strange, lived-in home and their decision to be openly troublesome neighbors.

Possible scenes and turns:

- Open two years after arrival, with Tikka retrieving a phone dropped through a grate while a human above tells a companion that there are monsters in the sewer. She knows enough modern speech to object to what the phone’s camera is being shown. The intrusion exposes an entrance, turning the joke into a threat.
- Follow her through the clan’s transformed lair: an elder hoards rechargeable batteries like gems; hatchlings repeat a cartoon’s heroic threats; the communal banner hangs above a feast cooked with bought food. Brief, concrete memories contrast the entire clan’s bewildered arrival with what these two years have enabled. The family is a community with conflicting appetites, not eighty-six extras behind a clever protagonist.
- The capture crew arrives. Its drone sees through the clan’s familiar hiding places and its long poles bypass an old pit trap. The crew’s cages and talk of separate buyers make scattering the clan a concrete danger. Vetch initially orders retreat, then discovers the rear exit is watched: concealment and flight cannot preserve their home this time.
- Tikka recognizes that the crew has brought a live camera connection to the surface. She proposes using the intruders’ own broadcast, but the clan supplies and changes the plan. Their counterattack uses old tunnel knowledge and two years’ worth of new toys: misdirected recorded voices, lights and decoys, a bicycle-powered moving screen, overhead nets, and more small attackers than the crew’s camera can follow. Keep the action legible and the danger non-graphic; the victory must be earned through the clan’s specific capabilities rather than miraculous hacking.
- The crew is caught; the clan takes its still-broadcasting camera. Instead of hiding from it, Vetch calls the household into view, and Tikka shows the cages, their intended cargo, and the captors. Eighty-seven living kobolds, their home, and their victory become impossible to edit into one blurry beast sighting. This is their choice to trade secrecy for witnesses, not an instant guarantee of safety or universal acceptance.
- End on the changed next evening. The clan has not become human or taken up sewer maintenance. It has made its entrance conspicuous, moved its defenses, and posted a hand-painted warning: **MONSTERS IN SEWER. VISITORS MUST KNOCK.** A cautious knock comes. Tikka checks the visitor through a repurposed doorbell camera, teeth bright with anticipation, while the whole noisy household debates whether to open. Their arrival is past; their future in this city has begun.

The prose may discover a better route through these choices. Protect the clan’s agency, the meaningful two-year transformation, the literal sewer-monster encounter, and the comic bite of the final invitation.
