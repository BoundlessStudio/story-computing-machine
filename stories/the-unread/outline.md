# The Unread

The request explicitly promises a genuinely perfect fortune-teller of twenty years, an afternoon reading for a young stranger that blanks her entire enchanted deck, and terrified whispers showing that Fate cannot look upon this person without a destiny. I infer a pleasure of supernatural dread and discovery: a practiced expert meets something that frightens the source of her authority. The request leaves the stranger's nature, relationship to the teller, scale, and ending open. My chosen payoff is a dangerous encounter with the kingdom's living Fate in which the blankness changes events, rather than merely teaching the teller to accept uncertainty.

Provisional direction: close third through Meret, the fortune-teller. The supplied woman's purple headscarf and shawl, brown hair, white blouse, gold jewelry, and violet-and-gold cards inform her appearance. She has earned her reputation; neither she nor her predictions have been fraudulent. Orrel, a young adult stranger, comes seeking the way to the royal Hall of Lots. They want to confront the being that has foretold the kingdom's next war and made a virtue of its inevitability. Orrel has no assigned role in that war, or anywhere else. They can be hurt and can choose badly; their absence from Fate's sight does not make them a passive key or an invulnerable weapon.

- Open with Meret completing an ordinary reading whose exact details begin to occur before the client has left. Orrel sits down that afternoon. Every card goes white, including its back; the little voices she has never heard agree on anything beg her to close the deck. They are transmitting the fear of living intelligences, not independently originating magic. One whispers that Fate is looking everywhere except at the chair.
- Meret tests the boundary rather than accepting Orrel's explanation. A future she can still see for someone across the square becomes unreadable when Orrel intervenes in it. The predicted danger remains physically real, but Orrel's unscripted action changes its outcome. Meret recognizes both the danger and the opportunity. Her old fulfilled predictions remain true; the stranger has introduced a new cause she cannot forecast.
- Orrel asks her to bring them to the Hall, where the royal reader can address Fate directly on the eve of a foretold war. They have sought other readers, all of whom treated the blankness as a threat to be driven away. Meret's particular skill matters: she knows how to distinguish a card's literal event from its apparent meaning, and how to conduct the dangerous audience in which Fate answers through the full deck. The Hall is an encounter chamber, not a stockpile whose destruction would cancel the future.
- Fate begins speaking through the cards while refusing to see Orrel. It offers Meret an exact, peaceful future if she abandons them, then presses the court to separate the pair. Meret and Orrel enter a ceremony that the teller knows in terrible detail: the king will accept the promised victory, and its precisely counted deaths. Her knowledge gives them a route through the court's predictable responses; Orrel's interventions make that route increasingly unreadable. The deck's panic grows as the young stranger approaches the place where its living source must appear.
- In the Hall, Meret learns that Fate cannot guarantee its own survival in an encounter with Orrel. It cannot assign them an ending and therefore cannot fix the outcome of a struggle with them. Orrel's intention is not to seize the privilege of choosing everyone else's lives. They mean to end the being's claim to give this kingdom one necessary future. Meret must use the audience to bring Fate into their present rather than let it remain a safe voice behind the cards. She knowingly spends the certainty that has protected her and the crown for twenty years; Orrel chooses to stay when escape is still possible.
- The encounter has a physical consequence: Fate is forced to face the person it has refused to see, and its mastery of the kingdom's futures breaks. The final action and precise cost remain open to discovery in prose, but Orrel must do something decisive and Meret's expertise must materially enable it. The promised war is left a human decision, with armies and grievances still present. End after a consequential first refusal of its script, with the surviving cards attempting a reading and finding that they, too, have become unread. Keep the awe and unease of what has been released; do not close with a moral speech or a routine job newly done without magic.
