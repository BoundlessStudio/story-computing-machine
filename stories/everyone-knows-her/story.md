# All the Ways They Knew Her

At the northern barricade, Maris Calder set all eight wheels on the old park road and made herself the same promise she made every summer: no foot on the pavement until the southern gate.

The promise had been harder when she was younger and believed difficulty required witnesses. Now it was private, which made it better. She bent her knees, pushed twice, and entered the festival between a rack of paper parasols and a man walking backward with three lemonades.

The road was smooth where bus tires polished it all year and gorgeous when there were no buses. For one afternoon it belonged to strollers, dancers, wandering eaters, children with painted faces, dogs alarmed by drums, and Maris. She knew how each of them moved. Small children launched sideways without warning. Couples joined at the hand had the turning radius of gates. A person carrying food would protect the food before protecting any other part of the body. Dogs telegraphed; teenagers did not.

She did not need people to notice her. She needed them to continue being themselves for the half second it took her to pass.

A trumpet opened a bright hole in the noise. Smoke from the grills made the sunlight visible. Maris coasted behind a wagon, counted the sway of two shopping bags, and slipped through just before they knocked together. Her left skate hissed over a paper napkin. Her right missed a dropped fork by the width of a wheel.

This was the pleasure she returned for. Not solitude exactly. Solitude was available on the paths before breakfast, with beetles ticking in the weeds and nobody selling anything fried. Here she could be among thousands of people without any one encounter having to hold. Every body offered a problem with an immediate, courteous solution. She read the gap, passed through, and left no debt behind.

“There she is,” a woman called from a drink stall.

Maris lifted two fingers without looking over. The woman could have meant anybody. There were hundreds of people to be there.

“Happy festival!” the woman added, more distinctly.

Maris answered, “You too,” because the words fit through a smaller gap than a conversation.

She crossed the first block with the lazy confidence of someone on a familiar floor. The stall positions changed each year, but the crowd kept its old tides: outward along the curbs, inward toward music, sudden dams wherever free samples appeared. She took the middle until a cluster of toddlers forced her toward the shade. Two teenagers there saw her and struck identical poses, one arm extended as if presenting a prize.

“It’s you,” one said.

The other waved both hands.

Maris glanced behind her. A dancer in silver streamers was coming up the road on stilts, tall enough to deserve anyone’s excitement. Maris pointed a thumb that way.

The teenagers laughed. One held up a folded blue booklet, but Maris was already past, listening to the clicks behind her as people raised their phones for the dancer.

At the dumpling stand, a man carrying a paper tray stepped left before Maris signaled. She went right to avoid him. He went right too, smiling in apology, then planted himself against the counter and swept one arm toward the open road.

“After you,” he said.

She carved past. That could still be ordinary. A good skater was visible in a crowd. The knee pads and wrist guards might suggest competence even if the scuffed boots did not. People sometimes gave her too much room. She preferred that to too little.

Then a woman with a sunhat tucked her elbow in before Maris had chosen a side. A family closed around its wagon. Two men carrying a rolled rug stopped across from each other and waited. Ahead of Maris, space opened in little sections, not quite a lane but the rumor of one.

Faces began to turn.

Maris slowed without letting either skate stop. Her body disliked the change before her mind found a reason for it. Slow crowds were harder than fast streets. At speed, decisions expired behind her. At walking pace, every glance remained available for inspection.

A man near the puppet booth said, “That’s the skater from the program.”

“Where?” someone asked.

Maris pushed hard. A string of festival flags flickered overhead. The drums behind her lost a contest with a fiddle ahead. On both sides, people carried the same blue booklet: folded into back pockets, curled around cups, used as fans. She had seen them at the barricade in volunteers’ hands and nowhere else because she had not been looking. Now they seemed to flash on every block.

She considered turning onto the grass, taking the wide path behind the stalls, and recovering the road farther down. It would keep her moving. It would also be cheating. The route was the road, northern barricade to southern gate. Fourteen summers. She had made it through a thunderstorm, a loose frame bolt, and one parade so dense she spent ten minutes rolling in place behind a papier-mâché fish.

Whatever this was, it would have to make room.

She ducked under a balloon string. A food courier wheeled a bicycle across her line; she tapped one brake, pivoted, and missed the rear tire. Someone cheered. More people joined in, a scattered clap that traveled ten yards and died of embarrassment.

Maris’s face went hot.

“What program?” she called, but the courier thought she was angry with him and shouted an apology. By the time she looked back, the people who had clapped were studying their food.

At the next crossing, a little girl stood in front of her with a blue booklet open against her chest.

The girl’s father caught her by the shoulders. “Don’t block the road.”

“But look.” The girl raised the booklet beside Maris as if checking the color of paint against a wall. “It is her.”

Maris swerved onto the crosswalk, circled the white painted figure there, and came back without putting a brake down. The father had drawn the girl to the curb. He looked mortified. The girl looked exactingly pleased.

“May I see that?” Maris asked.

The booklet had been folded backward. Before the girl could turn it over, three women in matching aprons called to the father from a stall. He answered them; the girl answered him; a stroller filled the space between Maris and all of them. Maris caught a glimpse of dark wheels printed against blue and then the crowd moved the booklet away.

“The photo exhibit,” the father said over his shoulder. “Halfway down.”

She was already three body-lengths past him.

Halfway down was where the road widened around an old traffic island. Maris knew it by the rough patch that tugged at her left skate and by the five seconds of shade cast by the trees. This year a canvas awning occupied the curbside lane. Blue arrows pointed toward it. COMMUNITY PHOTOS, they said, the only words she managed to read before momentum carried her beyond the entrance.

She could continue. She could reach the southern gate, take off her skates, then walk back and ask a complete question like a person with shoes on. The promise had rules. The promise did not say she had to be ignorant.

Behind her, somebody said, “She never stops.”

Maris made a wide turn around a planter and skated against the current.

It was her first circle back in fourteen years.

She approached the awning at a crawl. Interlocking mats covered the sidewalk beneath it, their edges curled by heat. She braked on the road and caught the center pole with one hand.

Her face, nearly three feet high, looked back from the first display panel.

It was an excellent photograph. Last summer’s sun had found the damp at her temples and a loose dark curl behind her ear. Her head was turned toward the route ahead; her mouth held the beginning of a grin. The photographer had caught both feet off the ground in the brief lightness between strides. Behind the portrait, reduced to pale overlapping squares, were other versions of Maris: red helmet, no helmet, short hair, long braid, old gray skates, current black ones. A dozen partial bodies crossed through one another while the large face stayed sharp.

The same design filled the program cover stacked at the entrance.

Across the panel, white type read: THE RIDER WHO NEVER STOPS.

Someone behind a table said, “Oh.”

Maris turned. The speaker wore a volunteer badge, reading glasses on a cord, and the expression of someone who had just seen weather arrive through an open window.

“Is that you?” the volunteer asked.

“You printed my face on a few thousand things,” Maris said. “You tell me.”

The volunteer set down a roll of removable tape. “Yes. You’re right. Sorry. I knew that was a foolish question.”

Outside the awning, people passed with the programs. Most looked from the cover to Maris and tried to disguise the second look as interest in the display. A boy pointed openly until the adult with him lowered his hand.

Maris held the pole harder. The metal was warm and slightly sticky. Her wheels wanted to roll backward on the road’s crown.

“Who took it?” she asked.

“The large one? A person watching the dance demonstration last year. You crossed behind the last row. We can pull the submission form.”

“And the others?”

“Many people.”

The volunteer came around the table but stopped several feet away. Maris noticed the stopped distance and resented being grateful for it.

“The exhibit is about recurring details,” the volunteer said. “Things that turn up in photographs over the years. That striped umbrella has been here since the first festival. There’s a terrier in seven different families’ pictures. We found you in fourteen summers.”

“You found me.”

“In the pictures. We didn’t know who you were.”

“That distinction feels important to you.”

The volunteer’s mouth tightened. “It did yesterday.”

That answer was inconveniently better than an argument.

Maris looked again at the panel. Fourteen summers was correct. She had first skated the road because a friend had canceled and she had refused to waste a day of perfect pavement. The next year she came alone on purpose. She had thought the repetition belonged wholly to her, a line laid down and erased each time by traffic the following morning.

“How long have you been collecting these?”

“Submissions opened in winter. Once we noticed the pattern, we put requests online and in the neighborhood bulletin. We asked skate groups. Nobody gave us a name.”

Maris had never joined a skate group. Her accounts were private and mostly abandoned. She spent the rest of the year moving through different streets in shoes.

“So you couldn’t ask me.”

“We tried to reach you,” the volunteer said.

“You tried to learn who I was. That isn’t the same task.”

The volunteer looked toward the large photograph, then down at the programs. “No. It isn’t.”

A pair of visitors entered the awning and halted when they saw Maris. One whispered, “Oh, wow,” with the delight of finding an actor still standing beside the costume. The volunteer moved between them and the display.

“We’re closing this section for an adjustment,” the volunteer said. “The other wall is still open.”

They went, reluctantly, to photographs of handmade boats and family picnics. One looked back twice.

“I want to see what you used,” Maris said.

The volunteer slid a plastic portfolio from beneath the table. “Would you rather sit?”

“No.”

Standing still on skates was more work than moving. Maris shifted her weight in tiny increments while the volunteer opened the portfolio to contact sheets. The first page held photographs from four households. Not one had been taken of her.

In one, two children chased soap bubbles beside a fountain; the edge of Maris’s red helmet appeared above a woman’s shoulder. In another, an elderly couple toasted paper cups at a picnic table. Maris was a blur behind them, recognizable only by the old gray skates and the orange laces she had forgotten owning. A third showed a tower of sugared pastries, a fourth a dog staring with grave suspicion at a brass band. Maris crossed the farthest corner of each.

The volunteer turned the page.

Different cameras, different years, different reasons to press the button. A baby held up toward a puppet. Three friends showing their painted cheeks. A marriage proposal that Maris remembered because the answer had been shouted so loudly she nearly hit a trash bin. She appeared in a sleeve, a bent knee, a dark comma of hair. Then, as phones improved, she sharpened. She grew older by degrees the pictures had no reason to care about.

Anger had been easier when she imagined one patient stranger waiting every summer with a lens. There would have been a person to tell off, a simple shape to the wrong. The contact sheets offered families, accidents, celebrations, and no gaze that followed her farther than a fraction of a second.

“They weren’t watching me,” she said.

“No.”

“You were.”

The volunteer rested one hand on the portfolio. “Once we saw the pattern, yes.”

On the display, the repeated fragments had been cropped, brightened, and arranged to move in the same direction. Whole afternoons had become evidence for a single sentence. Maris examined the caption cards tucked into the portfolio sleeves. The anonymous skater crosses the festival without stopping, one said. Through weather, changing crowds, and passing years, she remains in motion.

“Where did this part come from?” Maris tapped the sleeve. “Without stopping.”

“The photographs seemed to show it.”

“Photographs seem to show whatever happened for a hundredth of a second.”

She turned the next page herself.

There she was sitting on a curb, eating something from a paper tray with both skates stretched into the gutter. She remembered the burned roof of her mouth, though not what she had eaten. Another photograph caught her crouched over a wheel with a hex key. She remembered that exactly: the soft grind that warned of a loose axle, the fear that the bolt had stripped, the relief when it tightened.

In a wide family picture, everyone faced the camera except a small boy on the road and Maris several yards away. The boy had fallen. Maris was stopped with her palms out, waiting to see whether he would cry or spring up furious. She could not remember his face. The picture had preserved only the back of his green shirt.

“This one was cropped here,” she said.

The volunteer followed her finger. In the exhibit version, the boy was gone. Maris leaned forward, weight pitched into apparent speed.

“We were trying to keep the sequence clean,” the volunteer said. The words emerged automatically, then seemed to sour in the air. “I hear it.”

The next image showed Maris laughing on one skate while the other rested on a bench. She studied the hand of somebody outside the frame, holding her front wheel. No face, no clue. She did not remember the repair or the joke. For several seconds she wanted the missing memory with an intensity that embarrassed her.

“Can I have copies?” she asked.

“We need to ask the contributors.”

“Of photographs of me.”

“Of their photographs,” the volunteer said, quiet but not apologetic. “Some include their children. I can give you contact information where they agreed to share it, and I can ask the rest. I won’t promise for them.”

Maris looked down at the children in the photographs. She disliked the answer and believed it.

A gust bellied the awning. The big panel flexed, making her printed smile buckle and return.

“All right,” Maris said. “Here is what you can do now. Stop handing out that cover. Cover these panels. Give me the source list and the captions, with no form saying I approve any of this.”

The volunteer nodded after each item. “We have plain schedules at the information tent. I can have those brought over. The programs already distributed—”

“I know you can’t pull paper out of people’s hands.”

“And the family photographs can stay if we cover your portions?”

Maris looked at the soap-bubble children, the suspicious dog, the proposal. “Today. Until you have asked me about anything that makes me the subject.”

The volunteer did not call it a compromise or thank her for understanding. She picked up a radio and said, “Stop distributing the blue covers. Use the schedule inserts only. Bring the blank rolls from storage, please.” Then she took the tape she had set down earlier and began measuring brown packing paper across the large portrait.

The first sheet sagged. Maris caught the lower corner before it stuck to the display.

“Higher,” she said. “The tape will peel when the canvas heats up.”

The volunteer glanced at her wrist guards. “You have experience mounting exhibits?”

“No. I have experience falling because someone thought tape could do more than tape can.”

For the first time, the volunteer smiled. It was brief and tired. She found clips under the table.

They worked from the outside inward. On the family photographs, the volunteer folded paper masks only over Maris, leaving the actual subjects visible. The result was strange: brown slivers at the margins, rectangles hovering in open road, one neat patch where a helmet had been. It did not erase her so much as announce where she had been removed.

People kept approaching. Some accepted the explanation that the section had closed. One complained that it was the reason she had come to the exhibit. Another asked Maris if she would pose beside the cover before it disappeared. Maris said no. A man studied the packing paper, shrugged, and went to look at the terrier series.

Outside, people kept eating, playing, pointing, and moving south.

When the last large panel was covered, Maris checked the road. She could still glide to the southern gate without putting a foot on pavement, but the uninterrupted run was gone. She had held the center pole. She had circled back. Finishing now would mean only that she had obeyed the narrowest wording of the rule. She was suddenly uninterested in winning against herself on a technicality.

Behind the table, the mats were too soft and uneven for skates. She sat in the volunteer’s folding chair and loosened her laces. Her socks were damp. The air against her ankles felt indecently cool.

The volunteer returned with a thin stack of schedules, contact permissions, and a release form clipped on top. “The standard packet,” she said. “You don’t have to sign it.”

Maris read the first paragraph. It granted permission for her likeness to be displayed in future festival materials. She took the volunteer’s pen, crossed out the paragraph, and drew a line through the signature box. In the margin she wrote her name, Maris Calder, and beneath it the one place the archive could contact her.

Then she pulled the first caption sheet closer.

Outside, someone passing the awning said, “Wasn’t that the skater?” Someone else answered that they thought so. Maris turned to the photograph of the fallen boy in the green shirt and wrote beside its caption: Stopped here. Ask the family what happened next.
