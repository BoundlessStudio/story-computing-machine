# The Other Majority

The explicit promise is individual immortality apparently awarded through a nationwide popular vote, with a substantially more complicated truth underneath that public belief. I infer the pleasure of a political mystery whose revelation changes both the election and the meaning of survival. The intended tone is urgent, wry, and emotionally exposed; the scale is national, experienced through a dying candidate and the wife she has mourned for twenty-three years. Their disagreement must remain real after their reunion. The payoff is an electorate becoming visible and a protagonist risking the safety of people she has only just discovered, including her own eventual continuation, to end the lie. Neither a conspiracy, a particular technical mechanism, nor a happy ending was prescribed.

Provisional form: close third person with Isabet Orris, a famous, difficult concert pianist with a degenerative illness. Enough room for scenes, a negotiated physical escape, and an actual political aftermath. No fixed word count or connection to an established story is needed.

- Isabet's campaign photographs station returns: its independent count gives her a narrow nationwide majority. She wants the immortality treatment for more life, music, and bodily freedom, without a public-service justification. The electoral commissioner tells her she has lost. A concealed precinct outvotes her margin; its rolls include her wife, Maret Tal. Isabet refuses tomorrow's concession interview until she sees the ballots.
- Beneath the election hall she finds a physical computational habitat. Neural meshes, advertised as trauma protection, keep external recovery profiles supposedly deleted at certified death. The profiles were secretly continued instead. This violated consent; the people created nevertheless have lives and rights. Individual biological regeneration is genuinely possible and scarce. The election allocates that treatment, including possible return to a body for an archived candidate. By secret administrative custom, all citizens retain the vote after death. Officials calculate the combined result and publish false totals normalized to an apparent living turnout. Isabet's receipts expose that fraud. The archive receives public broadcasts but has no outgoing connection except its constrained ballot channel.
- Maret has twenty-three years Isabet knows nothing about, friends and political work, and voted for the rival physician who promised more archive processing capacity. Citizens endure reduced subjective time while the living are sold an exclusive miracle. Maret wants Isabet embodied but will not subordinate millions to their marriage. The new truth also faces Isabet toward her own mesh: this unchosen continuation will be her future if her body fails, though she cannot yet call that a cure.
- Maret opposes both permanent secrecy and disclosure without protection. The archive's representatives debate opening the country; some prefer the old safety and political dominance. Isabet possesses leverage the archive lacks: photographed evidence, an uncancelled national broadcast, and enough living supporters to negotiate independent custodians. With the rival physician's help, she recruits hospitals whose ordinary recovery vaults can preserve suspended mind states with little power, although running everyone remains costly. An archive vote authorizes voluntary transfers to several independent hospitals before disclosure. The commissioner must grant access or face immediate proof of false counts. Maret joins the first transfer and wakes at a hospital terminal. Others elect to remain in the original habitat; Isabet cannot choose their risk for them.
- The concession broadcast becomes a joint address from multiple physical sites. People answer the living, including intimate and politically inconvenient replies. Isabet admits her genuine defeat while exposing the fraud; Maret describes both archive life and its injustice. A central-site power cut interrupts one feed; the independent hospital feed continues. Redundant custody prevents a single disconnection from erasing consenting citizens, rather than evidence or a speech magically protecting them. The dead have consented to real danger, many to suspension, and Isabet is risking the habitat she may soon need. Public recognition and family access remain contested.
- Compress the aftermath around one concrete result: an open combined rerun, independently observed, which Isabet loses again. The rival accepts bodily treatment while negotiations over representation and public funding continue; many archive citizens remain suspended. Isabet has not sacrificed a treatment she never won. She has used her loss and public leverage to make secret citizens visible. At a two-way concert between the hall and the archive, her failing hands force her to stop. Maret refuses to pretend bodily loss is nothing. Their next conversation is something they can plan, while no conversation can give Isabet what the vote withheld. The vote still decides who receives bodily immortality; it no longer decides whom the country admits is alive.

Risks to test in prose: keep the physical transfer understandable and consequential without making this a technical lecture; make Maret's intervening life and political independence substantial; preserve dissent and incomplete political settlement; preserve Isabet's bodily desire without using reunion to erase death or disability.
