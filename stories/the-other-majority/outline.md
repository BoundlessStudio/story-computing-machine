# The Other Majority

The explicit promise is individual immortality apparently awarded through a nationwide popular vote, with a substantially more complicated truth underneath that public belief. I infer the pleasure of a political mystery whose revelation changes both the election and the meaning of survival. The intended tone is urgent, wry, and emotionally exposed; the scale is national, experienced through a dying candidate and the wife she has mourned for twenty-three years. Their disagreement must remain real after their reunion. The payoff is an electorate becoming visible and a protagonist choosing what to risk when telling the truth may cost her the bodily future she fought for. Neither a conspiracy, a particular technical mechanism, nor a happy ending was prescribed.

Provisional form: close third person with Isabet Orris, a famous, difficult concert pianist with a degenerative illness. Enough room for scenes and an actual political aftermath, provisionally 4,000–5,000 words. No connection to an established story is needed.

- Isabet watches the last districts declare. The displayed ballots give her a narrow nationwide majority; she wants the immortality treatment because she wants more life, music, and bodily freedom, without a public-service justification. Then the electoral commissioner tells her she has lost. An impossible precinct outvotes the entire published margin. Its rolls include her wife, Maret Tal. A concession interview is scheduled for morning; Isabet refuses it until she sees the ballots.
- The commissioner takes her into the protected archive beneath the election hall. Individual biological regeneration is genuinely possible and production is genuinely scarce. But it was preceded by neural continuation: a widely administered medical implant preserves a person's mind at bodily death. The dead have been awake, living, making things and fighting over politics in a physical computational habitat. The popular vote allocates the rare regenerative treatment, including return to a body for a deceased candidate. It does not decide who can continue to exist. Published living tallies have always omitted the larger electorate, and each apparent winner has actually won the combined vote. Isabet's close, independently monitored count has made the discrepancy impossible to conceal without recruiting her into the lie.
- Reunion through an archive display first seems like an offered bribe. Maret proves neither a frozen recording nor an agreeable ghost: she has twenty-three years Isabet knows nothing about, an occupation and friendships, and she voted for Isabet's rival. The rival promises more archive capacity. The dead suffer crowding and reduced subjective time while the living are sold an expensive, exclusive miracle. Maret wants Isabet alive and embodied, but will not subordinate millions to her own marriage. Isabet has been promising that immortals should travel, play, and enjoy their gift; she did not know whose conditions her proposed spending would neglect.
- Secrecy is an old political bargain, maintained by votes among the dead and by living officials who control their physical survival. Living taxes power people the taxpayers believe gone; the dead control the coveted award while fearing what an openly hostile living population could do to their habitat. The commissioner offers Isabet a private archive connection to Maret if she accepts the loss. Maret argues for negotiated disclosure with physical safeguards, rather than either permanent concealment or a spectacular announcement made over the dead's objections. Isabet must stop treating access to her wife as the whole country's problem.
- Isabet uses the national concession broadcast she has already earned to stage a joint address with Maret and other archive citizens, after arranging living witnesses inside the habitat and releasing evidence in several places. The proof is people answering the living, including intimate and politically inconvenient replies, not an official explanation alone. She exposes the withheld electorate and her own genuine defeat. She also refuses the comforting fiction that everyone who hears a lost loved one must accept the continuation or forgive the institution. Public anger and attempts to disconnect the archive make the risk concrete; the witnesses and broad disclosure prevent a quiet disappearance, without making a speech magically create consensus.
- After an emergency public settlement, the election is rerun with an openly combined roll and a separate, enforceable archive-access agreement. Isabet loses again. The rival's victory is no longer a falsified popular story; the greater issue, the dead's secrecy-backed political majority, remains a difficult constitutional negotiation. Isabet has secured ordinary family access at the cost of her campaign's central hope. The ending returns to music and choice with consequence: during a two-way public concert between the hall and archive, Isabet's failing hands force her to stop, and Maret refuses to pretend that losing her body is no loss. They make a plan for their next conversation without calling the loss a cure. The vote still decides who receives bodily immortality; it no longer decides whom the country admits is alive.

Risks to test in discussion: explain the concealed combined tallies without requiring everyone to be foolish; let Maret's intervening life and political independence make the truth substantial; earn safeguards and aftermath through action rather than a persuasive speech; preserve Isabet's bodily desire without using the reunion to erase death or disability.
