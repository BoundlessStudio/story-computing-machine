# The Closed Day — provisional direction

The Emperor survives an attempt in a crowded audience chamber. The Guard catch the attacker alive, then turn to face the Emperor: his anger is dangerous because it becomes orders, and his orders become reprisals against people who never held a weapon. Their oldest duty is to hold a captured attacker through a “closed day,” when she cannot be sentenced in the heat of an attempt. At the last attempt, the Emperor obeyed that narrow rule while ordering the attacker's relatives and neighbors seized. The Guard carried out those orders.

A veteran guard takes custody of the assassin, a woman whose brother died in that punishment. She meant to kill the Emperor; the guard cannot turn her into an innocent petitioner to make his duty easier. He signed the earlier seizure order and refused to hear evidence that her brother was uninvolved. He offers to hide her safely in a cell until morning. She refuses to disappear into his custody on those terms. In front of the other guards and a court recorder, she names his part in the reprisal and produces the order bearing his mark. Her demand is that his account of what happened be recorded beside hers before she goes with him. He can let her accusation vanish with her, or become a witness against himself.

The Emperor first orders her face uncovered and her kin named, then commands the recorder to destroy the page. This is his wrath in action, not a rumored power. The guard uses the closed day to resist the first order, but that rule cannot protect her family or the record. He must disobey openly, put his own mark on an account of the earlier seizure, and get both woman and testimony out of the Emperor's private chamber to an independent civic magistrate while fellow guards choose whether to stand with him. The assassin chooses to risk that exposed route instead of the hidden cell. A possible ending leaves her alive and answerable for her attempt, with the guard stripped of rank; a witnessed copy is beyond the palace, though the Emperor's power to punish has not vanished. Protection has a visible cost, and neither the Guard's earlier obedience nor the assassin's intended murder is excused.
