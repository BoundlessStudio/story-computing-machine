# Opus review: The Cat Who Kept the Door

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: PASS — the three voices are distinct, uptake holds line by line, and the decisive and final exchanges stay plain; only small snags at the tomb door.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, varied and dash-free; a few staging slips (which door, which "them", where she is standing).
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — every prompt promise is delivered and resolved; two minor questions (did Murrant know the date, tomb order against his life count).
- Edit scope: polish
- Existing review.md said: `Verdict: PASS`, `Dialogue: PASS`; agree, though it missed the minor knowledge and chronology snags below.

This is one of the collection's cleaner dialogue stories, and the edit pass should treat it that way. With the tags covered, the three speakers are still easy to tell apart. Isabet asks short, exposed questions in private and gives crisp orders in public. Murrant is sparing and dry, drops to single words under strain, and ends on plain admissions. Aphan speaks in unhurried passive decrees and uses words of its own ("station", "attendance"). There is no correction ping-pong, no tennis-match run, and nobody states the lesson at the refusal or at the end. The one place the house voice gets in is the lore at the engraved door, where Murrant briefly talks in maxims. The pass should stay light: fix the knowledge, chronology and staging snags, and leave the plain lines alone.

## Edit guardrails

- Keep Murrant telling Isabet "Something older than magic has awakened" right after he takes out the robe and scythe (L31). Keep the next sentence, "Older than any magic our people remember", too. It is a deliberate hedge (the outline's boundary: Aphan is older than this kingdom's magic, not necessarily all magic).
- The other prompt promises must stay visible: the three moons align (L15–L19); the scythe he swore never to use again (L99–L109); nine lifetimes of service (L69); a young, frightened queen (L33); Aphan as a made mind from earlier civilizations, asleep until tonight (L77–L85, L139–L143). Aphan must still be destroyed by the end.
- Rating is broadly accessible and non-graphic. The killings (L13, L109) stay brief and off the page.
- Universe, LOCKED `rules.md` "Living agency and magic": only life uses magic. Aphan acts only through machinery, projection and sound. The scythe only focuses Murrant's power. Isabet's rescue lever (L259) is purely physical. Do not give Aphan spells or give Isabet a new power.
- The ending depends on these setups. The scythe can part only a join it can see (L91); the barrier's hinges and the column's silver seam pay that off (L227, L245). "Only this one can give orders now" (L85) is why one cut ends the threat. The counterweights (L131, L251) make her lever believable. "That was my eighth life" (L115) makes this life his last.
- Length band is 2,500–4,000 words; the prose is about 3,470. Keep edits to clause size.
- Do not add wit, maxims or explanation to Isabet's lines, to Aphan's decrees, or to the final exchange. Their plainness is what works.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · She asks about the oath after narration says she was told

- Where: L99–L109, at the metal door, after she catches his robe
- Text:
> Her father had told her the story when she had wanted the scythe on her first banner.
> “What happened?”
- Problem: Ground/knowledge. The narration says her father told her "the story" of the oath. The next line has her ask Murrant what happened. The reader can't tell how much she already knew, so the question looks like either forgetting or the author resetting her so Murrant can tell it.
- Fix: Make her father's version partial. He told her only that Murrant had sworn it, not why. One clause in L101 does it. Leave "What happened?" and Murrant's answer (L109) unchanged.

### D2 · Minor · The tomb-door lore turns into maxims

- Where: L81 and L91, at the engraved door, before the shell opens
- Text:
> “People made a mind and gave it more power than they could take back.”
> “Later people tried. Several civilizations of them. We built our first hearths in their ruins.”
> I cannot cut my way through a thing with no seam, however long I wish at it.
- Problem: Here Murrant stops answering a frightened girl and starts narrating a chronicle. "More power than they could take back" states the story's lesson (Philosopher). It answers a simple question ("People made it?") that needed a simple yes. "Later people tried" has no object: tried to take it back, or to destroy it? The reader has to work it out from the scratched-out engravings in the next beats (Ground/referent). "However long I wish at it" is an odd idiom that stalls the line. His speech elsewhere is sparing and concrete. This is the one stretch where the collection's polished lore voice gets in. Isabet's questions here are fine and in character.
- Fix: Answer "People made it?" more plainly and cut the power maxim. Give "tried" its object (to destroy it). Keep "We built our first hearths in their ruins" as the one earned line. Replace or cut "however long I wish at it"; the seam rule itself must stay. Keep "That is where it thinks" and "Those were other places where it thought". Illustrative only: "People, yes. Long before ours. Later ones tried to destroy it."

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Staging slips: which door, which "them", where she stands

- Where: L23 and L41 (throne cupboard and "little door"), L129 (fallen tomb corner), L225 (shield), L241 (retreat on the bridge)
- Text:
> Under the lowest panel of its carved back was a cupboard she had always supposed held cleaning rags.
> Murrant slipped through the little door behind the throne
> a whole corner of the royal tombs had fallen through the roof
> The gold curve drew tight around them, scarcely above her head now.
> She scrambled backward onto the bridge.
- Problem: (1) The robe and scythe come out of a rag cupboard low in the throne's back. Two paragraphs later, cat and queen go "through the little door behind the throne" to the tombs, and at the end (L301) she shoulders that door open. The reader can't tell whether the cupboard is the door, and a grown woman walking through a rag cupboard is hard to picture. (2) They have just walked down through the tombs and saw no damage, yet a whole corner of them now stands in the chamber below. We can't tell whether it fell tonight (and they missed it) or long ago. (3) Grammatically "them" is the three arms, but the meaning is that the shield tightens around Isabet and Murrant. (4) She is already on the bridge at the threshold (L227), so she cannot scramble "onto" it.
- Fix: (1) One clause: make the cupboard the back of a low door onto the stair, or send them through a separate door beside it. (2) Say when the corner fell (for example, old and furred with dust), or pick another marker of scale. (3) "around the two of them". (4) "back along the bridge".

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Did Murrant know the waking night?

- Where: L85 (tomb door), against L11–L19 (feast) and L155–L157 (Aphan's audience)
- Text:
> It shut itself away to sleep until the sky reached a position its makers had chosen. Tonight.
> “Your companion knew.”
- Problem: Murrant spent his fourth life reading the builders' records, and "Tonight." suggests he knew which alignment would wake it. If so, the story doesn't explain his relaxed theft of the liver, the last-minute evacuation, or his letting a full court feast above the machine. Aphan's "Your companion knew" and Isabet's "Nobody told me" raise this, and the story drops it. If he didn't know which night, "Tonight." misleads.
- Fix: Smallest fix: make L85 say he could read that the sky would wake it, but not which sky. Then "Tonight." becomes something he learns now. If the editor wants him to have known, give it one plain beat later (not a speech or an apology scene at the end).

### C2 · Minor · Tomb order crowds his life count

- Where: L63–L69, the descent through the tombs
- Text:
> They passed the tombs of the family her grandfather had displaced, then a queen whose likeness had been chiseled off every coin.
> “My second life. She thought I might wander into the kitchens and get lost.”
> The next chamber held square coffins and no likenesses. Lower still, the kings lay on their sides inside jars.
- Problem: The descent reads as going back in time, so the collar queen is one of the more recent burials, yet she belongs to his second life. That makes his first life cover the first family, the jar kings and the square-coffin dynasty, and pushes lives three to eight into the last two dynasties. An attentive reader will trip on the arithmetic.
- Fix: Either make the collar a later life (for example, his sixth or seventh), which fits the fourth-life reading and the eighth-life princes and keeps the kitchen joke, or signal many landings and generations between the displaced family and the defaced queen. Change nothing else in the exchange.
