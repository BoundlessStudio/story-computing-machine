# Opus review: The Night She Knew

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — three lines explaining the daughter's ability have time logic a reader cannot follow; the rest of the dialogue is strong.
- Prose: PASS — concrete and controlled; only minor narrator glosses and reversal templates to trim.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — delivers the prompt fully within the length band; only small age and staging nits.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: PASS, `Dialogue: PASS`; disagree on dialogue, because its "chronology... remain causally coherent" misses the L87–L111 knot.

This dialogue is better than the collection norm. The mother and daughter sound like different people: a formal, defensive planner who keeps asking clarifying questions, and a sarcastic sixteen-year-old who answers early and falls into sensory fragments when she is overwhelmed. The arguments get petty and messy, and touch and consent carry half the conversation. The failures cluster where the daughter explains how her ability works. In three places ("About forty seconds from now", "That's why I said it wasn't first", the blue mug) the time logic is compressed past what anyone can reconstruct, so the reader is lost along with the mother. The edit pass should make each of those lines say one recoverable thing in the daughter's blunt, slightly clumsy words, without turning them into a lecture. After that, sweep a few reflexive last-word corrections and two small mismatches in the rinse exchange. Do not flatten the sarcasm, the mother's formality, or the banter at the tub.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Blocking · "Forty seconds from now" timeline knot

- Where: L81–L113, kitchen, the first explanation of how she knew
- Text:
> "Do you hear my thoughts?"
> "I don't know what you think. I knew what you asked."
> "I didn't ask it."
> "About forty seconds from now."
> We both looked at her phone. The screen had gone black.
> "I knew me saying no. And the fan making that noise. And this hurting."
- Problem: Ground/referent and timing. The mother has just asked "Do you hear my thoughts?" at L81, so "I didn't ask it" can't be about that question. It must mean the unspoken accuracy question. Then "About forty seconds from now" puts the asking in the future, and the phone glance only makes sense as timing it. Yet nothing is asked forty seconds later. L111 names the fragments of the exchange that has already happened: her "No", the "Ow" spot (L77–L83), and the fan. L265 ("Like the question and your answer") also treats that past exchange as the foreseen one. The mother's "So it's fixed" rests on the "from now" claim. A reader cannot build one timeline at the story's first explanation of the ability. The fan is also first heard only when she names it (L111–L113), so it reads as a prediction coming true rather than a detail she had foreseen.
- Fix: Anchor the whole exchange on "Do you hear my thoughts?"/"No", which L111 and L265 already describe. Change L91 so the mother says she hadn't asked it *yet*. Replace "Later when?" and "About forty seconds from now" with one line that places her knowing about forty seconds *before* the asking. Cut the phone glance at L99, or give it a real reason. Mention the fan's tick once around L77–L81 so the reader hears it with the "Ow". Keep "So it's fixed", "I didn't hear you", and "So I guessed the question." Illustrative only: "I hadn't asked it yet." / "I know. I got it early. Like forty seconds."

### D2 · Major · The rabbit "tomorrow" exchange does not parse

- Where: L171–L189, kitchen, second section of dye
- Text:
> "You brought it up tomorrow afternoon."
> "We are not doing that."
> "I know. That's why I said it wasn't first."
> "You don't get to be cruel because I'm trying to understand."
> "And you don't get points for trying tonight."
- Problem: Ground/uptake. "That's why" claims a cause the reader can't reconstruct. The mother refusing time-talk does not explain why the daughter said at L11 that the rabbit "wasn't the first thing". The mother's "I don't understand" registers her own confusion, but the reader is just as stuck, and the line is never cashed: L305 tells the rabbit story without returning to it. The escalation that follows is a mirrored pair, "You don't get to…" / "And you don't get…". It reads as a scripted comeback rather than a hurt mother and a sulking teenager.
- Fix: Keep "You brought it up tomorrow afternoon." (a good time slip) and "We are not doing that." Replace L181 with a reason the reader can follow even if the mother can't: she answered tomorrow's question early to get it over with. Illustrative only: "I know. That's why I answered it already." Break the mirror at L187–L189. Let the mother's hurt come out shorter and less therapeutic, and let the daughter's grievance about years of not asking land without echoing her mother's syntax.

### D3 · Major · The blue-mug glimpse doesn't explain why she moved it

- Where: L269–L277, hallway explanation (cf. L167, L387)
- Text:
> "I saw the handle in the laundry lint. I thought I dropped it in there."
> "So you moved it."
> "And then the shelf broke. Maybe because you put the sugar up there instead
- Problem: Ground/causality. "The handle in the laundry lint" reads as the mug broken in the laundry room, and "I thought I dropped it in there" reads as a warning of an accident there. Yet L167 has her carry the mug *to* the laundry room and leave it on the dryer, and L387 has it survive. Why would a warning about the laundry room send her into the laundry room? This is the only hit the story explains from the inside, and her guilt about the sugar depends on the reader following it.
- Fix: Make the glimpse and her reading of it match what she did. The simplest version: she saw the intact mug on the dryer among the lint, took that as where it was supposed to be, and put it there. Her sugar guilt then follows cleanly. Drop "handle" if it implies the mug broke. Illustrative only: "I saw it on the dryer. In the lint. So I put it there." Keep "A bag of sugar did not break that shelf." / "You don't know." / "Neither do you." exactly as they are.

### D4 · Minor · "You started before it sat too long"

- Where: L29–L53, before the first section is painted
- Text:
> "Turn around," I said. "We have to start before this sits too long."
> "You started before it sat too long."
- Problem: Ground/referent. "This" has no object on the page. The dye is "not mixed yet" at L13 and next appears already in the bowl at L55, so the mother's line floats. The daughter's twist, reading "this" as the secret that sat for years, only works as sarcasm, but as written it sounds like a concession ("you started in time"). "Your hair." shows the misfire, but the reader has to reverse-engineer the joke.
- Fix: Show the mixing in a clause between L29 and L47 so "this" is the bowl. Then either cut the twist and let "Turn around" stand, or mark the sarcasm so it plainly means eight years was too long. Illustrative only: "Oh, now we're worried about things sitting too long."

### D5 · Minor · Lottery and insurance-form lines depend on narration and unseen staging

- Where: L55–L75, first painted row
- Text:
> "Not lottery numbers exactly."
> "Your face is doing the insurance form."
- Problem: "Not lottery numbers exactly." first reads as the daughter hedging her own ability (she can do something close to lottery numbers). Only the next narration paragraph (L65) shows she means the question her mother was about to ask. She is also facing away (L55 "She faced away again.") when she reads her mother's face, and the dark-window reflection that would explain this isn't introduced until L201. The mother then asks whether she hears thoughts, so the reader needs to know the daughter *saw* the face rather than wonder whether she read her mind.
- Fix: Point "Not lottery numbers exactly" at the mother's intent with two or three words. Illustrative only: "Not lottery numbers exactly. Same idea, though." Plant the window reflection before L67 (move the clause from L201), or have her glance back over her shoulder before the insurance-form line.

### D6 · Minor · Reflexive last-word corrections

- Where: L193–L199 apology; L225–L229 channel-changing; L429–L441 routes
- Text:
> "That is generally when an apology happens."
> "That's also deciding."
> "See what?"
- Problem: These are correction ping-pong and straight-man setups. "That's also deciding." is a crisp paradox from a girl pressing the heels of her hands into her eyes, which is uniform fluency under pressure. "See what?" exists only to let the daughter state the point. The mother's pedantic apology line is in character once, but together with the others it makes sparring the default rhythm. The purposeful corrections are not part of this finding: the sugar exchange, "Both happened.", and "Of me?" / "For you." / "That wasn't the question."
- Fix: Replace "That's also deciding." with a raw, non-epigrammatic reaction: a sound, "Stop", or a report that it's still happening. Cut "See?" and "See what?" so that "You don't even know when you're doing it." follows "Not all of those were tests." directly. Keep the apology retort only if the other two are roughened.

### D7 · Minor · Two small mismatches in the decisive rinse exchange

- Where: L509–L517, bathroom, after she stiffens at the cap
- Text:
> "I know this," I said. "I believe you. I should have said it before."
> The next part came less cleanly.
- Problem: "I know this" has no stable referent. Right after she stiffens, it could mean "I know you'd flinch", "I know that's all you have" (L499, several paragraphs back), or an echo of L31. "I believe you" is the line the story needs, and the opener muddies it. The narration then says the next part "came less cleanly", but the line that follows is fluent and balanced, so the claim and the speech disagree.
- Fix: Cut "I know this," or make it concrete. Either show the unclean delivery (a false start or restart inside "I'll probably want to. I already want to.") or cut the narration's claim. Keep "I'm not going to ask what you see" and "you don't have to tell me because I want it."

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Onset age vs. the mother's earliest logged hit

- Where: L11 opening; L167 the Household note; L305–L317 rabbit
- Text:
> "Since I was eight. Maybe nine. And no, the rabbit wasn't the first thing."
> At seven, she had put both hands over her ears before the smoke detector failed during a storm.
- Problem: The daughter dates the ability to eight or nine, but the mother's first logged "hit" is at seven. A mother who keeps a dated note would notice. As written, it reads either as a slip or as an unflagged example of the "hits in little baggies" charge.
- Fix: Either move the smoke detector to eight or nine, or have the mother register the mismatch in half a sentence, which would sharpen the baggies accusation. Keep "third grade", "I was nine" and "wasn't the first thing" consistent with whichever you choose.

### C2 · Minor · Timer wording and hallway staging

- Where: L13 and L287, the phone timer; L243–L283, the hallway
- Text:
> The timer on her phone chimed from the kitchen, although we had never started it.
> I stopped beside her.
> She kept her eyes on the wall between us.
- Problem: A phone "timer" set to a clock time ("She had set it for 8:20") is an alarm. "Although we had never started it" hints at something uncanny, and the next sentence explains it away; decide which it is. After "Don't touch me," "I stopped beside her" implies the mother was already next to her. The daughter then moves into the hallway while the mother "stayed in the kitchen", and a "wall between us" would block the flinch the mother sees at L283.
- Fix: Call it an alarm or a reminder, or cut "although we had never started it". Change "stopped beside her" to stopping short or an arm's length off. Make "the wall between us" the doorway, or the hall wall she is facing.
