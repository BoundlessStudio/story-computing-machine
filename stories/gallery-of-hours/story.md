# The Sitter’s Fee

Three times that November I had to give Iria Senne her teeth back. There was a plane tree outside the end window of the Sill Street Sample Room, and when the leaves fell, a black twig showed through the glass of her two front teeth. A collector would come to the rope and put his tongue to the place in his own mouth where he thought he saw a gap in hers. I could give Iria another six inches of lead and get her to step along her plinth, and there was the lovely mouth she'd paid for, down to the little chip where she'd bitten a cherry stone as a girl. She'd paid to keep that too.

It was my job to see that the current got to every person on that floor. The teeth were a favour. So was the first photograph she made of me. The guard at the goods door took one look at the number of years between me and the girl on my badge and made me wait outside in my shirtsleeves while he sent upstairs for somebody to identify me. Iria took the new picture against the concrete. When I sold two more years to get my stove replaced, she said we might as well have a pair.

It got to be our custom to do my portrait after a sale. I wore my yellow dress twice and after that I thought I'd see if I could come up with something different every time. There was one hat, a flat white felt with a single black quill, that I went to three shops to find and then we neither of us liked it on me. In the picture I have it in my lap. You can tell by the way I'm holding it I mean to get my money back.

One Thursday towards the end of that November Iria crooked a finger over her phone. “Dolly, here's one in the good collar that I never showed you. Come and admire yourself. If I'd had that face I would have kept it and lived on bread.”

“You'd have wanted something on the bread.”

It was a pretty picture. I had just sold five years to a man over the house line and he'd called me Auntie when he rang off. The look I had been giving his end of the wire would have curled the paper off a wall. What I remembered about that dress was how often I'd had to put it on sitting down because of the sticking zip. I had never known I could look like that in it.

She enlarged my face between two glass fingers. There was my own mouth, big as a peach. Down at her feet a corner of grey wool had worked out from behind the stand. I'd slept there the night before. Iria tucked the fringe of my blanket out of sight with her toe and let me go on looking.

“Do see to my lead, there's a dear. I've got a hair caught under the collar,” she said. I let out more cable, gathered the soft stuff under her bun and worked a finger in beside the plug. She lifted one shoulder, ticklish, as she'd been in the flesh. That lock of hair was there in the photograph she'd paid the makers to work from when they lifted her out of her first body. There was young Iria on a beach, getting her hair blown in her mouth, a fine thing to keep if you could afford the years. My finger came away with last week's fluff. It had the hot, homely smell of the dust you get in an iron.

“I showed Mr Loane your pictures,” she said, with mine still open between us. “He's booked the room this afternoon. He'd like one more for the set and he wants to be here for it. Is there anything nice in that bundle of yours?”

“My other shirt. I didn't come here to sit, Iria. I didn't go home.”

I rented a room six floors up the steep side of Sill Street hill. Twice in a month I had lost the callout money on a gallery fault, coming down those stairs sideways with both feet to a tread. The fee went to the first technician to lay hands on the job. Last night I had let the cleaner lock me in. There was a room at street level coming vacant in Barley Lane. I could pay the monthly rent down there. The woman who showed it to me had written the deposit on a slip of paper and I still had it in my shirt pocket.

“When you say he wants to be here,” I said, “does he mean for the photograph, or while I'm selling?”

Iria laid the phone against her trousers. Through the clear stuff I could see the little bar of white in her thigh that bent her knee for her. “He wants to see it all the way through. I haven't offered him any years of yours, Dolly. I've offered to show him my pictures again, with you in the room this time. He came up here Tuesday and looked over my shoulder, and do you know what he asked? Didn't I have any games on this thing? I showed him what I have.”

Last winter a gentleman had offered to rent her for a season in his dining room. I'd brought her a picture from the spot where he wanted me to hang her lead. It gave her a close view of the back of a grandfather clock. She let him have Thursday evenings here instead. His dinner guests went home with any number of photographs of the sweep of her neck, the one clear thumb busy on the screen. Her own prints went into cardboard boxes in my cupboard behind the spare fuses.

I didn't much like the idea that Loane had seen the one with my collar before I did. I made her bring it up again and had another good look at it before I went to let him in.

Mr Loane brought his own folding stool. He took it out of a long blue cover and wiped all four rubber feet on the doormat. They gave him a certain authority in my eyes, those carefully wiped feet. I had known owners to sweep in here with six guests and a damp dog and tell me I was in the way of their view.

He passed the little fellow who sat on his black cube all day with both thumbs going on his phone, and opened his stool beside the young man with the backpack. That young man stood with his curly head tilted to the ceiling, apparently taking a grave interest in the reel from which his power line descended. The raised face had made money for a succession of owners. If you stood a little behind him on his left and put your head at the angle of his, you could see the tiny, perfect image of the street in the polished hollow of the reel. You could watch somebody in a yellow coat walking a dog through the sky.

“There was a delivery van going along up there backward the other day,” Loane told him. “I lay on my back to see it. My wife thought she'd better go for help. How often do you get a van?”

“Tuesday's your day,” the young man said, without taking his eyes off the ceiling. Loane lay back on his elbows to have a look. He'd have stayed there for Tuesday, too, if I hadn't told him there was a much better view down our end. I was impatient to find out what he would pay.

I got down the box with the badge print in it and took the lid off for him. The picture of me with the hat in my lap lay on top. Loane wanted one of each photograph, all the way back to the badge. He put a price on the set, to be divided between us. My half came to a quarter of the deposit on the room. Iria looked up to see what I thought. Here was a woman who had spent part of her ninetieth birthday listening to a gentleman of fifty tell her what it was like to have been young. The face she'd chosen to take into the years ahead was her face at twenty-four.

“We'll buy a better box for the prints,” I said. “This one's let the corners go soft.”

She had to put the phone down to smile properly. We had sold the first thing we'd made together and for about twenty seconds we could simply enjoy it. In the gap before anyone spoke I heard the rain down the end of the room, gathering weight on the roof until it fell past the big window in ropes. Warm light from seven chests lay on the floor under the black plinths, and on each side of the room, above the places where glass merchants used to set out their samples, the concrete went very dark.

“For the sitting itself I'd pay twice that,” said Loane. “And of course the buyer pays you for your years at the house rate. May I move the stool over this side, Miss Soper? I'm trying to work out where I'll be while it's happening.”

“Mr Loane. I see where the prints will go. What will you do with the sight of me?”

He was pushing down the seat of his stool to get the lock to take. He stopped and looked at me. “Take it home, I suppose. The same thing I did with the backwards van. Look, Miss Soper, I've gone backwards and forwards through those pictures a dozen times. In the one with your felt hat I think I can see where you got angry with the hat. Where does that face go when you sell the next few years? I can hang two pictures beside one another. I'd like to see what happens in the space between. You can tell me that's private. It is. That's why I booked the room and came alone.”

“I've sold a year and come away with a bunion,” I said. “You're going to have a disappointing afternoon if it's my feet you want to see.”

“Then I'll have had a disappointing afternoon. It's possible I should be allowed one at this price.” He got the stool to stand and sat with both knees up in the air, a long fellow in a short coat with his hands hanging between his thighs. “Four years, I thought. A photographer I buy from once told me anything under five years was so little you could undo it with a haircut. I was hoping you might prove him wrong.”

I knew what four years came to at the house rate. Those four and the two fees added up to the deposit and rather more. I could buy a table to eat from. There had been a thumb mark in the paint above the sink in Barley Lane. When the woman who showed me the room went to answer a knock I stood against the mark and considered where I would hang my dish towel and how far the tea tray had to travel on a cold morning. She called twice from the front door before I heard her. I wanted to go back there so badly that I had begun putting out my hand for Loane's without a word about who was getting the years.

“And who's buying the four years?” I said. “Who have you got for me?”

“Me,” said Iria. “I ring downstairs and ask if they can find me ten years. I've done it five minutes before the office closes. There are always ten. I got to thinking when Mr Loane asked whether he might see you sell to someone, here I am within four feet of you the whole working week and I've never bought so much as a morning from you. You know I'll look after them, if that counts.”

“I'd be quite happy for you to waste them looking at vans. Let me use your phone, Iria. I'd like a picture of you while they're going in.”

She put the heel of one hand over her screen. The warm light that came from her chest shone through it to the little bones of her fingers. “I'm very glad to have you take my picture, Dolly. You need only ask. Suppose we get one now, while there's still a bit of daylight. Where would you like me?”

I bent to the hook behind her plinth, got the release for the reel and paid out cable. She held her phone in her teeth to free both hands and hitched up her loose trousers. She could manage the step down on her own but she let me put out my elbow. We walked together to the band of bare concrete in the space where the glass merchants' shelves had stopped, the flex running out warm through my left hand. She squared her shoulders till the dark wall behind her defined both of them. The phone came out of her mouth dry. Her clear tongue touched the chipped tooth. The first time she had done that for me I'd sighed with pleasure. I could have taken her picture then and there, and had one of the loveliest pieces on Sill Street to keep for my own.

“See what you can get of me like this,” she said. “You can always tell which way the hair ought to go and I never can. It's a nuisance having the interesting part of you right round the back of your head.” She pulled out the pin that held up her bun and the clear coil began to sag. I've seen a whole room go still for that.

I put the pin back in for her. “I want to see what your face does when you get another four years. This afternoon I can do that for you. I want a picture of it for myself.”

“And I believe Mr Loane would like a print.” She said it to me, looking at the stool and its occupant. He rose and walked as far as the rope. We could still hear the little ring on his raincoat belt slipping through the buckle.

“Come back to your stool, Mr Loane. If you want the photograph I'd just as soon have you hear me.” She sat on the edge of my workbench, feet on the floor like a woman waiting for a train. “Dolly, when I first came up here I used to oblige everybody. A gentleman wants to see my clothes from behind. He has a little girl with him who'd like me to make a scary face through my hand. A lady comes in and now I must put my head so for the photograph, since just there, just around the jaw, I look exactly like her first girl who died. By the time we closed I had a splitting headache. It took me fifteen years to discover I could pick up this thing, read the shipping news if I chose, and give them all as much of me as I'd meant to. I've sometimes spent whole afternoons rearranging the order of the people I ring up. I needn't even take a picture. I can do something of my own while they look.”

I wanted to tell her how good I had been to her in that time. Nobody got to see her with a gap in her teeth if I could help it. She'd had her sleeping hours whenever I'd been on the late shift, with every last bit of light off that her supply would allow. I had a piece of paper in my shirt pocket with the amount of the deposit on it. I could give her a look at that if she wanted to see what it cost me to oblige people. I kept my hand there over it until the first thing I wanted to say had gone away.

“I took my dress round to three shops to find a hat to sit for you in,” I said. “Think of what we can do when I get a turn behind the thing. Everybody on a plinth in this room has sat for me a hundred times. I've turned your feet and I've fussed with the lights and gone home at night and there are the names of the people who made your bodies in the catalogue and my name never once. I want to take one picture where you haven't had time to decide what to give me. He can have a print of mine to go with the one you do. You can have one. It's my picture to keep.”

“If you take it and I look awful?”

“I'd keep it, Iria. It's the one I want.”

She scratched behind her ear with the corner of her phone. I got down on my knees to sort the kinks out of her flex and presently in the polished floor, just above the reflection of my own bent head, I could see her turning the phone on herself. Her reflected mouth put on one smile and then another. She let both of them go.

“I'd like a print the same size as his,” she said. “And do get something under those poor knees. There's a whole blanket here going to waste.”

I brought the blanket out from behind her stand. By then she had turned the camera round and she was holding it out for me to see which side the button was on. “I'd be glad of both pictures,” Loane said. I shook his hand on it.

I put the blanket on the floor in front of Iria and sat cross-legged on it to arrange our feet. Mine in their split work shoes, hers with light running along the fluting of the soles, might have made a good picture. Her phone was still hers and she wanted my face. I shifted back on the blanket to get the angle she liked. Loane sat off to our right. We could hear his stool creak every time he leaned forward to see what I was doing.

My sale cuff was on a long cord. I plugged it into the foot of Iria's plinth. The years she bought from me would go up into the ceiling and come down again in the same slack lead I could see bumping against her shoulder. She put four years' money on her screen. I fitted the cuff to my left arm and put my right thumb against her offer. A few thin coins vanished from my share for the house. The cuff stirred under my sleeve.

I had meant to sit quite still and let Loane see for himself how very little there was to see. Instead I turned my head to see whether he was watching. There was such a quantity of him looking at me, all legs and damp coat and two great interested hands, that for a minute I was ashamed. I would have welcomed an instruction from Iria. She had a good one for people who kept sticking their chin out in photographs, as I did. She'd ask them to make a little shelf on the top of their heads, for a bird to come and land. I'd seen a bishop fall for that. I raised my chin another half inch deliberately, hoping she would remember it.

“Your hair,” Iria said. “Dolly, there's the most wonderful piece of your hair sticking straight up as though you've just got out of bed. I could get it against his coat if he would be good enough to come a shade to his left.”

He picked up his stool without taking his eyes off me and put himself where she wanted him. I could feel the thing I'd slept on last night under the backs of my knees and a little of the shame went out of me with the first hard shove of years. For me it had always begun behind my ears. A tightness worked forward under my cheekbones until I could feel, entire and aching in my upper jaw, a tooth I'd paid to have out at thirty. Then my hand on my knee would become extraordinarily heavy. I got it up before that happened and held it out between me and Iria, the way I would have done if I were trying to get her to dance. The big vein on the back was swelling. I looked over my knuckles at her. She could have that much of me and welcome. I heard the little sip of sound the phone made when she took the picture.

For a few seconds I had no wish to relieve her of the camera. She could make my hand fifteen feet high and I should have liked to see it. The cuff had tightened on my arm. I could hear rain smacking the pane and the long breaths I was taking and two distinct little knocks from the reel as it traveled. Iria was moving closer. She tipped her phone and laid it flat in my hand. She left her hand under mine a moment till I had a grip.

“If you could stand up, Dolly, I could look straight at you.”

“I can see you from down here.”

I wanted her mouth against the dark wall. Looking up from the blanket, I had the bar of the window going through her throat and the phone of the little man on the cube glowing through her stomach. I shifted sideways till the dark concrete came up behind her. Her chin divided the dark into two clean halves. At the ceiling the reel had stopped turning. The young man with the backpack had lowered his face to watch Iria's.

I had a clear view of her when the years got there. All through the business of putting up with the cuff and wondering how my hair looked and watching Loane watch me, I had supposed I knew what the years would do for her. She would have the look of somebody who gets into a warm bed, all the room to stretch that she can use, and is rather sorry for the person who has to get out. Iria shut her eyes. Inside her cheek I saw her tongue run up to that chipped tooth, and her jaw worked, once, as if she'd got something lovely in her mouth and was biting down to keep the juice. When she opened her eyes her whole face pushed forward with a little greedy hitch. Four more Novembers. She could read the shipping news every day of them if she liked. She was so pleased to have them that I felt the pleasure go straight through me and, God help me, I was pleased to have put it there.

I put my thumb down. When she heard the shutter Iria put her tongue back behind her teeth. I stayed where I was waiting for the rest of the time to pass out of me. They didn't give you anything to do with this bit at the end. I got my feet straight in front of me, working carefully so the kinks in the blanket wouldn't stick under my heels. The shops on Sill Street were lighting up for the evening. At this hour the little red scissors above the barber's would be lit in the reel, a sight our young man liked well enough to tip his chin another inch to see. He stayed where he was.

Iria came and squatted on her heels beside me. She let me slip the cuff off by myself. It left a band of little red diamonds which might have been there whenever I sold and might be new. Loane had stayed where we'd put him. The money to get out of the high room was there in my account whenever I could get to my feet.

Iria wanted more than anything to take back the phone and find me the picture I'd just made. I could feel her bright head hovering at my shoulder. When I put my thumb and finger to the screen they had a little trouble coming together in the right place. I managed it. Her face opened up. I had the warm inside of the lip, and the nick in the tooth she'd chosen to keep. I had got the instant before she'd begun to look for me again.

“Come and look at you,” I said.

She put her cheek up against mine, the clear hair come out of its pin and tickling the corner of my mouth. Where I had seen every stitch in the good collar that afternoon her face was a gold blur. I pushed the phone a whole hand's length farther away until I could see her properly. After a moment Iria reached under my elbow to take some of the weight, and we went on looking at her.
