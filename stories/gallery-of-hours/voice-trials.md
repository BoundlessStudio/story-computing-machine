# House Time

## 1

Before the members came in, the people on the plinths liked the radio. At five to four the man with the backpack would lift his face to the ceiling track, where, by a trick of the old lamp works' polish, he could watch the first hats coming through the door upside down. It pleased him to give them the names of the horses coming into the home stretch. Here came Ugly Money on the rail, followed by My Mother's Fault, a length behind with a fresh application of the whip. Some afternoons he got Coralie Hecht laughing so hard her overhead lead danced all the way up to the concrete.

Odette Foy liked the room best at five to four. Today she had come straight from prying up the steel shutter on a baker's doorway, and she could smell the sweetness of the new bread on her clothes. It was a pity she hadn't got hold of any. She had a sliced loaf in the freezer at home from which you could remove a piece only by committing to its destruction.

“Mrs Foy, would you come over here and look at what my hand is doing?”

Coralie stood bent over the phone they had cast for her out of the same clear stuff as her body. Beside her Gil Parran was setting a black camera on three legs. He had paid to keep a human mind going for a whole extra year, and at the end of that year he meant to take another picture from exactly the same spot. Between the first picture and the second, one minute on Odette's watch. She'd have liked to know what the seller got. One of her customers on the hill had sold two years to stop the bank taking his half of a duplex, and the bank had got it all the same.

She leaned her tool bag against the end of the service bench. The warm light in Coralie's joints showed clean through the phone. A thumb was coming down on its edge, again and again, as if the woman in possession of it were trying to kill some little thing.

## 2

The first two times I went out about Coralie Hecht's thumb, Gil Parran kept a finger under her chin. I kept my mouth shut. Four Thursday mornings at the North Lamp Works made the payment on my van, and he was a good customer in other respects. He paid for parts before he saw them. There's a species of rich man who'll ask to see the dead part, and then put the thing in a drawer for fear you've left some money in it. Gil had room to devote to better things. He had seven people who would never die arranged down one wall.

On the third call I said, “Mr Parran, I'll have to bill you for the loan of that finger if she's going to need it every time she wants to use the phone.”

Coralie laughed. He took his hand away, which was what I'd come for. She held her thumb down on the phone to keep my call lamp lit. You ought to have seen her when nobody was touching her. In the clear bun above her left ear they'd kept the little hairs too short to be caught up with the others. With her free hand she tucked one behind her ear and it came loose again. Then she bent over the phone with the private, rather ugly friendliness you see on a woman's face when she gets a message she didn't think was coming. Back at my bench the call lamp went out.

“There,” she said. “I've been doing that for you three Thursdays running.”

“I've got a better collar for your lead out in the van. It'll follow you when you do that.”

“Will it catch my hair? If Gil pulls my hair out from under that collar one more time I'm going to put my foot through his shoe.”

Through her trouser hems you could see the thick soles, and through the soles the black stone she stood on. The phone was a piece of her too. Its single working feature called a technician. That afternoon she was going to use it to ask me to stop a year.
