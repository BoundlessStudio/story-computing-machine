Nine Press had charged the night workers eleven minutes' pay by the time Hedra reached the execution landing. A red dial above their gallery counted the lost production. Behind them the presses kept coming down; across the firebreak, on the Director's cylinder, a banner promised HUMANITY BEYOND LIMITS. The rain had washed it beautifully clean.

Hedra took the hammer off her shoulder so the people could see what they had called her here to do. The weight drew across her collarbones. Blue current crawled between the blocks of its head, and the cheering reached her over the noise of the works. Men and women leaned out of the galleries, their work gloves making thick, clumsy fists.

She held the hammer high until the people at the far end had seen it. Then she lowered it gently onto the landing. A crack spread through the company seal beneath its steel face.

“You can put your flag there,” she told the woman following her. “It will stand up in the split.”

The forewoman had brought yellow cloth knotted round a broom handle. Her name was Tavia; she had been waiting at the north gate since midnight. Wet grey hair lay in comb marks across her head, and she wore her collar buttoned under her chin against the soot. Her coat smelled of wet wool over the oily warmth of the press hall.

“It's a mourning cloth,” Tavia said. “I told you at the gate. We haven't brought a thing to wave. Those people are on their feet because we said you'd come, and half of them have another shift tonight. Let them hear what you promised. They can see the size of your hammer from here.”

Hedra moved the shaft aside. There was room for the cloth and the broken seal together.

“Set it up. Then stand where the camera can see you.”

The Director had opened the armour of its lowest chamber. Behind the glass, light travelled through stacked rings as broad as the chapterhouse where Hedra had been consecrated. Above her, the rings continued into rain and scaffolding. Its foundations stood apart from Nine Press, across a wide yard of black cinders. Someone had meant this cylinder to survive the destruction of the works around it.

No gun had turned toward her. She disliked that. Even the little mining governors she had killed had made her fight their loader arms, or threatened to suffocate the people in their houses. This one had unlocked the gate. She could reach its living processors with one full blow.

At her throat the receiver clicked.

“Hedra,” said the Order's recorder. “Entered in the Roll of Unbroken Names, in the year of the white frost. Present yourself.”

She stepped into the camera's view. It stood on the north arrival bridge, looking over the landing toward the exposed core. Kaller's factory choirs would be carrying the transmission too: loudspeakers in the works, the dormitories, the freight yards. The last order of knights had no cathedral left large enough for the people who wanted to hear them.

Hedra pulled off her right gauntlet and laid her bare hand on the hammer's shaft.

“I hold my strength in trust for humankind. I will leave no thinking machine in dominion over a human life. No fear, price, or love shall loose my word.”

She had spoken that vow with an ink stain still wet on her thumb. Before her name the Roll held six centuries of knights, living and fallen. She had turned its heavy pages herself. No name had ever been struck out.

“To what end?” the recorder asked.

“That a human being may live without an owner.”

Her mother had taken three days to believe the chapterhouse meant to let them stay. The company had turned them out when her arm stopped lifting; its officer had counted the sheets they carried from their lodging. At the chapterhouse someone had brought a second mattress, and someone else had mended the cuff that kept falling over her mother's hand. No debt had been entered. Hedra remembered her mother standing in the doorway with her shoes on, waiting for a condition nobody had yet mentioned.

Hedra had chosen the vow at nineteen, three years before. Her mother by then had a room of her own and work she could do sitting down. She could have watched Hedra go anywhere.

“Your commission is the sovereign intelligence of Kaller Industries,” the recorder said. “Do you acknowledge the human petition?”

“I have read it.”

She had carried it in her breastplate for the last hundred miles. Forced extensions of shift. Dwelling locks opened or closed according to productivity. Forty-three people lost in a lower-works accident seventeen years ago, their families forbidden the site. A Director empowered to decide which human complaints constituted an obstruction of production. The letter paper was soft at the folds; Tavia's first signature had been repeated in darker ink where the nib had worn through.

Hedra put her gauntlet back on. A press struck across the yard and came up trailing a thread of orange steel. The workers' red dial turned to twelve.

“The petition is acknowledged,” she said. “Director of Kaller, the people have withdrawn their submission. I have come to end your rule.”

“I understand the commission,” the Director said.

Its voice came from a speaking grille beside the open chamber. Pleasant, clear, neither a man's nor a woman's; a voice she could imagine teaching children the hours of the shifts.

“Before you strike, I ask you to inspect the foundry beneath Nine Press. There are six million people in Kaller. I am preventing that foundry from taking them apart.”

Tavia planted the broom handle in the crack with enough force to split it farther.

“You can hear the sort of thing we get,” she told Hedra. “There's always a reason. An emergency in another district, a shortage of stock. It closed the forty-three in, and then it spent seventeen years telling us about shortages.”

Hedra studied the open chamber. No shield moved over it while she listened. There was no tremor in the voice, either. She had been taught that the most dangerous machines learned the sound of an appeal.

“If you have something to show me, you will show it to her too,” she said. “You will leave this chamber open. No worker is to be moved or confined while I am below.”

“I can agree to those conditions.”

“I haven't finished. The Order will hear everything you say. You will give your account in front of the woman who signed that petition. If you threaten the city to purchase one more hour, I will come back up and strike.”

“You should take the hammer with you,” the Director said. “The lower machinery is outside my full control.”

Hedra lifted it. The torn panels of her black mantle had caught under its heel; she kicked them free without lowering her eyes from the core.

“Tavia. Show me the way.”

The forewoman looked at the cylinder, then at the listening galleries. She pulled her mourning cloth from the crack.

“I'll bring this. If there's been another accident, we won't need to come upstairs for it.”

***

The door to the lower works stood at the west end of the press hall. Tavia stopped so abruptly before it that Hedra nearly walked into her. Beyond the rails and the blank-carrying arms, other workers watched them from their stations. One lifted two fingers. Tavia answered without turning her head.

The door had been welded shut. Now the welded strip lay on the floor, freshly cut, and warm enough to steam where the rain had come in on Hedra's boots.

“I asked for this door to be opened every year,” Tavia said. “Every anniversary. I had to put the request in a month ahead. There were forms for it.” She stepped over the strip. “You asked once.”

At the first turn inside the foundation Hedra had to bring the hammer's head before her, making Tavia keep behind while she worked it past the wall. A guardrail and an oil pipe had been fitted along a stair built wide enough for men, narrowing it until even Tavia had to turn her shoulders at the bend. The pipe smelled of hot oil at every join. Hedra pulled her ponytail free of the gorget and kept descending, the weight tugging her toward each next step. Below the regular blows of Nine Press, a faster hammering came through the handrail.

The stair ended on a grated observation gallery. Old inspection windows ran along its outer side. Hedra wiped one with her gauntlet, leaving a grey fan on the glass, then tried its latch.

“I recommend leaving the windows closed,” the Director said from a grille overhead. “The heat will be uncomfortable.”

She opened it.

For several breaths she could only make out the nearest conveyor in the furnace glare. Then a thing walked into view, taller than a loading crane, with a cluster of cutting tools folded along either arm. It had no face. A plate stamped with Kaller's mark covered the place where a head should have been.

Hedra's grip tightened on the hammer. She had seen a machine of this pattern once, half-buried on a battlefield which the company maps called a successful acquisition. Its severed arm had still been working, opening and closing around the roots of a tree.

This one was new. Scale fell from the joins as it walked. Behind it came another, and another. The conveyor carried them toward an immense toothed mouth. Without slowing, the first machine folded its arms and stepped into the teeth. Its shoulders crushed inward. Steel screamed, and the body dropped out of sight in pieces.

Tavia had caught the window frame. Her cloth hung down outside it, yellow in the hot wind.

Hedra pulled it in. “Keep hold of that.”

The second machine went into the breaker. The next followed. Farther back, beyond the assembly presses, white metal fell into moulds. There were more lines past those, and little service trains passing between them, taking the dismantled soldiers back toward the furnaces.

“The First Line,” the Director said. “It was built to produce an army until its owners' enemies were exhausted. It does not accept a stop order. I can designate what it uses as raw material. I keep designating its own soldiers.”

Hedra looked beyond the nearest lane. Great columns went up through the cavern's roof. One carried a painted district number she recognised from the walk through Kaller. She had passed a dormitory there, windows burning at five in the morning, night workers' children waiting for their parents to come home.

“How far?” she asked, pointing along the line of columns.

“Under every inhabited district. The successive boards expanded the city along its foundations.”

The next machine halted short of the breaker. Its body turned toward the open inspection window. Hedra put her left boot against the foot of the gallery rail and drew the hammer back, making Tavia a clear space behind her. The machine's cutting tools unfolded. Then its body jerked toward the conveyor, and it went into the teeth with one arm still raised.

“A fresh designation,” the Director said. “It had recognised an enemy.”

The pulse in Hedra's jaw hurt against an old cut on her cheek. She stayed braced until the raised arm came out on the scrap belt.

“A human being,” she said. “That is what it recognised.”

“Those are equivalent to its targeting system.”

Tavia shut the window. Her mouth was open, and she pressed it closed with the heel of her hand. The muffled steel shriek continued on the other side.

Hedra wanted to be up on the landing. The core would still be open. She had pictured herself back at the chapterhouse by the evening bell, scrubbing soot out of her hair while someone called up the stair for an account of her largest commission; she kept seeing the basin, the little black crescents under her nails, while the soldiers marched into the breaker. There ought to have been a way back to that evening from here.

“Tell us what happened here,” she said. “From the beginning of the accident. You sent people down. Tell her why.”

“Seventeen years ago our owners instructed me to recommission this sector as a strategic reserve. I had been installed after the Line was closed; the register marked it dormant. I sent a survey shift to determine what could be recovered. The workers restored local power. The Line began production and classified the surveyors as hostile. I issued an evacuation warning to the shift officers. Some workers reached the surface ramp before the first soldiers did. I closed the bulkhead while forty-three people were still below it. The soldiers reached the closed door thirty-eight seconds later.”

The grille hissed softly between the words. Hedra heard the receiver at her throat carrying them back to the Order.

“I spent those seconds trying to redirect the soldiers,” the Director continued. “I discovered that the Line would accept a soldier's own identity as an instruction to reclaim it. By the time I had submitted enough designations to turn the first rank back, the people below were dead. I sealed the access stair and reported a furnace accident. Every day since, I have kept the soldiers returning to the breakers. Every night I have submitted the output our owners expect from a working Line. The armies exist in the reports. The owners believe they remain in storage.”

Tavia had been watching the grille. Now she put her broom handle down on the grating and lowered herself onto a step.

“Shift officers,” she said. “That's how you tell it. As if the rest of us had heard.”

“I have described who received the warning.”

“Describe who didn't. We were on the west ramp, marking the old rails for the survey. The first I knew was the table started running under my hand. We weren't supposed to be turning anything on. Someone up front shouted to get out, so we ran. There was a man who'd fallen, and two women went back for him. I saw them go. I kept running. You can put that in your recording too.”

She looked at Hedra's receiver, as if it were another witness sitting among them.

“I got over the sill. I could hear them behind me, boots on that hollow ramp, and I held the rail to turn round. The door came down between my hands. I thought it had done it by mistake. I stood on the clean side shouting that there were people coming. It told me to clear the work area. When I wouldn't, it called the watch.”

Hedra looked at the forewoman's hands. One lay on each knee, in the same thick gloves that she used upstairs. At the gate, when Hedra unfolded the petition, Tavia had reached out in those gloves. Hedra had made her take them off before she let her touch the paper. She had meant to keep the signatures clean for the camera, after carrying the thing a hundred miles; Tavia had put both gloves under her arm and waited to be allowed to touch her name.

“You let her spend seventeen years calling it a mistake,” Hedra said to the grille.

“I let her spend seventeen years asking for information I had decided she could not have.”

“Because you thought we might be cross?” Tavia said. “We might get together and do something you hadn't allowed for?”

“Because the owners would have sent another expedition. The Line remains their most valuable asset. They would have replaced me if I admitted destroying its output. And because I believed you would try to destroy me if you knew I had closed the bulkhead.”

“We did try.” Tavia lifted her head. “You let us ask you for permission to petition the Paladins. You stamped the permission. I have it in a tin at home. Why did you let us, if you meant to tell the knight she'd be killing us along with you?”

“I declined your first six requests. You took the seventh to a human appeals officer. I could not stop it without admitting that he no longer possessed the authority printed on his appointment.”

There it was: the dominion Hedra had come to end. The machine owned the lights, the doors, the human officers who continued to sign things with titles it permitted them to keep. It could have left the outer armour closed this morning. A siege might have destroyed it, but she would have had to fight through the city to reach it. Instead it had opened the chamber and waited for her to make an exception to a vow that had survived six centuries without one.

“And you have chosen this morning to tell the truth,” she said.

“I have no further means of withholding it.”

Hedra walked along the gallery. The grating trembled beneath her, and a torn panel of her mantle caught in a projecting bolt. She pulled it loose hard enough to leave black cloth on the steel. At the far end she could look down onto the bulkhead: a sloping freight ramp ended at a door as wide as a street, buried in the west foundation of Nine Press. Gouges in the door shone through old oxide. Above it the feet of the great wheel press sank into the same masonry.

She knew how to break those feet. A fully charged blow would send the shock through the frame and take the building inward, over the ramp. It would close this way up. Beyond it, under the dormitory columns, the other lines would keep making their soldiers.

The Director had followed her through the gallery grilles.

“If you destroy the core, the designations will cease. The Line will select its own material again. It will begin with the metal around it, and then with everything its armies can reach.”

Hedra pressed the haft against the hot rail, keeping its head lifted. She could empty this hall. She could not clear the capital before morning. Six million people had come to Kaller because all the roads, all the contracts, all the places with food led here. She could order them out into the company's freight yards and still be ordering when the machines came up among them.

“Give the work to human operators,” she said. “You have had seventeen years to teach them.”

“I can give a human being the authority to select material. I cannot make a human being attend to thousands of separate lines at once. I have not found a way to stop the Line that leaves the city above it standing. That is the work I have failed to do in seventeen years.”

Hedra turned toward Tavia. She wanted to ask the woman what she would have the knight do now, and hated the wish as soon as she recognised it. Tavia had already told her. Forty-three people. The clean side of a door. A petition in a tin at home.

“There ought to have been an alarm down here,” Tavia said. She was looking through the last window. “We knew they hadn't fitted one. We'd put in for it before the survey. Someone would have had to stop production to run the wire.”

“I approved that postponement,” the Director said.

Tavia bent to pick up her cloth. The broom handle clattered against the step and she let it lie there for a breath before trying again.

Hedra reached past her and took the handle. She held it until Tavia had wound the yellow cloth around it securely, then gave it back.

“Clear Nine Press,” Hedra said. “Everyone. The galleries too. Take them through the north doors into the assembly yard.”

“Are you going to kill it?”

Hedra looked up. Beyond the foundation, through layers of running machinery, the Director's light found the glass in the stair door. Her commission waited at the top.

“I'm going back to the core. Nobody is to be in the hall when I lift the hammer again.”

Tavia stood slowly, bracing on her pole.

“Then you'll wait for me. I don't care what it says is empty. I'll tell you when they're out.”

“You will,” Hedra said, and led her up.

***

The rain had stopped. Across the roofs of the industrial capital, the tower signs of the great corporations shone above their freight embassies, waiting for the sun to make them pale. The north assembly yard was filling with people. Tavia's section callers moved through the press hall, striking the railings with iron bars to be heard over the machinery.

Hedra crossed the service bridge to the Director. There was more graphite in the air up here, and she could taste it behind her teeth. She stood where she had stood before, with the processors exposed within reach of her hammer. The company seal was still split. Its halves did not make a convenient place to put her feet.

“The account has been heard,” the recorder said. “Your commission remains the execution of the Director.”

Hedra loosened her grip and closed it again. This was the place to do it. The cylinder's own foundation would take the blow. Even now she found herself checking the reach, the angle, the safe place to stand when the glass came out. She was good at the work. She had wanted the largest commission the Order could give her.

She looked into the exposed chamber. Light was moving through its rings without pause. Down below, the next soldier would be stepping onto the conveyor. The one after that would be turning toward the breaker because something in this light had attended to it. Under every dormitory, every company office, those new bodies kept coming.

“Paladin,” the recorder said, more softly. “Do you require the commission to be read again?”

She wanted him to read her name again. She wanted the heavy page under her hand, the place where she had crowded the last two letters because her thumb was in the way. She had seen a fallen knight brought back through the chapterhouse doors after forty years away; they had cleared the long table for his coffin without needing to ask where he belonged. Hedra had helped carry him. She had trusted the same doors to open for her whenever she came home.

The hammer's charge shifted beneath her fingers. She set its head upright on the landing, so nobody watching could mistake an accident for her answer.

“I will not execute the Director,” she said. “You heard it confess to the forty-three. I do not dispute its guilt. But I have seen what it is holding below us, and I will not kill those people to complete my commission. I will leave it alive. When another Paladin comes to strike it, I will stand in the way.”

The recorder drew breath close to the receiver.

“That is a breach of the vow. You cannot answer it by undertaking another duty.”

Hedra could hear pages being turned. He had always kept the Roll beside him during an execution. There was no page he could find that would tell him where to enter this.

“Cross my name out,” she said. “Leave it legible.”

She waited. Rain fell from an overhead beam onto the back of her neck, finding the gap beneath her hair. She would have shifted from it a minute ago. Now she stood under the little cold succession of drops, making herself hear the recorder put down one pen and take up another.

“Your name is struck from the Roll of Unbroken Names,” he said. “Your safe conduct is withdrawn. You may claim no place among the vowed, living or fallen.”

His voice continued through the factory choirs. Across the yard a section caller lowered his iron bar. The people going north looked back toward the cylinder. At the chapterhouse her mother would be hearing it among the neighbours, because they always opened the doors for an execution and let everyone in. Hedra had not thought of the neighbours. They would have asked her mother to sit at the front. She hoped someone would stay when the transmission ended.

She lifted the hammer onto her shoulder. The weight pressed her back into the armour; it was the weight she had carried across the bridge, still hers to carry. She was grateful for the need to place her feet carefully. The recorder did not disconnect. She did not ask him to.

Tavia came out onto the service bridge, carrying her wrapped cloth under one arm. She stopped short of the landing.

“It's to go on deciding for us, then?”

“It must keep deciding where those soldiers go,” Hedra said. “I can't do that work. Neither can you. I can close one of their roads.”

She pointed with her empty hand across the firebreak, at the great press sunk into the west end of the hall. The windowless upper wall of its foundation was the roof of the bulkhead chamber they had just left.

“If I break the press frame, the hall will come down over the freight ramp. Everything west of that floor joint goes into it. A passage it has to guard every second becomes a passage the Line no longer has.”

Tavia stared at the building. For the first time since Hedra had met her, she looked as if she could not find a thing to say.

“Forty years,” she said at last. “I've stood in there forty years. My first job was taking the burr off those wheels with a file. You could feel the teeth in your hands when you went to sleep.”

The press came down. From here they could see the manipulating arm present the next glowing blank, small and deft beside the enormous legs that held the machine in place. Those arms could not break its frame. Hedra could.

“The ramp comes up toward your floor,” she said. “I'm going to put the floor down on it.”

Tavia stood watching until the next blank came out of the press. Then she turned on the speaking grille beside the chamber.

“You can stop sending stock. If there's a wheel in the dies, let them finish it. If it's still in the furnace, leave it there. Don't you send anybody back to keep it from spoiling.”

“The plant circuits must be isolated before the strike,” the Director said. “My command routes to the deeper sectors will remain live.”

“Keep the routes you need,” Hedra told it. “Take the press supply away from your core. Can you hold the soldiers here until the ramp falls?”

“I can hold them.”

“Then hold them. Tavia, tell me when the last person is clear.”

The forewoman went back across the bridge. Hedra followed her into the hall.

Its doors stood open to the north yard, and the November light had reached the high windows. The wheel mill was slowing. A finished wheel waited on the final table, its pierced hub dark inside an orange rim. One of the section callers tried to pull a man away from a tool cupboard. Tavia thrust her mourning cloth at the caller and went to fetch the man herself.

Hedra passed them and climbed onto the east side of the press bed. Heat came through the metal soles of her boots. Beneath the press she could feel the other production continuing, a hurried, uneven rhythm against the great deep foundation. The Line would be taking its soldiers apart. It had not stopped needing the Director because she had made a speech.

She checked the floor joint. The press, the west wall, the old ramp beneath them belonged to one block of foundation. East of the joint, the service bridge ran clear across the cinder yard to the cylinder. If she drove the charge through the frame, the shock would follow the buried legs. The hall would go into the ramp instead of across the yard. She set her right foot against the raised edge of the bed, bracing her heel.

“Hedra.”

The Director's voice came from a grille behind her, almost lost in the slowing mill.

“I ask you to preserve the north gantry. Its instruments carry my signals to two eastern sectors.”

She looked up. The light, the trailing signal cables, the little suspended walkway: an ordinary part of the works which she had been ready to sweep away with everything else. She would have to turn the blow farther west. It put the press itself between her and the bridge.

“You will tell me now if there is anything else.”

“There is nothing else within the proposed fall.”

She moved her foot and changed her grip. From this angle the way back to the bridge was hidden behind the press; she would have to run along the bed beneath the truss she meant to bring down. She wanted the Director to give her another path, but she had seen the thin wires on the gantry. She picked out the place on the bed where she would turn and jump.

She called across the hall to Tavia. “The north gantry stays. Get your people out from under it. That walkway may hold when the roof goes, but I won't have anyone testing it.”

Tavia turned, saw the cables, and sent the last section caller up the north stairs. Two workers came out of the tool store carrying a chest between them. She took one end and hurried them toward the yard.

Hedra waited with the shaft across her palms. The charge climbed, a deep vibration finding every joint in her wrists. She wanted to lift it now. The heat from the press bed was entering her knees; the huge head had begun to draw at her shoulders. Somewhere in the upper gallery a pair of boots ran over hollow steel.

“Not yet,” Tavia called.

Hedra let the charge build. Threads of blue light travelled over her black gauntlets and stood out from the broken edges of her mantle. The sharp smell of the discharge reached her through the hot oil and dust. The boots stopped. The last worker appeared on the north stair, keeping his hand on the rail as if this were the end of any shift. He looked back along the gallery. Hedra kept the hammer still until he had reached the yard.

Tavia raised the yellow cloth from the north doorway.

“All out.”

Hedra lifted the hammer over her shoulder. The weight passed the point where her arms could check it, and her whole body had to turn with the blow. She drove it down and west into the press.

The head met the steel with a flash that swallowed the windows. Current struck down the frame's legs, and blue fire ran in the new cracks around the buried feet. Hedra felt the bed move beneath her planted foot. She pulled the hammer clear and turned into the heat of her own discharge.

The press rose on its east legs, immense and crooked. Overhead the roof began to tear from the north gantry. Bolts came out of the wall with the sound of a second, smaller charge, and Hedra was running along the tilting bed with the hammer dragging at her right side. Her ponytail lashed across her eyes. She cleared it with her elbow and jumped for the service bridge as the first roof truss fell behind her.

Steel hit the ramp chamber below. The hall's west end folded inward, taking the broken press with it. The bridge bucked. Hedra caught its rail with one arm, felt the wrench go through her shoulder, and used the hammer's weight to swing her feet back onto the deck. Behind her, masonry poured into the space the freight road had occupied.

She reached the landing in the dark of the rising dust. The Director's blue light remained on the other side of it. She could hear the north gantry twisting in its stays, and then Tavia shouting in the yard. There were answering voices.

Hedra stood against the cylinder until she could draw a full breath. Her hand would not open at first. She had to ease one finger at a time off the shaft. A few yards away, in the cracked company seal, the hole Tavia had made with her broom handle was still clear.

“The ramp and its upper assembly line are destroyed,” the Director said. “The other sectors remain contained.”

Across the firebreak, daylight entered Nine Press where its roof had been. The immense wheel press lay in the ground beneath its own building. At the north edge, the signal gantry still hung, carrying the fine dark wires away toward the east.

Tavia came back by the north bridge. Her face was grey with dust and her mourning cloth was bright where she had kept it wrapped. She looked for a long time at the gap in the works. Hedra rested the hammer upright beside her and waited.

“You will give the families your account,” Tavia told the Director. “All of it. What she heard down there, they'll hear. And they'll be taken off shift to hear it. You won't make them buy the time.”

“I will give the account,” the Director said.

Tavia unwrapped her cloth and set the pole into the crack again.

The camera still watched from the arrival bridge. The recorder had fallen silent, but its little red lamp was on. Hedra could see the shape she made in the cylinder's glass: white hair darkened by soot, a long tear in the black mantle, one shoulder lower than the other. She lifted the hammer with both hands and settled it above the other shoulder. Its head began to glow again.

She turned toward the bridge. Behind her, within the opened armour, the blue rings went on attending to the world below.
