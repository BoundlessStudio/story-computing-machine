# Opus review: The Weight They Never Counted

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the cook's first line is an incoherent punchline, and outside the crisis the leader and crew share one dry register of corrections and trade maxims.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, well paced, few dashes; only minor narration glosses, unclear test numbers and drainage texture.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — the prompt is fully delivered, but "leader" and "loader" are one letter apart in nearly every scene, plus several small staging and knowledge slips.
- Edit scope: heavy-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS"; disagree — it missed an incoherent line and the crew-wide maxim register.

The protagonist's voice works. They hedge, minimize and tack a qualifier onto every request, then grow by believable steps into short, specific limits ("No," "Yes."). The clay-shelf crisis has the best dialogue in the story because everyone speaks only to the job. Everywhere else the crew converge: the leader, cook, clerk, teamster and wheelwright each get a signature maxim or a correction of someone's wording ("That means some", "It's cautious", "Work isn't a favor. Neither is pay.", "They get the hill they get", "Paper deserves what it gets"), so they differ only in their nouns, and several of the maxims cannot be decoded. The edit pass should keep the plot, tests, numbers and contract. It should cut or plain-ify the closing quips, give each crew member one concrete thing they pay attention to, and have the leader push back on the protagonist's minimizing by examining, asking and waiting rather than by correcting their words.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: second person; the protagonist is the weakest in their village and joins a trading caravan the moment they turn eighteen to escape bullying; the caravan leader asks, directly and without mediation, how in all that's holy they are lifting and comfortably carrying 200 kg without breaking a sweat. Her spoken question at L59 must keep "how in all that's holy", two hundred kilograms and the lack of sweat.
- The prompt's rewrite request asks for all dialogue to be reviewed and rewritten, which covers dialogue-level rework. The active craft profile is prospective-2026-08-21. Length band is 2,500–4,000 words (currently about 3,400); cutting quips must not take it below 2,500. The tone should stay broadly accessible.
- The plot depends on these facts: two sealed 100 kg scale blocks; the evening test (200 kg comfortable, pull behind the right knee at 240, stable loads only); sloshing loads cutting capacity (the village water yoke at L55, the half-filled cask at L189), which pays off in the half-filled oil casks at L313–L315; the 180 ordinary / 200 cleared ruling (L199); missed meals and the benching; the seven-day timeline; and the market terms (160 ordinary, 200 on level ground with the loader checking, one rest day in seven, apprenticeship rate, six-week trial).
- Universe: the altered hill population is story-local, and its makers stay unknown. Keep the copy's uncertainty ("bred, built, or repaired"; whether they agreed). This fits premise.md LOCKED "Original sources for most surviving systems are unrecoverable; provenance is layered through copying" and the LOCKED personhood protection in style-guide.md. The story has no proper nouns for people or places. Keep everyone unnamed and role-labelled; C1 changes one label.
- Do not flatten: the protagonist's qualifiers and swallowed apologies, the road hand's gruffness, the loader's "Again", the crisis work calls, or the protagonist asking for a *lower* limit at market.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Blocking · Cook's entrance line is an incoherent punchline

- Where: L85–L89, camp, the leader calls you behind the supply wagon
- Text:
> "Private," the leader said when the cook craned her head from the fire. "Unless we discover an appetite. Then it becomes your problem."
> "Every appetite becomes my problem," the cook said. "That's why none of you die ignorant of it."
- Problem: Ground/referent plus Punchline Patch. The leader's "Unless we discover an appetite" only sets up the cook's comeback, since nothing on the page has pointed to appetite yet (the food questions come later). The cook's reply cannot be paraphrased: "it" could be the appetite or the problem, and "none of you die ignorant of it" has no recoverable meaning. The reader meets the cook through a line they cannot parse.
- Fix: Cut both quips. The leader can just wave the cook off, and the cook can answer with a plain grumble or order that shows her job and her standing in camp. Illustrative only: "Fine. Don't keep them past supper." Keep "Private" and the canvas screen.

### D2 · Major · Leader corrects answers instead of asking; one correction answers a non-question

- Where: L93–L103, boot check behind the supply wagon; L245–L251, the morning report
- Text:
> "That wasn't my question. Any pain?"
> "That means some."
> "That is not a report."
- Problem: Ground/uptake plus correction ping-pong. "Soles are thin" (L93) is a statement, so "That wasn't my question" answers a question she never asked. Her next reply, "That means some", is another correction, and so is "That is not a report" days later (and "It's cautious" in D3). Each time the protagonist minimizes, the leader corrects the wording. That becomes her only way of speaking to them, and the cook, clerk and teamster use the same move. The dynamic is right (the protagonist minimizes and the leader will not accept it); the form is the house tic.
- Fix: Keep the dynamic but change how she does it. At L97, cut "That wasn't my question" and ask directly, or have her press the heel and watch the flinch before asking. Replace "That means some" with a follow-up question or a look that draws out "The left heel rubs." At L251, cut "That is not a report" and have her wait or ask one concrete question, so the scraps in L253 come out under questioning, as the narration says. Illustrative only: "When did you last eat?" Keep "Report." and "Then who accepted it?"

### D3 · Major · "Generous / cautious / the work" exists to land a maxim

- Where: L199–L207, after the morning-test ruling
- Text:
> "That sounds generous," you said.
> "It's cautious."
> "I meant the work."
> "Work isn't a favor. Neither is pay."
- Problem: Punchline Patch plus Philosopher. This is four lines of correction ping-pong: the leader corrects the protagonist's ambiguous "generous", the protagonist corrects her reading, and she closes by stating a lesson. "Neither is pay" answers something nobody raised. The beat underneath is good, since the protagonist treats employment as charity, but the exchange turns it into a thesis that the next action (pricing the rate) already shows.
- Fix: Keep the protagonist's gratitude reflex in one clear line and have the leader answer with action: turning to the clerk to price porter rate, ration, salt and boots is her answer. Cut "It's cautious", "I meant the work" and the maxim. Illustrative only: "You don't have to keep me on."

### D4 · Major · Recognition beat: muddy referent, thesis close, narration gloss

- Where: L167–L179, the copied survey page behind the supply wagon
- Text:
> "And yours?" she asked.
> "Mine isn't. Not there."
> That's not a miracle. It's an unpriced risk.
> The words should have disappointed you. Instead, they gave the strange fact edges.
- Problem: This is where the protagonist learns their rank is only local, and three things blur it. (1) Ground/referent and attribution: "yours" could mean your back or your habits, and "she asked" follows the loader's snort while the cook, also "she", is in the scene. (2) The leader's "Here" takes up "Not there", which is good uptake, but she closes on a "not X, it's Y" maxim that states the story's theme. (3) The narration then tells the reader how the protagonist took it (see P1).
- Fix: Make the question specific and attributed, so "Mine isn't. Not there." reads as the disclosure it is. Illustrative only: "And your back?" the leader asked. Keep "Here, two hundred kilograms moves without four wages" and the cost sentence, and cut "That's not a miracle. It's an unpriced risk." The protagonist's request "Could we measure it properly?" can show that prices steady them; cut L179 or reduce it to one concrete sentence.

### D5 · Major · Crew speak one house voice of trade maxims

- Where: L189–L199, crew dropping in on the morning test; L219–L227, the four-day montage; L155–L163 and L233–L243, the cook
- Text:
> "Put that much in the wrong quarter of a bed and you'll bow the axle. I'll leave the whole wagon for you to carry."
> "And don't make the mules match your pace. They get the hill they get."
> "This is the number you forgot."
> "Hear that breathing?" she asked on a long rise. "That's the pace. Not the schedule."
> A road leans even when your eyes don't.
> If you sign the wrong one, hunger will not improve the arithmetic.
> "It will come from somebody's figures. Right now it's coming from my pot."
> "The axle did not eat your portion. Somebody else did."
- Problem: Identical Voices plus Explanatory chorus. In the test scene, each passing crew member gets a one-line maxim, and then the narration notes that "The leader studied all four contributions"; the montage repeats the pattern with one signature saying each. Cover the tags and the teamster, wheelwright, clerk and cook are interchangeable except for their nouns: each speaks a dry declarative with a twist or a "not X, Y" correction. Several also fail Ground. The teamster's line responds to nothing in a flat-ground yoke test, and "They get the hill they get" has no clear meaning. "A road leans even when your eyes don't" is broken syntax. "Hunger will not improve the arithmetic" never connects signing a figure to hunger. The cook's scenes run on the leader's correction rhythm (see D2).
- Fix: Keep what each line concretely adds and drop the maxim wrapper. In the test scene, keep the loader's slosh diagnosis and at most one other contribution that feeds the ruling (the cook's meal tally, handed over plainly). Cut the teamster's line, turn the wheelwright's into a concrete axle concern or cut it, and revise "all four contributions" to match. In the montage, keep the loader's "Again", the wheelwright arguing with a hub, and "Check the gap above the springs". Have the teamster talk about a specific mule and a specific stop, and let the clerk's gross/freight distinctions stand without the hunger line. The cook's care should show in feeding and bossing people, as the bread shove at L243 already does; replace her corrective comebacks with plain orders or complaints. Use the voice map for targets.

### D6 · Minor · Pricing exchange: cryptic objection, maxim close

- Where: L207–L215, the clerk prices the porter rate
- Text:
> "The rate for four hands assumes four hands can split."
> "Write what it saves, not what you hoped it would save."
- Problem: Ground/referent. The reader has to work out that the porter rate is priced as replacing four workers, and that four workers can split across tasks while one cannot. The leader's closing line is another "not X" maxim.
- Fix: Have the clerk state the objection in plain money terms (one porter cannot be in four places and still needs a spotter). End on the leader's instruction ("Then price one hand and a required spotter") or on the clerk writing it down grudgingly, and cut the closing maxim.

### D7 · Minor · Final negotiation decorated with last words

- Where: L373–L399, the contract at the market
- Text:
> "Emergencies don't read contracts," the leader said.
> "I call it before I lift. If the ground changes, we stop and crib it. If someone orders a catch, I refuse."
> The loader, sorting hooks nearby, said, "Costs fewer knees."
> "Paper deserves what it gets," the wheelwright said from beneath a nearby bed.
- Problem: The negotiation is earned: this is a trading caravan, the protagonist's body needs terms, and the reversal of asking for a lower limit is the story's best idea. But three turns in it are aphorisms. The leader's challenge is abstract when the clay shelf offers a concrete one. The loader's quip steps on "Yes.", the strongest line the protagonist has. The wheelwright's line is opaque (do paper plans deserve to be sent back?). The protagonist's L381 is also a neat three-part policy from someone whose mouth has just gone dry.
- Fix: Make the leader's challenge concrete. Illustrative only: "And if a wagon goes over like the fifth one did?" Let "Yes." end its exchange by cutting or moving the loader's line. Cut the wheelwright's quip or make it a plain grumble about bad load plans. Roughen L381 slightly (drop one clause, or let one come out haltingly) so the firmness stays and the parallel polish goes.

### D8 · Minor · The prompt question reads as a transcription

- Where: L59, the first-day rut
- Text:
> "How in all that's holy can you lift and comfortably carry two hundred kilograms without sweating?"
- Problem: The line reproduces the prompt's wording almost as written ("lift and comfortably carry"), so it sounds composed, not blurted by someone who has just watched it happen.
- Fix: Keep it direct and unmediated, with "how in all that's holy", the two hundred kilograms and the missing sweat, but make it something a person would say. Illustrative only: "How in all that's holy are you carrying two hundred kilograms like that? You're not even sweating."

### D9 · Minor · "Them" in the benching line

- Where: L263–L267, the morning after the missed meal
- Text:
> The loader arrived with a sling over one shoulder. "Not loading them today."
- Problem: Ground/referent. In this second-person story, "them" has not been used for the protagonist before this line. Readers can take it to mean the wagons (the next line names wagon columns) or the sling loads.
- Fix: Point it clearly at the protagonist, either by addressing them or by saying it to the leader with a nod toward "you".

### D10 · Minor · Departure taunt is a crafted epigram that clashes with village facts

- Where: L19, crossing the boundary
- Text:
> "Careful with that bundle. It may be the first thing heavier than your pride."
- Problem: The joke's frame is muddled (is it mocking weakness or pride?), and it implies the protagonist has never carried anything heavy, when the story later shows them hauling grain jars and stone baskets every day. The polished register is the crew's, now in a bully's mouth.
- Fix: Make the taunt crude and about rank: last place, the short field share, being sent back. Keep the second jeer ("before supper"), which already works.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · Narration explains the line just spoken

- Where: L83, after the silence; L179, after the leader's verdict; L377, after "Then I put it down."
- Text:
> That was worse than being laughed at. Laughter told you the rules. Silence kept changing them.
> Risks could be measured. Prices could be written down.
> The sentence was small. It stayed where you set it.
- Problem: Each of these ends a beat with a neat summary of what it meant. L377 sits on the decisive exchange and turns the protagonist's plain line into an emblem.
- Fix: Cut L377 entirely; the leader's follow-up question already shows the line held. Trim L179 as described in D4. At L83, keep "That was worse than being laughed at." and either cut the two-sentence aphorism or replace it with a concrete instance of the silence.

### P2 · Minor · Morning-test numbers lack units

- Where: L189, the test at first light
- Text:
> At two hundred forty, the pull returned behind your knee.
> When the loader suspended a half-filled water cask at one side, you stopped at eighty.
- Problem: Coming right after "two hundred paces", "two hundred forty" reads as paces, though it echoes the 240 kg of the evening test. "Stopped at eighty" could mean kilograms or paces. The crisis pays off this setup, so the reader needs to know what the slosh cost.
- Fix: Add units ("two hundred forty kilograms"; "eighty paces" or "eighty kilograms", whichever is meant).

### P3 · Minor · Drainage as road texture

- Where: L279–L281, the clay shelf
- Text:
> leaving the high-side drainage strip sticky
> broke through the crust over a drainage hollow
- Problem: Policy nit. The style-guide's "No default water infrastructure" rule (CANON) covers drainage used as incidental texture, and the prompt does not ask for it.
- Fix: Describe the ground without drainage works, e.g. a soft high-side verge, or a rain-softened hollow or washout under the crust. Keep the geometry: the uphill wheel breaks through and the bed leans uphill.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Major · "Leader" and "loader" are one letter apart

- Where: throughout (28 and 26 uses), e.g. L85, L129–L131, L295–L305, L321
- Text:
> The leader looked to the loader.
- Problem: With no personal names, these two role labels carry attribution in almost every dialogue scene, and they differ by one vowel. In fast reading, tags and actions slip between them ("The loader reached for the rear ties." then "The leader came up behind you").
- Fix: Rename the loader's role everywhere to a label that looks nothing like "leader", e.g. "the rigger" (he rigs the slings and yokes) or "the packer". Make this the last edit so that the excerpts in this file can still be found by search.

### C2 · Minor · Two different clerks

- Where: L13–L23 (village clerk); L83 onward (caravan clerk)
- Text:
> The seals proved sound. After the wagon came out, the clerk cleaned the blocks
- Problem: The only clerk the reader has met is the village one. At L83 "the clerk" appears in the caravan with no introduction, so for a moment it reads as if she came along.
- Fix: Introduce her as "the caravan's clerk" at L83, or give the village official a different role word.

### C3 · Minor · Team size changes

- Where: L327 and L351, the clay-shelf crisis
- Text:
> "I can slack the rear pair. Leaders stay braced."
> The teamster walked both mules for cuts.
- Problem: A rear pair plus leaders means at least four animals, but "both mules" reads as a two-mule team.
- Fix: "walked the rear pair for cuts" or "walked every mule".

### C4 · Minor · The stuck side flips

- Where: L43 and L79, the first-day rut
- Text:
> The rut looked shallow until the sixth wagon put its left wheels into it.
> Crib the right rear before we pull.
- Problem: The left wheels are in the mud, but the order cribs the right rear.
- Fix: Change it to "Crib the left rear", or, if the right-side crib is deliberate, give the reason.

### C5 · Minor · The narration knows more than the manifest could say

- Where: L267 and L313, the benching and the crisis
- Text:
> "No." He pointed toward the clerk's seat. "Copy the third and fourth wagon columns. No lifting."
> two half-filled oil casks wrongly returned to the uphill rail after inspection
- Problem: Ground/knowledge. The copying shown on the page was of the third and fourth wagons, but the crisis wagon is the fifth. A manifest would record a side (left or right), not "uphill", which only exists on today's slope, and it would not record that the casks were put back "wrongly". The protagonist's spoken inference at L315 is sound; it is the narration that credits them with more than the paper held.
- Fix: Say they also copied the fifth wagon's column (or all the columns) the day before, have that column list the casks at the rear on the right rail, and let the protagonist connect the right rail to today's uphill side. If they noticed the casks were unwedged, show when.

### C6 · Minor · Why the leader carries the survey page

- Where: L165, behind the supply wagon
- Text:
> Between route permits and faded toll schedules lay a copied page
- Problem: The answer to the prompt's "how" comes from a document the leader happens to carry, with no reason given. That also sits oddly next to her astonishment at L59.
- Fix: Add one clause on where the copy came from and why she kept it (for example, it came bundled with the hill-road permits, or she had taken it for a copyist's tall tale). Keep the fragments, the broken original and the copyists' argument.
