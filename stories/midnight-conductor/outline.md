# No Room for Banners

## Promised experience

Certain city buses cross from the human world into the realm of forgotten gods at midnight in the heaviest rainstorms. A young beastfolk secretly works as a conductor on the crossing and takes lost spirits to places gone from every map. “nope; i dont like it try again” asks for a fresh story and leaves the cause of dissatisfaction open. No particular relationship, adversity, scale, or ending is specified.

I read the pleasure as a secret journey hiding in plain sight. The source image puts a watchful young rider within warm reach of the green bench and hems her in with rain. Beyond that glass should be a much larger, stranger geography. The conductor should have to do the guiding, and a stop should mean something worth crossing for. This take uses the closeness of the bus for the human scale and lets the landscape turn on the scale of a god.

## Takes considered

- **Escape on one run.** A former god diverts a city bus toward her ceremonial court to conscript the ghosts aboard. The conductor sees a chance to set them down at an erased loading yard as the god bends the street alongside it. I chose this for the moment when the girl has to call an impossible stop while the right curb is still in reach. The bus, realm, secret job and lost destination all cause the turn.
- **A hostile fare.** An erased neighborhood's surveyor rides alone to the little court where she used to measure the starting line. In a two-hander the conductor has to decide whether to fulfill that request when the surveyor reveals why the place was taken off the map. The strongest scene is a reckoning while the bus waits at an address the surveyor did her best to destroy.
- **A living passenger's account.** A skeptical night-shift regular catches the wrong bus during a storm. After the conductor helps a ghost down at a vanished shelter, she asks the regular whether to put him off safely in the human city or take him all the way to see what another rider is afraid to see alone. The testimony makes the hidden service a discovery, with the cost of becoming known as its tension.

## Provisional direction

Mette opens up the second part of her Crookwall run after the normal riders go home. Only the driver is in on it. She likes the moment of calling a stop where the automated voice has nothing to say, and wants a whole route of her choosing, beyond the few turnings she knows. She's come hoping to ask the Marshal for it. Rella, a dead candle-yard porter who has found newer streets where every remembered turning should be, boards with other lost spirits headed for Tallow Yard. No map shows the yard anymore. Its loading gallery is still there in the gods' realm. They want the yard's open back gate, which leads out of the old ceremonial quarter. That used to be their way to get home when the Marshal's procession held up the public streets.

The Marshal, once worshipped as the patron of processions, takes an assembled company for an occasion and draws the street and the bus toward her court. She wants Mette to be her caller there and offers to lay any of her old roads for Mette whenever she asks, if she brings this party in. At the last bend Mette realizes the god's moving street will lift the bus right beside the loading gallery, a place the procession never entered and which the Marshal can't move. Rella knows the gallery from the inside. Mette sees where the bus can reach it. She turns down the offer and has the driver catch the lip while it lasts. She goes over first to open a stiff inward gate and take the passengers' hands at the sill. The street is already drawing away by the time she gets the last rider down. She sends the driver on to make the return crossing as the storm eases and has to wait off-map for another storm.

The local rule belongs to the Marshal. She can move streets on which she once led a procession. She can still exert her power after losing her worship and standing as a god. The yard has never been one of her streets. Lost spirits are continuing people and can walk from it wherever their own roads lead. This run is their way out of a specific quarter, with no claim about anyone's universal afterlife.

## Craft plan

**Teller.** Try close third in Mette's present experience, alert to angles, sounds and how long people take to find their feet. Her pleasure in anticipating a turn makes her a little smug about knowing where others will go. Try first person past as a livelier, slightly boastful account of the night she missed the bus, with a teller still vexed at a good decision's cost. Keep the wit when she is frightened.

**Mette.** She knows the short run and the Marshal's old roads. She tends to plan riders' routes for them before they've finished asking and gets sore if they know a better way. She wants Rella to trust her without hovering, and the Marshal to see how good she is at the job. She gives a direction with the part that matters first, then sometimes lets a rider in on the small joke she's been saving. Under pressure she says the name and takes a breath so a scared person can catch up. The source gives a single youthful, feminine-presenting figure watching ahead in profile, her visible eye rose-colored, composed among the bus's warm lamps and sheets of rain. The grey-white pointed ears turn before her shoulders do. At the lift the fixed window light moves along the silver of her hair while she stands very still, listening for something steady outside. She leaves the headphones in her seat for the usual stops and can hear the gallery's loose shutter through the storm with bare ears. She keeps her long pale tail out of the doors by habit, then uses it to balance at the moving threshold. School-black jacket, red bow and plum plaid read as ordinary passenger clothes. Pulling the red bow undone gives a visible marker for the narrow place to step. Keep the hosiery in the silhouette and the gaze on her work. The picture's ability to wait lasts through the hardest scene.

**Rella.** The porter knows the yard better than he knows the current city, and has sent his companions in two wrong directions already. He wants Mette to commit to a stop before he risks promising them another one. He suspects she works for the Marshal, and is embarrassed to need a kid's help even once he trusts her. He can describe an entire shift when he means to give one direction, in loading weights, widths, the nick on a hook and the things a boss failed to see. When hurried he starts speaking directly to the shyest member of the party. His own want is to take the yard's back gate for once without being in charge of the people behind him.

**The Marshal.** She remembers what it felt like for streets to fill with people who had answered of their own accord. Her mistaken premise is that she can guarantee that company by getting everyone to the place where they were once happy to come. She wants Mette's real voice to lend her gathering the sound of a freely called invitation. She has seen the girl's technique. She addresses people as if she's pausing to let an entire room quiet, gracious about minute hardships, increasingly precise in the imperative. Her account of how the first procession gathered has a beginning and an end, and is a personal inducement to Mette. Powers matter in action. The old streets rise at her gesture, a whole other hillside lights for the march, the driver is losing her choice of roads.

**The conversation owed.** As the bus is taken, Mette has to hear why the Marshal wants a living caller and answer the actual offer. Rella has to tell Mette how the porters slipped out through the yard when the procession cut the city in two. This is an argument over where the bus will deliver everybody. Rella needs the girl to hear him over the offer. Mette needs him to tell her in the order she can use. The Marshal wants the conductor to call her court as a voluntary stop.

**Place.** Crookwall's candle quarter is now a human city's night bus bend between a late bakery and grade-separated truck lanes. A level terminus on Aster Street would give the coach room to turn, and the Ragbank Viaduct would give us height but no old working entrance for it to find. Here the road grade and a private freight gallery almost touch only while the Marshal pulls the old road up toward her court. In the other realm the old courts sit at fantastic heights, human roads climbing straight into their lit arches. Behind a blank brick wall Tallow Yard is two lines of loading doors around a square open to the sky. The only way in from the bus side is through its shoulder-high loading gallery. Late autumn, midnight to the fading of one downpour. Bread racks rattle across the human shopfront, the wet green bench is warm under a coat, the bus's rear air doors hiss, the high shutter in the other quarter knocks twice against its iron staple, and old candle fat smells sweet as it warms in the lanterns. Three white wick hooks mark the gallery sill. A near match of door and curb on the human side sets up the much larger reach required on the other.

## Scenes and ending

1. **11:58, Crookwall bend and first stop after the crossing (about one quarter).** Mette waits for the human shift workers to step off. A stop chime before the bend is ordinary and the driver lines the doors with the curb. Then the hardest part of the rain erases the far lane at midnight, and a second night bus is briefly visible taking a different god-road. Mette guides one ghost off at a vanished tiled shelter before Rella speaks for his company. She takes Tallow Yard for an easy request and is impatient to get on to her own errand. Rella hears her say they'll pass the Marshal's court, and needs a clear promise before he tells the others they're safe. The reader gets one fulfilled, ordinary request on the other side before hearing the extraordinary one.
2. **The road rising to the court, the bus beside the gallery (long scene, more than half).** The new party has drawn the Marshal's eye. From the rising bus Mette sees streets tilt uphill together and recognises today's driver losing the fight to hold the usual line. The Marshal puts the offer to the girl she wanted to ask, and Rella cuts into it with the order in which the old porters used the gallery. (The shutter is a recognizable alarm to anyone who carried a box through it in a crosswind.) The god swings the grade up. Mette hears the old shutter and sees the wick hooks when the road brings that sound level with the wet window. She names Tallow Yard, has the driver brake in alignment and steps across first. Give time to the unnerving wait for the first person to follow, the noise from the procession court and the abrupt difference between the moving aisle and the firm place under her shoe. Mette could retreat while the gap is still small. She stays to let the reluctant riders decide and help some of them down. She only sends the bus on after the last one has crossed. Rella has evidence of whose conductor she is and decides to stop speaking for the whole party.
3. **The Tallow Yard loading gallery and open yard, after the cloudburst (short).** The driver puts the bus onto the return road while it is still there. Mette watches the green-lit window and her empty place slide out of reach. The departing spirits find the yard's gate, a work entrance once crammed with candle boxes. Beyond it, path lights answer feet going in different directions, on smaller routes out of the quarter that nobody is being marshalled down. For a closing action, Rella leaves his place at the head of the company and asks Mette to walk as far as the back gate with him. She accepts the company, then stops at the gate to watch him take his own way. Her first visit to a place beyond her route has taken her past a known return.

Summarize her acquaintance with the established stops and leave induction, the deceased passengers' histories apart from the account Rella needs to give, and the longer consequences of getting home outside the frame. The ordinary curb alignment, the Marshal's offer to have Mette stand at the head, and Rella's suspicion that she won't follow them through the gate load the final images.
