# Clover Stair, by Request

At the Rope Street shelter a woman leaned through a man's shoulder to read the timetable. First her forehead disappeared into his wet coat, then the tip of her nose. The man was eating a roll. He looked up when the 17 came out of the rain, and it was a relief to Nemi to see the woman's face come whole again.

The bus slowed. Heat from under the green seat was getting through the wet stripes of Nemi's stockings. Her tail lay along the warm crack where the seat met the wall. She knew the woman's face from Tuesday, when she'd gone past Rope Street on the way home and heard her say *Clover Stair* to a pair of retreating taillights. Tonight rain came off the roof of the shelter in a sheet so flat it looked like another pane of glass. Midnight was two minutes off.

The man got on with the last of the roll bulging under his cheek. By the time he'd tapped his card Nemi had him placed two forms above her at school, always asked to carry chairs down for assembly because he was tall. Behind him, the shelter's lightbox held an advertisement for the Clover shops. A man in a pink shirt was giving a little girl a ride on his shoulders, both of them looking in through the window of the new chemist's.

“Clover Stair,” the woman said from outside. She spoke patiently, with one hand at the open door. “Whenever you get there, love.”

The tall boy caught Nemi's eye and looked down at her red bow. “You came straight from school? At this hour?”

The woman was still out under the rain. Nemi put a finger to the cup of her silver headphones and looked at the woman over the boy's shoulder. “Get on this bus,” she said, as though into the headphones. “I've got a seat for you.”

The boy nodded at the headphones and went on to the back. The woman at the door considered the bright route number without moving her hand.

“I've been to the shops up there,” she said. “I sat opposite the chemist's till it opened.”

Nemi pushed a wet hank of silver hair out of one eye. She had room on her bench already, but she swept the damp end of her tail into her lap to show it. “Stay on past the railway. I'll tell you when we're at the steps.”

The woman got on. She took one look at the green bench and pulled out a clean handkerchief to sit on. Nemi watched her pinch the ends into pleats so they lay flat. The handkerchief was the sort that used to come in folded packets of three and it looked newer than her hands. “Wolla,” she said, when she was settled. “If you have to call me.”

The boy and a woman with blue hospital trousers under her coat got out at the last stop before Cresset Viaduct. The boy put up his hood and stood under the sign for a second, looking back down Lornet at the lights in their descending rows. Then he made off at a run. As the doors folded shut, Joris put the radio volume all the way down. That was how he drove the other side. Nemi took her headphones off and shook her pointed ears free of the hair that had gone cold against them.

At midnight they reached the brick mouth of the arch. In rain like this there were three city buses that could go under a bridge and come out somewhere else. She knew the 17 best. There was a place under the arch where its wheels always felt for a downhill that never came. The white bands of the road markings went under the bonnet and the driver let the bus coast, taking them in so gently that for a little while the kick of the wipers against the glass was the only way Nemi could tell they were moving.

When they came out, dark brick ran ahead of them across a high bank with nothing built beneath it. A god as big as the arch they had left sat squeezed under her altar table. She held a fat candle in her hands to keep the wet off the wick. There were more candles than there had been a year ago, and still no one to come and light them. The air that came in at the seams of the bus smelled of wet stone and candle wax. Far below, the 9's headlights were pitching down another road.

Nemi got up to guide a man in a button factory apron off at Button Yard. He had been getting out in Lornet at the stop for the new Button Court flats and going around every side of the building looking for the yard. When she showed him the narrow passage into it from the bus, the wind brought in the hot smell of ground horn. He stepped out and turned round to smile at her. She hadn't seen any of his teeth since he got on.

Wolla sat up now as though it were bad luck to find yourself slouching as you drew near. Nemi knew when to expect the landing light, because it usually slid about two seats down the aisle just as the driver braked. He braked tonight and the aisle stayed dark. In the white of the headlights there was an oblong of earth where Clover Stair ought to have begun.

“Go a little nearer,” Nemi said.

The driver eased the bus forward till the light touched the raw ends of the bottom bricks. They might have been looking at a place where somebody had pulled up a tree. There was an iron gatepost lying on the exposed earth, with the white cord still knotted round it. Nemi had stopped once to wind the loose end of the cord out of the way of the door. She'd tied it to the rail of the bottom step. The cord lay slack now, white against the dirt.

Then the landing light went over the roof. Wolla reached past Nemi for the window, leaving the square of linen on the bench. High on the slope, the lit windows of a building were passing from one side of a stand of bare trees to the other. There was the wide one above the landing and there, higher, the little round one with the red glass. Nemi made out a moving elbow where it cut off half the light. Timber creaked with such a low note that she felt it first in the pole she was holding. Somebody was walking away with the whole building.

The walker lowered his head under a branch and Nemi saw the Long Dancer's familiar cocked ear. Whoever broke the tiles of his face at the old Lornet station had left him that much. The rest of the picture showed a crowd catching up to him, a woman in a paper crown smiling as though she could hardly bear to wait her turn. They had lit candles for him at the top of the hill in those days. The label above the picture said *SPRING PROCESSION, DATE UNKNOWN*. Now the Long Dancer's heel came down on the hillside and a shudder ran through the bus wheels. Dust came out of the heater.

Wolla said, “He's got the Stair too.”

The Stair lay against him all of a piece, starting at his hip and rising to the hall on his shoulder. His hand was wrapped all the way over the foot of it, with his thumb along the bottom step. He had the hall level. Nemi had caught a glimpse of two people moving behind one of the windows and for a second it had looked as though it would be possible to watch them continue right past the windows of the bus. Now the slope opened up in shelves of sharp rock that only the Dancer's long stride could cross.

“Where's he going with it?” Wolla had both hands on the rail now.

“Up where the road turns back,” Nemi said. She could see him setting his feet down in places he could turn easily with a building on his shoulder. At this pace there might be five minutes before he came to the level ground up top. The 17's road curved back downhill there, and the Dancer would have high country in front of him for miles.

“There's somebody I came to see,” Wolla said. “My sister Meral. She's been dead six years. If she found her way up, she'd have stayed. Meral could dance in shoes you wouldn't put on to go across a kitchen.”

Nemi looked at the lights and thought how easily she had told Wolla there would be steps. “Do you want me to let you out where they were?”

“So I can stand here and watch him get smaller? I can do that from the warm.” Wolla let go of the rail to put her handkerchief back in her pocket. She had to look at her coat to find the opening. “Does your driver know Gilder Lane?”

“There's a wall at the top,” Nemi said. She'd once pushed her bicycle up to it.

“The wall came later, love. There was a gate with a square hole in the stone post where the latch went. One wet night Meral and I went up with the band. I got out to open the gate for the van, and there I was holding it wide, with rain coming off the top of it and down my neck. Alf drove for the band. He stopped by me to tell me he had nobody for the accordion that night. I let him tell me this. Meral was sitting next to him with her accordion on her knees. Alf wanted me to ask her to play. I stood out in the rain till he turned his head and asked her himself. Then he went through and turned the van right round in the yard. He never once had to back up.”

Joris had been letting the bus roll slowly up the road. Gilder Lane opened on their left, where the headlamps found a narrow slope of bricks. He stopped with the front wheels still on the road. Wolla had come as far as the end of Nemi's seat. She stopped doing up her coat and looked out at the lane.

“I called the dances till the band took a break, then I went down for air. My dance shoes were still hanging on the rail. I got one on and stood there looking at the bottom step. It was iron, and if you wanted somebody to come down and get you, you gave it a rap. You could hear it all the way up to the hall. I had a little tap of my own worked out to see if Meral would know me. I had my foot up when she leaned out over the landing and said, ‘I've saved you one.’ That was how she always did it. Up I went with the other shoe in my hand, and I think I did the whole first turn with it caught between us.” Wolla slid one of her hands under the other on the rail. “That was our last dance. I'd like her to hear me coming before she sees me, for once.”

Up the hill the Dancer took a great step across the bare rock. The landing light passed across the top of the lane and flashed through a square hole in a stone pillar. Nemi knew the pillar from the 17's usual route home. She'd always thought it was the end of a wall. In the wash from the hall windows she could see two dark lines where the wheels used to go, running up the lane and straight through the gap beside the stone.

“There it is.” Nemi went up front. “The gate. Right by our last bend. We could get up there while he's still coming around the hill.”

Joris brought the wipers round to their highest speed. “I can take the lane if there's room to turn at the top. I want to come down with my headlights in front of me.”

Nemi bent so close her hair touched the windscreen. She waited for the wiper to pass and could still see both lines leading through. *I'll tell you when we're at the steps*, she'd said, lifting her tail out of Wolla's way. She remembered how warm the seat had been and how easy it had been to say it.

“There's as much room by that post as there is at Button Yard,” she said. “Take the lane. Pull up facing downhill and he'll come past our door. I'll show him where to put the Stair.”

“If I have to back out, you'll stand by the rear window and tell me what I'm about to hit.” He waited for Nemi to take hold of the pole, then turned the 17 off the road.

The headlamps scraped their way along the brick wall to the left of the lane and caught the long stalks of something growing straight out of it. On the right there was scarcely enough dirt for the rain to strike before it fell away. Every time the driver took his foot off the accelerator, a few more pieces of brick went ticking past their back wheel and down the hillside. Nemi put a foot under the heater, her tail wide to balance herself, and kept both eyes on the narrow patch of brick she knew must be coming up on her side of the bus.

The pillars of the old gate appeared on either side of them, with the stone hole exactly where she'd remembered it. Beyond it the paved shelf was wide enough for Joris to turn left in one sweep. He brought them round facing the way the 17 usually took home. Outside the front door lay a stretch of flat road wide enough for the foot of the Stair. Nemi heard a quick scrape of music, as though a bow had caught on a wooden edge. The Dancer was very close.

The driver set the brake. Nemi reached under her collar and pulled free the red bow. When the doors swung out she held an elbow around the front pole and stretched the bow into the light outside. The Long Dancer set one foot on the rock beyond the shelf. As his heel came down the bus rocked on its springs. He caught the red moving by his ankle and bent sideways to see who was there. The whole side of the hall came into view above them, running with rain. At one window hands appeared to pull up a blind. There were people inside who wanted a look at what was holding up the dance.

“Clover Stair,” Nemi called. “I've got someone for your stop.”

He breathed in to speak. The cloth of his shirt rose behind the slats of the stairs, pushing out a handful of sickly yellow leaves the wind must have caught there miles away. “I'll take your passenger to the top. Come along, whoever's for the Stair.”

He brought his palm around to the front door and held it there like the floor of a lift. It would be a moment's ride to the room with the lights. His other hand still covered the whole bottom tread. Nemi put the bow in her blazer pocket, where it immediately began to come out again.

Wolla came to stand by her in the door. “I need to get off at the bottom, love. I came to call my sister.”

“If she's dancing upstairs, you'll see her from my hand. I'll set you right by the window. I've done it for feet that couldn't come any other way.” He held his offered hand where she could see it. His nails were worn close and clean, and rain ran into a little cup in his palm.

Nemi said, “We got ahead of you because there's room on this side to set down the steps. Right here, where the light from the door is. I can get her off there.” She leaned out far enough to show him the flat stretch of road. Nemi could see the ends of the bricks at the edge, with darkness where the row ought to go on. She held tight to the pole. If he walked on from here, all she would have left for Wolla was the seat she'd given her in the first place.

The Dancer lowered himself far enough for the side of his face to come nearly level with the top of the bus. “I left the bottom step on the ground below that hill for years after they stopped coming from Lornet. That used to be my place to listen. A child who'd never climbed so many would count the steps up to eight, then get to ten somehow after that, and all the rest of them would cheer at ten because they could hear the child was going to make it.” He shifted the weight on his shoulder and there was a faint slither of furniture overhead. “Some nights when your bus went by I thought I heard them again.”

Nemi felt Wolla reach for the door rail. Wolla planted one foot and drew the other back. Two quick strikes of her heel on the bus floor, and then another after a space Nemi could have fitted one strike into. Wolla waited just that long again before she spoke.

“There,” she said to the Dancer. “Will you keep time for us, if I get her down? The band used to take the pause too fast and I'd have to make them wait.”

The Dancer looked down at Wolla's shoes. Mud edged the stitching where the sole met the leather. One heel was worn on a slant. There was room enough in the sound she'd made on the floor to hear where each of the three strikes had begun and ended. He brought his free hand down to the side of his knee and tried two taps with his fingertips. Nemi thought at first that he had missed the third, but he waited as long as Wolla had done and gave it its own weight.

Then he turned in place. It took him time to get the foot of Clover Stair round to the door. He held the weight of it on his forearm while he eased his fingers off the step, one by one, and Nemi stood with her hand out showing him how much room there was as the metal edge came down through the rain. It stopped at the height of an ordinary pavement outside the front door. There was a long held note from the hall. Wolla had both feet just inside the rubber strip along the floor of the 17. Nemi could have touched her shoulder without reaching.

“Is this close enough for you?” Nemi asked.

“I used to come down backwards in my best pair,” Wolla said. She put one shoe out and felt for the tread with the flat of it. When she was sure, she put the other beside it, and looked back so Nemi could see she had made it. Then she turned her face to the landing. The bottom of her coat was dark all the way round from the westerly rain that had got through the doors. Nemi wanted to call and tell her she'd wait, in case nobody came and Wolla turned and found the door shut. She looked back at the driver instead and held her hand flat. He took his hand away from the switch.

Wolla struck the tread twice. The stair took the blows all the way up with a hollow sound that was round and full even to Nemi's ears, which had always found the warning chime on a bus a little sharp. It was hard to let the pause lie there. In it Nemi could hear rain finding its way into the trough over the door, the steady swell of the idling engine, and a floorful of dancers high up coming down together on a chord. Wolla's third blow rang longer than the first two. She stood down there with her head up, and out of all the voices that came past the open doorway at the top there was one that said her name.

“Meral,” said Wolla. She caught the rail and went up a step, then the next, her whole coat swinging with her. A woman with her arms bare had come down as far as the bend. She stood where the light from the hall reached her hands and took hold of the rail too, to let Wolla come up to her. Wolla saw she was going to wait and laughed once, hard, where anybody could have heard it. She offered her hand first.

Joris glanced at Nemi past the door, and this time she nodded. There was hardly any light at the bottom of the Stair after the bus doors came together. She got hold of the ribbon that was working its way out of her pocket and raised it to the glass to give the Dancer the all-clear. He took up the Stair. The hall rose with it and came level with his shoulder again. As he turned toward the ridge he tried out the first two taps with one vast heel. He waited, and on the last beat took a stride that carried both women and the lighted hall out of reach of the road.
