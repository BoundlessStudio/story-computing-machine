# Provisional direction

The speaker crosses the uncovered square only after the Eyes of God have set beyond the coastal horizon. Through a narrow sea-facing opening in the shelter, they have watched the satellite's glint pass a fixed mark and arrive a few minutes later each day. Everyone else sees a person stepping into deadly daylight and calls it a test of faith. The speaker has let a person close to them believe that account, partly to comfort them and partly because it felt good to be thought brave.

That person, an adult with their own urgent reason to reach someone across the square, means to cross at yesterday's safe hour. Today the Eye will still be in view. The speaker offers to go in their place and asks them to wait; neither offer can make the visit for them or give them a way to travel safely the next time the speaker is absent. The speaker must confess the count and predict where and when the glint will appear before it does. The companion then has to choose what to do with the minutes they have, knowing the speaker concealed this ordinary, life-preserving knowledge for years. The satellite can still fire; an earlier strike and its human consequence should make the danger felt without using a death as an experiment.

The possible ending belongs to the companion: they check the mark for themselves and decide when to leave cover, perhaps carrying the count to the person they went to see. A correct calculation cannot recover the lost trust or guarantee that every urgent visit ends well. The story's pleasure is the simultaneous public picture of fearless faith and private attention to the changing angle of a light, followed by the moment another person can read that light without relying on the supposed saint.
