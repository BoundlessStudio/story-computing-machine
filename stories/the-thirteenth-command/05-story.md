# The Thirteenth Command

The pressure shock caught Edda Rook with one hand on the old dispatch board.

She had come into the cistern command gallery for a paper copy of one route
chart, nothing more. The southern pressure chamber had gone silent six minutes
earlier, and Sula Brant's repair crew was still inside it. Harven Coil's watch
had the upper stairs. Drouth League fighters had the lower gates. Somewhere
between them, three people waited for Edda to remember which maintenance
passage the old maps had forgotten.

Then a shot struck a pipe collar. The board flashed white. Stone rose under
Edda's boots, and the gallery ceiling came down to meet her.

In the dark, block letters opened across her sight.

`SYSTEM UPDATE: HOST FOUND`

`HOST CAPABILITIES CONFIRMED`

`SEEKING CLASS APPLICATIONS`

Records flickered behind the words: thirty-six years of flood calls, gate
orders, pump failures, evacuation grids. Her own voice, younger and quicker,
assigned crews through storms she had almost managed to forget.

`CLASS FOUND`

`APPLYING CLASS: COMBAT CONTROLLER`

No, Edda thought, although thought was all she had.

`PREVIOUS EXPERIENCE CONFIRMED`

The records accelerated. She saw four neighborhoods blinking red during the
great surge, then two workers' beacons trapped beyond a closure line. She heard
herself give the protocol order that saved the western district. She heard,
again, the silence after.

`APPLYING PREVIOUS EXPERIENCE`

`LEVEL UP`

The words repeated too quickly to count. Each repetition forced some new
arrangement into the display: priorities, routes, pressures, voices separated
into channels. Nothing touched her memory or her will. It merely indexed what
she already knew in language built for a battle.

`LEVEL 13 REACHED WITH PREVIOUS EXPERIENCE IN CLASS`

`RETENTION DECISION DEFERRED UNTIL STABILITY`

`WAKING HOST`

Edda woke with lime dust in her mouth and Harven gripping her shoulder. Blood
tracked from his temple into his gray beard, but his other hand held a rifle
steady on the lower door.

"Tell me you can see the board," he said.

The board had been dead since before Sula was an apprentice. Now pale lines
shone across its glass and echoed over Edda's ordinary vision: pipes,
galleries, and a field of uncertain red marks.

"I can see too much of it."

"Good. The south chamber seals in ten minutes. I need a Controller."

"You have a watch captain."

"I need the doors that only answer to the thing in your head."

Edda pushed herself upright. "It isn't in my head. The lattice tagged my civic
seal."

The distinction sounded thin even to her. The old command engine had applied
permissions while she lay unconscious. It was damaged cells, conduits, and
archived protocols, not a mind. Its flat notices answered no questions.

Another panel unfolded.

`TEMPORARY CLASS SESSION ACTIVE`

`MESH RANGE: COPPERWELL CISTERN`

`CELL LIFE AT CURRENT LOAD: 00:11:02`

`BEACON LINKS REQUIRE INDIVIDUAL ACCEPTANCE`

`AVAILABLE DATA: TRANSMITTED POSITION / VOICE / PULSE`

Edda looked past Harven. Two watch members crouched at the lower door, waiting
for the next League push. Beyond the wall, a pressure needle chattered hard
against its stop.

"I want Sula's route," Edda said. "You get nothing else until I have it."

Harven's jaw tightened. A thump sounded below, followed by three close shots.
"Find it while we still own the gallery."

Edda accepted the temporary session.

The world divided into layers. Old structural sensors painted pressure in
shades from cool gray to a yellow that hurt to look at. The watch beacons
appeared as requests, not people: eight points asking permission to transmit to
her. She accepted them one by one. Each point sharpened into location, voice,
and a pulse mark where the wearer allowed it.

Harven pointed to the two at the door. "League is climbing through the west
filter. Get those two behind them."

"If they agree."

He gave her a look. Edda sent the route with its source attached: survey map,
thirty-one years old. Both beacons accepted. The pair slipped through a side
arch and moved along the east spur while Edda watched their lights slide over
the map.

The route overlay exposed the east stair three levels below, its manual
spillwheel marked with an old service flag. Edda remembered eighteen jam calls
and the same remedy every time: lift before pulling. Irrelevant to the pair's
route, she thought, and left the flag in place.

It was appallingly familiar. During flood season, a city became lamps on a
board. The person at the board learned to keep the lamps moving and not imagine
their faces.

A new request flashed from beyond the southern bulkhead.

"Edda?" Sula's voice crackled into her ear. "If that's you, blink the request
twice."

Edda blinked it twice.

Sula accepted. Her beacon resolved deep in the south pressure chamber, beside
two weaker work signals. Relief struck Edda so hard she had to grip the board.

"Found you," she said.

"I hadn't misplaced myself."

Some habits, at least, had survived apprenticeship.

An alarm cut across them. The watch pair had stopped in the east spur. Their
map lights showed open corridor. Their voices reported a wall of broken stone.

"That roof came down three years ago," Sula said. "The old board never took
the repair revision."

Edda saw the League marks advancing only because a damaged stair sensor
reported weight. If she trusted the map, the pair had forty seconds. If the
sensor was wrong, she would route them toward gunfire.

She pulled the maintenance reports from her own authenticated archive, found a
handwritten waste-channel inspection, and laid it over the survey. "Back seven
paces. Hatch at knee height. It will be narrow."

"How narrow?" one of them asked.

"The record doesn't say."

They accepted anyway. One beacon vanished into sensor shadow, then the other.
For five breathless seconds Edda had only their voices and the scrape of gear.
They emerged behind the filter stair just as League fighters entered it.
Harven's pair fired into the ceiling, showering dust and stopping the advance
without trapping anyone below.

The lower door held.

"Useful," Harven said.

Edda watched the false corridor remain serenely open on the lattice map.
"Dangerous."

The cell clock read `00:09:47`.

More requests arrived. Every surviving watch beacon was requesting the mesh
now. Voices crowded Edda: a cracked valve on the north walk, movement under a
drain grate, two wounded at the ration gates, one body no one could yet reach,
pressure climbing in a pipe no one could see. The class separated the reports
by urgency, but it did not make them true. It gave confidence grades based on
old sensors and distance. It offered routes through walls that existed only on
paper.

Edda did what she had done through thirty-six wet seasons. She asked who could
verify. She repeated orders with their reasons. She sent one team to cover the
wounded and told another to abandon an empty stair Harven wanted defended.
When three voices spoke at once, she narrowed them into separate threads and
felt the effort as a band tightening behind her eyes.

Sula's chamber remained the fixed red center of every choice.

"The south gate is twisting in its track," Sula reported. Metal groaned behind
her words. "If we brace the lower hinge, we can reach the service crawl."

The lattice drew a predicted collapse around the chamber. Sixty-eight percent
under present pressure. Eighty-one if the lower hinge took another shock.

Edda muted the forecast from Sula's link.

"Brace it," she said. "Then take the crawl."

Sula did not move. Her beacon stayed exactly where it was.

"What did you just hide?"

"Nothing you need while you're under the gate."

"Then it wasn't nothing."

Harven was issuing orders beside Edda, preparing another push. The gallery
shook. The forecast climbed to seventy-three percent.

"Edda," Sula said, quieter. "Tell me the red number."

Years ago, two workers had asked whether the western closure would reopen in
time. Edda had given them the approved reassurance because panic interfered
with clean dispatch. She had kept her voice level until their beacons went out.

"Seventy-three percent chance of chamber failure if you load the lower hinge,"
she said. "The model is using an obsolete gate profile. It could be badly
wrong."

"Alternatives?"

"Stay where you are and the automatic seal reaches you in under eight minutes.
Force the service crawl and the gate may come down sooner. I don't have a safe
route."

Sula took one audible breath. "The waste vent was widened last dry month. It
isn't on your archive because the work hasn't been certified. We can take the
brace through there and set it from the far side."

Edda found the vent on the old plan, too small for shoulders and thirty years
out of date. "Can all three of you fit?"

"We are prepared to find out."

"I need better than that."

"No. You need honest uncertainty. That's what you have."

Sula transmitted a rough measurement from her work slate. Edda added it to the
map as a current report, clearly marked unverified. The forecast dropped to
forty-six percent.

"Your choice," Edda said. "I recommend the vent."

"Accepted."

The three beacons began to move.

The pressure needle struck its stop again. This time Edda felt the rhythm
through the soles of her boots: three fast impacts, a pause, then two. It was
not the random battering of shots. It was a feedback surge, the kind that
developed when gates at opposite ends of a water system fought one another.

She stripped the troop routes from her display and spread the structural
telemetry wide. The lattice highlighted the main cistern wall as sound because
its oldest sensors said so. Edda ignored the comforting green. She watched the
delays between the north walk and south chamber, counted the beats, and
remembered a pump station that had split before its gauges admitted anything
was wrong.

"Stop firing," she said.

Harven looked over. "Tell them."

"Everyone. Both sides. The concussions are feeding a pressure cascade."

"The League has the west gallery. If my people stop, they reach the ration
controls."

"If nobody stops, the main baffle tears loose. After that, the cistern wall
gets the full wave."

The lattice assembled her observations into a forecast. It had wide margins
and too many amber sources.

`PROBABLE CASCADE WINDOW: 00:05:31–00:07:04`

Harven read it from the board. His face changed, but not enough. "Then we use
the scour gates now. Seal the south chamber, open the gallery sluice, and drive
the League out before the baffle goes."

The class offered the same template. Blue arrows closed the south bulkhead.
Water scoured the disputed gallery. Enemy beacons disappeared from the model.
So did Sula and the two repair workers.

"The pressure wave will pick up silt from the lower bed," Edda said. "You may
save a cracked cistern full of mud."

"May. Your forecast may also be wrong. Mine ends an armed assault in ninety
seconds."

His plan was not cowardice. Harven checked the chamber of his rifle, then
gestured his squad toward the east stair. He meant to go down with them. He
simply knew which deaths he could put in the acceptable column.

A burst of open-band static crossed the lattice. Then an unfamiliar voice,
strained and breathless.

"Copperwell watch, open the ration channels. East bank has days, not weeks. We
did not come for your city."

The League signal lead was broadcasting from somewhere near the west gallery.
Harven reached for the cutoff.

"Leave it," Edda said.

"They came with rifles."

"And you mean to answer with a flood."

"I mean to keep water in Copperwell."

On the board, both victories ended in the same broken blue shape. The League
could seize the ration controls and tear open gates against full pressure. The
watch could scour the gallery and drive the cascade into the south wall.
Neither side had the other's sensor data. Each believed the cistern would
survive long enough to win.

A notice opened beneath the forecast.

`LEVEL 13 PROTOCOL AVAILABLE`

`COMMAND 13: COMMON CAUSE`

`REQUIREMENT: ONE DECLARED OBJECTIVE`

`HOSTILE BEACONS MAY ACCEPT / REFUSE / WITHDRAW`

`ACCEPTED TACTICAL DATA SHARED SYMMETRICALLY`

`ESTIMATED CELL EXPENDITURE: ALL OPERATIONAL RESERVE`

Edda opened the detail. Common Cause would invite every beacon in lattice
range, including those tagged hostile. It would share accepted positions,
voices, hazard readings, and every route Edda published. No hidden team. No
private map. Before acceptance, an invite showed only the declared objective
and whatever structural data Edda chose to publish; no live beacon data crossed
the field. No compulsion. Anyone could refuse, lie through an unlinked channel,
or use the knowledge against them.

The cell clock read `00:05:58`.

"Run the scour template," Harven said.

"No."

"That is a lawful emergency order."

"Then put my refusal in your incident report."

"Sula is dead if that chamber seals. I know. But if we lose the cistern,
thousands queue at dry taps tomorrow."

Edda searched for a private route, some clever thread that could save Sula
without showing the League Copperwell's defenses. Every version failed at the
same place: the jammed south gate needed hands on both sides, and the manual
spillwheel on the east stair had to turn at the exact moment the ration
channels vented. No one side controlled all three.

Abstaining would not keep her hands clean. It would only leave Harven to name
the cost.

Every command had a cost. The honest ones carried it in plain sight.

Harven turned toward the stair. "I will do it without the lattice."

He withdrew his beacon link. Each squad member's beacon registered a separate
withdrawal after his, the last pausing for one long second before it vanished.
Harven could lead them toward the stair. He could not choose their links for
them.

Edda selected Command Thirteen.

"Objective," the open channel prompted in flat text.

She spoke to Copperwell's watch, Sula's crew, and the people who had entered the
cistern with rifles because their settlements were going dry.

"Nobody drowns. The cistern holds."

She published the cascade forecast with its uncertainty. She published the
obsolete structural maps, an uncredited correction to the waste vent's
dimensions, and the fixed locations of the ration controls, south gate, and
manual spillwheel. No person's position appeared. She published the cost: the
remaining lattice cells, the city's tactical secrecy, and the chance that
anyone accepting might still betray the rest.

"I cannot make you agree," she said. "If you accept, everyone in the field sees
what you transmit. If you refuse, the doors stay open for you to leave. If we
keep fighting, the wall likely fails before six minutes. Those are the facts I
have. They are not guarantees."

For one long second, no beacon changed.

Then Sula accepted.

Only then did her light turn white at the center of the south chamber. Each of
the two repair workers accepted in turn and appeared beside her. Four watch
beacons joined on separate confirmations, then two more. Across the west
gallery, League signals appeared for the first time, scattered among the old
pipes. Five accepted individually. Three did not. One accepted, withdrew at
once, and disappeared down the lower stair.

Harven remained absent.

The shared field filled Edda's sight. Voices struck together, and the band
behind her eyes cinched until the edges of the gallery went dark.

"Sula, will you take the south node?"

The request contained authority only over information Sula's group chose to
route.

"Accepted," Sula said.

The map divided. Sula took the gate brace, combining her current measurements
with a League report from its far side. Edda assigned the neutral maintenance
walk for moving wounded people away from the west gallery. Watch and League
members entered it from opposite ends, weapons slung but hands never far from
them.

"Brace team needs four," Sula said. "Two each side. The hinge will jump on the
first vent."

Two League beacons and two watch beacons moved toward the jammed gate. They
could see one another now. For a terrible moment, nobody crossed the final
corner.

Then a watch member shoved the timber brace across the wet floor. A League
fighter caught it. Four sets of hands lifted together while the gate bucked
above them.

The signal lead transmitted a pressure reading from the west controls. It
contradicted the lattice by almost a quarter.

"Sensor age?" Edda asked.

"Hand gauge. Reading now."

Edda marked it current, human-reported. The cascade window shortened.

`00:02:41–00:03:18`

Her right hand began to tremble. The class prioritized a red surge at the north
walk, and she nearly ordered an immediate vent before Sula cut across her.

"No. If north vents before the brace seats, the south gate slams."

Edda had missed the hinge-load marker. It sat at the edge of her narrowing
vision.

"Correct," she said. The word tasted like fear. "South node controls pulse
timing."

She delegated it. The display quieted by one degree.

At the neutral walk, someone fired. The report cracked through every accepted
link. Beacons froze.

"Shot went high," a watch voice said.

"Refused beacon at the drain," the League signal lead answered. "Not in the
field."

Common Cause had stopped nothing. It had only made the choice visible.

"Move the wounded," Edda said. "Anyone unwilling, withdraw by the lower
stairs. Nobody pursues."

Two beacons left the field. The others began moving again. They carried the
wounded past one another through ankle-deep water while the pressure climbed.

"Brace seated," Sula called. "Ready for first pulse."

"Spillwheel?" Edda asked.

Silence.

Harven and the three squad members had been nearest when they withdrew. Without
the manual wheel, the vent sequence would hammer shut against trapped pressure.
Edda sent the invitation again, not as an order but with Sula's timing, the
shortened window, and the same objective.

The lattice cells fell under one minute.

One squad member accepted and appeared beside the spillwheel. A second
accepted. Then the third. Each beacon carried its own confirmation; Harven's
position remained dark.

"Harven," she said on the open band. "I do not need you to trust the League. I
need you to decide whether the objective is true."

Nothing.

Then the watch captain's beacon reappeared on the east stair.

He accepted.

His position resolved among the three waiting squad members, three levels below
the gallery. Harven's pulse ran fast. His voice did not.

"Wheel is jammed."

"It always sticks before the first quarter turn," Edda said. She had dispatched
maintenance there eighteen times. "Lift, then pull. Do not force it downward."

On Sula's count, the south team locked the brace. Harven and the three squad
members heaved the wheel upward. Across the west gallery, League fighters
opened one ration channel by a hand's breadth.

"First pulse," Sula said.

Water struck the pipes with a sound like a building taking breath. The floor
tilted. One of the south beacons winked out, not withdrawn but lost behind a
sensor shadow.

Edda reached for it and lost the timing thread.

"I have the gate," Sula said. "Watch the wall."

Edda obeyed. She watched the delayed pressure readings ripple north. The old
sensors lied high, the hand gauge read low, and the truth moved somewhere
between. She gave Harven the range rather than a clean number.

"Second pull between four and six seconds. Your judgment."

"Understood."

He chose five.

The spillwheel turned. The ration channel opened another hand. The brace team
shouted as the gate jumped, but it held. A deep wave passed under the gallery,
lifting dust from the ceiling in pale sheets. Edda's vision shrank to the thin
blue curve of the cistern wall.

The curve flexed.

It did not break.

Pressure emptied eastward through the ration channels, not as the torrent the
League had tried to seize, nor as the weapon Harven had meant to release, but
in measured, muddy pulses. The baffle's rhythm slowed. Three fast impacts
became two, then one, then the ordinary groan of old stone under water.

Sula's missing beacon returned.

"We are all here," she said.

Edda sat down hard against the dispatch board.

The cell clock showed nineteen seconds.

No one called the ceasefire a peace. The watch kept the upper gallery. The
League kept the west ration controls. Between them, wounded people lay on the
same maintenance walk while the jointly opened channel carried a narrow stream
toward the east bank.

Harven climbed back into the command gallery, wet to the shoulders. He set his
rifle against the wall rather than in his hands.

"They will want the full gates open," he said.

"Copperwell will want them shut."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow remains difficult."

The lattice offered one final set of choices.

`STABILITY THRESHOLD REACHED`

`EXPORT SYMMETRIC INCIDENT LOG?`

Edda sent a copy to the civic archive and to every beacon that had accepted
Common Cause. The log listed the League assault, Harven's scour order, her
refusal, the map errors, the uncertainties, the withdrawals, the wounded, and
the person who had died before the shared field opened. It would not create
justice. It would make a bloodless victory harder to invent.

`RETAIN COMBAT CONTROLLER CLASS AS PERMANENT PROFILE?`

Edda selected discard.

The overlays vanished. The world became stone, bad light, human voices, and a
board darkening beneath her hand.

Sula emerged from the south passage after dawn, limping slightly and furious
about the state of the gate. She embraced Edda once, hard, then stepped back.

"The city will build another command board," she said.

"Harven will."

"A civilian one. Repair crews, ration stewards, east-bank signals if they
choose. Current maps. Shared costs."

Edda looked across the gallery. Watch members and League fighters stood apart
again, negotiating how long the narrow channel would remain open. No glowing
field made them cooperate. They had only a cracked cistern, an honest record,
and the memory of whose hands had held the gate.

"They will ignore half our commands," Edda said.

"Then we will have to make better ones."

Edda had spent years believing that leaving the board meant she no longer chose
who carried the danger. The cistern had cured her of that vanity.

"One rule," she said. "Every command carries its cost beside it."

Sula offered her an arm for the broken stairs.

Together they climbed toward the argument waiting in the daylight.
