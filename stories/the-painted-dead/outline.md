# The Painted Dead — provisional direction

## The experience promised

The explicit premise is a realm where everyone sees the supernatural, the dead have skeletal faces and walk openly, and an extraordinary living woman has passed as dead for three hundred years by painting her face. Her history includes dancing with forgotten kings, attending civilizations' funerals, and becoming beloved in the underworld. A newly appointed male Reaper first discovers the deception; his attempt to claim her soul reveals why she hid. The anticipated pleasure I infer is a reversal of apparent authority: the novice meets a woman with a vast, stylish, consequential history, and his discovery makes that history mean something different. The language suggests macabre elegance, wonder, accumulated grief and wit; it does not prescribe romance, a maternal bond, an antagonist, a rescue, her powers, or the ending. The supplied portrait suggests red hair, elaborate black-and-white skull paint, a broad black hat, black lace, and self-possession. Attached instructions remain production guidance, not additional story requirements.

## Chosen direction, open to revision

A gothic liberation story, approximately 4,000–5,000 words, mostly close to the woman, Belisane, with the new Reaper Tereth's discovery legible in action. Belisane possesses the rare living gift of recalling a recently dead person into bodily life. The living sorcerer-king Azravan bound her to his own agelessness and used that gift to make conquest repeatable: soldiers and servants could not escape him by dying. She escaped his custody, but the old working still joins their souls and keeps both bodies from dying. Taking hold of her soul exposes the other end of that working and gives him a route to find her. She has consequently hidden from Reapers as well as royal hunters, although death itself is no horror to her. Reapers leave the already-dead alone, and the king's hunters seek a living resurrectionist; the visible skull makes both searches pass her by. Among the dead she has made a life in which nobody knows her as an instrument of resurrection.

These are invented local mechanics, not universal rules. Tereth is a living person newly invested with a Reaper's office; his own living agency powers his scythe. The dead retain their identities and can handle ordinary objects but cannot cast magic. The underworld here is one society and manifestation of the underlying afterlife. Its inhabitants routinely decorate their bone faces with paint, veils, wax cheek pieces and festival masks; her painting, gloves and dress belong to a culture of elaborate faces rather than requiring three centuries of universal inattention. No one has previously suspected that she is alive.

## Concrete movement

1. **The forgotten king's dance.** In the Low City's ballroom, Belisane teaches a king whose country and language have vanished to stop counting military steps in a dance. They have a specific old quarrel about his dancing and she knows the musicians' missing verses. Their talk establishes that she has been his friend for three hundred years. Living visitors and skeletal dead mingle without pretending the supernatural is secret. Her welcome is affectionate and irreverent, earned over centuries. Tereth arrives to make his first rounds. A pin catches her cheek while she rescues his dropped ceremonial hood from a dancer's heel; the paint parts over living skin. He notices a pulse when he catches her wrist. She recognizes the danger before he does and leaves. He has discovered an apparently dead person who has been alive for three hundred years, not merely a living visitor.

2. **A civilization's funeral.** He follows her through a procession for a culture whose last living speaker has just died. She has attended several such funerals across the three hundred years and joins its final public song in a language Tereth cannot recognize. Its dead already know her; the last speaker is joining a society that has welcomed her for generations. Tereth's training has taught him to recognize artificially prolonged dying: he sees an old binding taut beneath the apparent dead face and misdiagnoses a soul that has been prevented from completing death. His first offer is to free her, not to punish an ordinary visitor or enforce an invented universal lifespan. She refuses. He believes her fear of the binding is fear of its maker, assures her that he can cut the claimant away, and starts the collection. Her warning that taking hold will bring the king reaches him as the claim exposes the second end. His novice error is confidence in a diagnosis, not knowingly ignoring a fully explained mechanism.

3. **What his scythe catches.** His living power draws her soul and the binding into view: two joined loops, one entering her, the other running back to Azravan. A movement of the scythe pulls on both; cutting blindly would take both souls. The claim gives Tereth direct, bounded glimpses of what the binding was used for: a soldier hauled back from his family's funeral, Belisane refusing another recall, and her flight from the king. It does not grant him her complete memory. Her practiced control of inward soul movement stops her own extraction. This plants both her knowledge and the exposed second loop before the climax. Azravan's distant voice comes through the caught binding and names her old office. Tereth understands that her continued life is real, not a death he should complete, and that she has been a fugitive from enforced life. He stops trying to take her.

4. **The king comes to collect.** Azravan follows the opened route bodily into the underworld to reclaim his resurrectionist. Belisane has to decide whether to escape into anonymity again or use this first physical encounter in three centuries to end his claim. She chooses to stay and asks Tereth to leave the binding exposed and give her the scythe. He could escape the danger by abandoning the claim and his tool; instead, the glimpses of compelled resurrection lead him to trust her choice about her own soul. Their exchange explicitly reverses his opening assumption that he knows what freedom she needs. The forgotten king uses the military count she knows from their dances to move their friends together; she can anticipate his turn without exposing her own movement. The funeral bearers lower their heavy ceremonial cloth over Azravan's reaching arm at her request. Particular people and shared habits buy her the interval; the dead do no spellcasting. She tells them who has come for her, and their actions answer the exposed living face.

5. **Her claim.** The scythe is an instrument that living users shape with their power, as Tereth's first attempt has shown. Belisane supplies her own. She has spent centuries feeling the binding from within; with both loops exposed by his unfinished claim, she holds her own soul inward as she did in beat 3, separates it from Azravan's loop, and cuts his loop alone. The working that keeps their bodies ageless breaks. She uses the scythe to carry his newly mortal soul outward before he can make another binding. No new power to reverse a route appears here: the tool does the taking, her established gift holds her own soul safely within, and her knowledge selects the cut. The decisive knowledge, risk and action are hers; Tereth's changed judgment keeps the opportunity available. The king acquires a skeletal face among people he once refused the right to remain dead. No soul is erased. His inability to cast after death makes his defeat lasting, and the dead take him into their own custody.

6. **An unhidden face.** The binding's destruction ends her imposed agelessness; she remains alive and will age ordinarily. She owes the underworld no new service in exchange for sanctuary. Before returning his scythe, she and Tereth acknowledge what his attempted rescue nearly cost and what his decision to listen allowed; he will wait until she actually dies. Back at the interrupted dance, she washes away the rest of the paint. Her friends' loyalty is already proven; the unfamiliar thing is being seen with the living face she has kept private for three hundred years. The forgotten king resumes their old argument about the steps and offers his hand. She dances among her friends in the life she chose, with a future that finally belongs to her.

The plot's central tension is whether discovering her living soul will restore the ownership she escaped. The revelation changes the immediate danger and gives her a chance to end it. Her old friendships, the public reality of the dead, the limits of dead magic, and the material act of painting her living face all have causal jobs. The anticipated ending rewards her centuries of chosen belonging and her successful refusal of exploitation, not the Reaper's rescue or her martyrdom.
