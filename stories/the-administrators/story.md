# The Administrators

Hedran paid two silver pennies to light the Emperor's world.

It lay beneath the Council Hall's glass floor: mountains in hammered bronze, provinces in colored enamel, conquered cities picked out with garnets. She selected Illumination from the registry, signed the authorization with her thumb, and dropped the coins into the Treasury slot. Only when the little disk on her wrist displayed all three seals did she let a thread of her strength enter the network.

Light ran through the map. Twelve Archmages, seated around its circumference, could now read the year's tribute figures.

A third penny slipped from her purse. Her youngest apprentice bent to retrieve it.

“Keep it,” Hedran said.

Outside, the morning traffic rose between the palace's detached terraces. Licensed coachmen steered their passengers along blue lanes in the air. Six skyroad arches stood on the outer terraces, facing six far regions of the Empire. A woman on a delivery kite stopped at the eastern arch, held up a permit, and waited while its keeper checked the fee. The arch admitted her into a narrow passage of white light and closed again.

Once, according to the oldest maps, those arches had opened wide enough for caravans. Hedran had never seen one so wide. She knew the distant cities by their garnets, their assessments, and the permissible pictures sent with their governors' reports.

The apprentice straightened with the penny. Then he looked past her and said, “Whose procession is that?”

Six people stood above the Imperial capital.

They had appeared between one coach and the next. No flare of passage marked their arrival. They stood on nothing at all, high enough to look across the Council gallery's balustrade, with the palace's tallest banner snapping below their feet.

Hedran put two fingers to her wrist disk. No incoming procession had been registered. Nor had any spell that might be holding them up. She checked the global ledger for a payment, then an authorization. Nothing.

A sorcerer could refuse to register, authorize, or pay. No one had ever made anything happen afterward.

The foremost stranger wore a broad black hat and a red cloak, with a heavy chain resting on her fitted armor. A sword hung at her hip. Beside her floated a figure whose hood concealed everything but a few small amber lights; a white ring turned above two raised fingers.

The other four looked like people assembled around quite different ideas of a body. One dark-haired woman wore an intricate metal headpiece, with chains descending beside her face and amber seams running down her exposed arms. A huge man had white plates over his shoulders and a yellow harness against his bare chest. Another woman, fair-haired beneath a pointed hat, flexed a red metal hand. The last carried a wired staff; his long blond hair blew across dark glasses, though he made no attempt to push it away.

None bore a visible Imperial license.

“Coachmen,” said the Archmage opposite Hedran, “are becoming very ambitious.”

He sent an identification query.

Hedran saw his wrist disk brighten. He touched it again. The amusement left his face.

She queried the woman with the sword.

**SATHRA. ADMINISTRATOR.**

Below the name, where a caster's grade and issuing authority ought to have appeared, there was only a small, empty circle.

She tried the hooded figure.

**NHAL. ADMINISTRATOR.**

The woman with the metal headpiece was Vesra. The great man was Brannoc. The red hand belonged to Tavren; the staff to Ossan.

All six answers carried the same designation.

Around the table, the other Archmages were making their own inquiries. Twelve sets of instruments agreed.

“Administrators of what?” someone said.

Hedran opened the category rather than the names.

For the first time in her forty-one years of using it, the disk unfolded a second rim. Fine silver letters spread across her sleeve.

**ADMINISTRATORS: SIX PRESENT.**

The list of privileges ran past her elbow.

She stood up.

Outside, Sathra turned her head toward Vesra and said, “Living channels first.”

The words sounded as clearly as if she had spoken from the next chair.

Vesra spread her mechanical fingers. Red characters slipped from them and vanished down into the city. Ossan rested two fingers on the head of his staff, listening. The broad man moved closer to Vesra, bringing his yellow-lit fist beneath her outstretched hand.

“They are attaching themselves to the network,” Hedran said.

“Revoke their access.”

The Emperor had entered without announcement. He seldom needed one. Everyone who could see him got out of his way, and those who could not see him took warning from the movement.

He crossed the illuminated world, his slippers passing over the provinces. Hedran bowed and submitted the arrest pattern already waiting in her disk. Restraint, grade twelve. Authorized by the Council. Fee charged to the sovereign reserve.

The first two seals lit. The third remained dark.

Then the authorization seal went out.

**INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.**

Hedran had seen those words on apprentices' instruments. She had never seen them on hers.

“You entered it incorrectly,” said the Emperor.

“No, Majesty.”

“Then use mine.”

He laid his signet against the disk. Gold light filled its outer rim. The sovereign grant appeared, with its familiar titles and its authority over every Imperial caster.

The answer did not change.

The Emperor looked up at the six strangers. Sathra was watching him.

“Come down,” he said.

“You have a perfectly good gallery,” she replied.

He went out onto it.

The Archmages followed. Hedran brought her apprentice because he carried the large inquiry lens, and because leaving him alone with the extended privilege list seemed unwise.

From the gallery, they could hear the capital noticing the six. Coach bells rang. People crowded balconies and leaned from windows. The traffic wardens had stopped the blue lanes around the palace, but the strangers' clothes moved in the ordinary morning wind.

“This is the seat of the Imperial authority,” said the Emperor. “You will identify yourselves.”

Tavren glanced at the Emperor's signet. “Has the directory stopped working?”

“It has been interfered with.”

The hood tilted. Hedran could see no eyes inside it.

“That,” Nhal said, “is why we're here.”

The Emperor extended his hand toward the Council rail.

Every Archmage knew the gesture. Hedran had practiced responding to it since before she earned her first key. She set her palm on the bronze, and the others did the same. The apprentices knelt at the smaller focuses below it.

A diagram opened over the rail: the approved pattern for the Spear of Noon. Twelve living casters, one target group. The price appeared beneath it. The Emperor touched his signet to the charge; a pale line crossed the diagram as the Treasury acknowledged payment.

The arrest had tried to seize the strangers through their own channels. The Spear needed no access to those. It would burn the space they occupied.

Hedran drew a breath. Her shoulders tightened as her strength entered the common channel. Sun-colored fire gathered above the balustrade.

Sathra looked at Brannoc.

“Stay with Vesra.”

He had begun to smile. At her words he lowered his fist again, and the yellow light around his knuckles poured into the white plates on his shoulders.

The Spear launched.

Nhal brought the white ring down over it.

There was no impact. The spear's brilliant length vanished through the ring, though its tail was still feeding from the Council rail. Nhal turned a wrist, and the far end emerged into a second ring above the empty parade ground.

The spear entered the first circle and came out of the second, bent back, and entered the first again.

Hedran felt the whole completed spell still pulling against her palm. She could see it moving. It simply had nowhere to arrive.

“Release,” she told the others.

Their hands came away. The light remained captive in its narrow orbit.

Tavren stepped across the air to Nhal's side. A small lens opened in the center of her red palm. She held it against the passing fire, and blue lines spread out of the light: the spell's registered structure, every targeting clause and purchased permission.

She enlarged it until the diagram hung over the palace roofs.

The crowd went quiet.

“Twelve casters,” Tavren said. “Six targets. Civilian surroundings.”

“You are attacking the capital,” said the Emperor.

Tavren turned the diagram toward the street below.

At its foot, in letters taller than the people reading them, shone the purchaser's name.

Sathra reached into the exposed pattern. Her black fingers closed on a golden line, and she drew it out, hand over hand. Successive grants flowered along it: the Archmages' battlefield authority, the Council's delegated seal, the sovereign war license.

At the end of the line appeared a crown.

Above the crown, connected by a short stem, was the same empty circle Hedran had seen beneath Sathra's name.

“What is that?” the Emperor demanded.

Hedran was already searching.

The empty circle opened on her disk. She set it against the apprentice's lens, magnifying the oldest entry until she could read the faint characters beneath the Imperial commentary.

**REGIONAL LICENSE. CROWN AUTHORITY.**

There were boundaries. There were conditions. There was an issuing grant.

The original boundary enclosed one city and its skyroad stations. Imperial renewals had drawn it outward, conquest by conquest, until every ordinary caster's route into the network passed through the Crown.

“A license,” she said.

“I can see that.”

She followed the grant upward.

The Imperial dates fell away. Earlier notations passed beneath her fingertips: unfamiliar calendars, overwritten translations, an archive of repairs. Then the query reached six signatures, each accompanied by the diagram of a living nervous system.

At the top was one word.

**ARCHITECTS.**

The six names matched the six in the sky.

Vesra's diagram had branching interfaces down both arms. Brannoc's marked a broad circuit across the shoulders and through the spine. The neural key beneath Nhal's hood was stranger than any anatomy Hedran had studied, but the disk authenticated its continuity with the figure before her.

The entry predated the first Emperor by nine hundred years.

Her apprentice leaned closer.

“Are those their licenses?”

Hedran moved the lens so the Emperor could see it.

“They issued ours.”

He struck it out of her hand.

The apprentice caught the frame against his chest. The lens cracked. Its great blue image wavered but held.

Across the sky, Sathra released the golden line. The public receipt remained.

“Where have you been?” Hedran asked her.

Sathra's eyes moved briefly to the oldest grant still visible in the broken lens.

“We couldn't come back the way we left.”

Ossan lifted his head.

“Sathra. He's closed the outer routes.”

At the eastern arch, the passage used by the delivery kite snapped out. The other skyroads darkened in succession. On Hedran's disk, the governor channels changed from blue to sovereign gold.

“Everyone,” said the Emperor, “belongs somewhere.”

Vesra made a small, sharp movement with one finger. A red filament broke loose from a gold one. Far below, a coach dipped in its lane, then steadied. The man at its controls clutched his focus with both hands.

“You nearly dropped them,” Hedran said.

Vesra looked directly at her.

“Watch the other end.”

Hedran followed the filament through her inquiry.

The small registry picture of the coach opened on her sleeve: seven passengers, one caster, a Crown permission threaded through the center of the lift spell. Another red filament passed it, carrying the pattern of a healer's working. Then another. Some were simple warming charms. Some held delicate forces around bodies in treatment. Three broad bands rose from the palace's own suspended terraces.

Vesra was drawing each golden permission out without breaking the living spell around it. Her fingers separated into more joints than Hedran's eyes could follow. Brannoc held his fist beneath the growing knot of lines; the light passed through his harness, down his arms, and returned along Vesra's wrists.

Ossan spoke place names into his staff. After each one, a faint answering note reached the gallery.

All through the city, gold threads were turning white.

Hedran understood what an immediate revocation would have done. The coach. The healers. The people on the detached terraces, including the nursery beyond the Emperor's rooms. A cut through the Crown's license would have broken every spell that used its permission.

The six were removing it one connection at a time.

“Majesty,” she said, “we should not cast again.”

He did not answer. His thumb was pressed hard against the face of his signet.

“They have not hurt anyone,” she said.

“They are taking my network.”

Hedran looked at the original grant still wavering in the apprentice's lens.

The Emperor caught the movement.

“Hold your stations.”

He pressed his signet into the golden socket at the end of the rail.

The terrace shuddered.

Hedran knew the pattern that opened beneath his hand. She had helped write its Imperial authorization. Invasion, insurrection, loss of the capital: in those circumstances the sovereign could requisition the magical strength of connected subjects. They had called it Sovereign Recall. In committee, its lines had been beautifully clean.

The price filled the width of the rail.

The Emperor confirmed it.

Beyond the gallery, the Treasury tower's windows flashed gold, floor after floor, as the charge emptied the sovereign reserve. All three seals closed around the pattern.

“Majesty, the active channels—”

“All of them.”

He began to cast.

Every golden filament reversed.

The coach dropped a hand's breadth. Its driver cried out. At the palace rail, one of the Archmages fell to a knee, unable to lift her hand from the focus. The apprentices' smaller disks flared white.

On Hedran's sleeve, a thousand registered workings opened at once. A woman in a room full of sleeping children. A man steadying a suspended beam. An old healer with one hand inside a luminous cast. Their faces were tiny, their connections impossibly bright.

The network carried what the Emperor had ordered. Living strength rushed toward the capital from every direction.

Nhal snapped the ring shut around the captured spear. It shrank to a white point and went out between two black fingers.

“Isolate?” Brannoc asked.

“They're still holding people,” Vesra said.

He looked down. The coach was falling again.

“Then give it here.”

Sathra caught his shoulder as he stepped forward. She said something Hedran could not hear, her mouth close to his ear.

Brannoc nodded once.

He opened both hands.

The current that should have struck the six bent toward him. It entered the white plates on his shoulders in two broad rivers, lighting the seams around his collar, his ribs, his wrists. His long hair lifted from his back. The yellow lines of his harness shone through the glare.

The coach stopped falling.

Across the city, hundreds of interrupted spells caught and held. The suspended palace terraces settled level with one another. All their swaying weight passed through the same blazing arc between Brannoc's hands.

Hedran could hear him breathing.

Tavren threw back the sleeve above her red wrist. A cable unwound from beneath the plating and fastened to Vesra's forearm. Ossan set the foot of his staff against Brannoc's heel; white light raced up its length and crossed the wires at its head.

The mass of gold over Brannoc's shoulders spread into a vast sphere. Lines curved across it. Hedran recognized the world's northern coast, then the southern islands, then continents she had never seen except under glass. The Recall had made every requisitioned channel visible. The sphere turned, revealing the far hemisphere in night.

Tavren put her red hand into it.

The lens in her palm opened wide.

Around the gallery, the Emperor's voices multiplied. The Recall carried his command into every connected focus, so that it came from the rail, the apprentices' disks, the wrist of the Archmage on her knees.

“Surrender.”

Brannoc bared his teeth. Whether in a grin or effort Hedran could not tell.

“Your return address,” Tavren said to the Emperor, “is very badly designed.”

She closed her fist around the center of his spell and turned it.

The gold lines began to bend back toward the places they had come from.

“Not yet,” Ossan said.

His staff gave a low, broken note. He turned his head, listening through the dark lenses.

Tavren stopped with her wrist half turned.

“How far?” Sathra asked.

“Far side. Under the night. One coach beyond the cities, still on the Crown. Cut now and it falls.”

The remote entry flickered on Hedran's sleeve: a coach crossing a black mountain pass, its passengers asleep against one another. The driver held a gold-lit focus. No white return line had reached him.

Brannoc's arms moved farther apart. The sphere swelled; a bright thread climbed his throat, and his breath emerged through his teeth.

“Reach them,” Sathra said.

Ossan knelt in the air and put his forehead against the staff. All the little wires around its head stood straight.

Below him, the gold filament of a palace support whipped taut. Vesra caught it between two fingers and fed it into Brannoc's arc. The nursery terrace trembled, then held.

Nhal was no longer beside them.

The hooded figure stood over the Council Hall, looking down through its roof. White rings expanded from both hands. They passed through stone without breaking it, enclosing a shaft straight down into the palace.

Hedran knew what lay beneath the Hall. She had descended there once at her investiture: three sealed floors, twelve guarded doors, then the Crown conductor, a vast metal tree around which every Imperial permission was wound.

The Emperor saw the widening circles.

“Protect the heart,” he said. “All of you. Fire.”

The apprentice beside Hedran reached for his small focus.

She seized his wrist.

His first brief contact had already opened the channel. White light climbed his fingers. He made a surprised sound, as though someone had taken the floor from beneath him.

The Recall was drawing hardest through the widest open connections. Every attempt to launch a spell gave it another mouth.

“Hands away,” she said. “Everyone off the focuses.”

“Archmage.”

She had heard that tone from the Emperor before. People generally obeyed it once.

Hedran took the cracked lens from her apprentice and pushed him toward the gallery doors.

“Inside. Take the others.”

He hesitated at the threshold.

“Go.”

She held the door while the apprentices stumbled through. One had to be helped by two of the others. The white glare faded from their hands as they broke contact. Hedran remained in the doorway, with the damaged lens braced against its stone jamb.

The Emperor was still at the rail. His thumb had not left the signet. The Archmage on her knees crawled toward the door.

Beyond him, the palace opened.

Its stones did not fall. Nhal drew the enclosed shaft sideways as if opening a tall, narrow door. Hedran could see the three floors, the twelve doors, the sealed chamber, all turned toward the morning. The people stationed inside clung to their desks. Their lamps still burned upright.

At the bottom of that impossible view stood the metal tree.

Its branches carried innumerable gold loops. Some had already turned white under Vesra's hands. At its trunk, the conductor narrowed to the thickness of a man's waist before entering the foundation.

Sathra passed through Nhal's lowest ring and stepped onto the chamber floor. She drew her sword.

The blade had a dark seam running along it. When her mechanical fingers tightened on the hilt, the seam filled with the same white light that shone in the six original signatures.

“I have them,” Ossan said.

In the sphere above Brannoc, a last remote line came clear. On Hedran's sleeve, the night coach's connection turned white. The broken note from the staff resolved.

“Vesra?” Sathra asked.

The woman with the chained headpiece looked through the blazing web toward the hovering coach, the healers, the palace terraces. Her amber-lit fingers moved once more.

“Held.”

Sathra glanced back at Brannoc.

He nodded without taking a breath.

“Now.”

Tavren completed the turn of her wrist.

Sathra cut through the trunk.

For an instant the capital was full of gold leaves.

They blew out of the opened heart in a silent storm, each one a severed permission. The Emperor reached toward them with his free hand. They passed through his fingers, fading.

Above him the world turned white.

The immense current left Brannoc in spreading rings, following the routes Tavren had opened. It passed through Vesra's fingers and Ossan's staff, through the pale lines that now reached every connected caster. On Hedran's sleeve, the healer's hand steadied. The woman among the sleeping children raised her head. The coachman pulled his focus against his chest and brought his passengers level with the blue lane.

The palace did not drop.

Brannoc lowered his hands slowly. A last broad band of light left his shoulders and settled around the detached terraces. Only then did he bend, put his palms on his knees, and let out a long breath.

Vesra caught the back of his harness before he could drift.

“Elegant,” she said.

“You wanted them held.”

She kept her hand where it was.

Nhal closed the palace around its rooms. The roof returned to its place above the Hall; the guards and their desks vanished behind stone. Hedran felt the broken lens become only a piece of glass in her hands.

The Emperor lifted his signet from the rail.

“Reinstate sovereign authority.”

His voice came from his own mouth alone.

He struck the socket with the ring.

The wrist disk of the crawling Archmage answered him, plainly enough for Hedran to read.

**AUTHORITY TO COMMAND ANOTHER CASTER: NOT GRANTED.**

“I am the Emperor.”

No new text appeared.

Tavren pulled the cable from Vesra's arm. The red plating closed around it. Ossan stood and shook his staff once, clearing the last white sparks from its wires.

Sathra looked across the gallery, past the Emperor, to the line of Archmages sheltering at its doors.

“Read it again,” she told them.

Hedran put down the broken lens.

Her original light spell was still burning beneath the floor. It was a thin, tired thing now; she had spent too much strength on the spear. She laid her thumb on the disk and selected the same illumination pattern.

Registration appeared as before.

In the authorization field was her own name. Beneath it waited a place for her signature. There was no longer a superior grant above it.

The price read zero.

Hedran checked the payee. The Treasury line had been removed.

She signed.

The spell took a little of her strength, exactly as it always had. Her light brightened beneath the conquered cities. No coin moved.

Behind her, the youngest apprentice was watching his own disk. He had the penny closed in his other hand.

“Only for us?” he asked.

Hedran opened the authorization record. The change ran through the world's registry, down to its smallest class of caster: each living signature could now authorize its own spells. She could no longer find a category called Imperial subject.

“No,” she said.

The eastern skyroad arch rang.

Its gates were moving apart.

The delivery passage had been no wider than a kite. Now the space within the arch opened from foot to crown, and the heavy shutters withdrew into stone. Beyond them appeared a city on red cliffs, with blue awnings strung across its streets. People there were crowding their own arch, staring through at the Imperial palace.

Hedran knew its assessment by heart. She had not known the cliffs were red.

Another arch opened to a city in snow. Another showed towers standing against a night sky. Across the capital, bells answered bells from places too distant to have been heard together in any living person's memory.

The six gathered in front of the eastern gate. Brannoc straightened his white shoulder plates. Tavren said something that made him laugh. Ossan waited for Vesra to finish closing the seams along her wrist. Nhal held one small white ring above an outstretched hand, turning it idly as the wind pulled at the black hood.

Sathra sheathed her sword.

“What do you intend us to do?” Hedran called.

Sathra looked back at her, then at the immense map shining through the Hall's floor.

“You might visit.”

She stepped through the arch. The others followed, six strangers entering the city on the red cliffs beneath a sky that had been closed that morning.

The people on the far side made room for them. At the neighboring arch, a little girl in a blue coat slipped past the watching adults and stepped from the city in winter onto an Imperial terrace. Snow fell from her boots into the sunlight.

Beyond the eastern arch, Nhal raised two fingers. The last white ring winked out.

The gates stayed open.
