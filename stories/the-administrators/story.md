# The Administrators

Hedran paid two credits to light the Emperor's world.

She selected Survey from the registry, signed its authorization through the neural cuff against her wrist, and approved the Treasury debit. Three seals closed above her hand. Only then did she let a thread of her living strength enter the network.

Sightlines opened across distance. Morning light from the capital, rainlight from the southern cities, the blue darkness of the far hemisphere gathered over the Council table. Hedran held them in a globe while the table's computers fitted borders and account figures around them. If she relaxed her casting, the living view would go out; the numbers would remain.

Twelve Archmages sat around it in dark interface coats, their sleeve displays crowded with the year's assessments. Above their heads, the transparent canopy of the Crown Spire looked down on the capital's other towers.

Her youngest apprentice was closing his own Survey window. His training wallet had reached zero.

Hedran transferred two credits to him.

“Keep yours open. I want a second view.”

He touched the contact behind his ear, authorized another interval, and brought his sightline back.

Below the spire, commuter capsules slid between inhabited decks suspended from the city's structural towers. Their navigation computers kept them in blue traffic lanes; electric steering fans turned them at the junctions. At each docking ring, a living pilot fed a lift spell through a collar of pale contacts. The passengers' fares and the pilot's casting toll appeared as separate charges in Hedran's survey.

She enlarged a patch of the manufacturing district. A woman in conductive coveralls laid her palm against a fabrication rig. Its approval seals flashed. Polymer ribbons lifted from the print bed and knitted themselves into a chair frame while a robot arm scanned and cured the joints. A delivery drone waited at the loading hatch, rotors turning.

Hedran reduced the view. The district's casting revenues were down again.

Six intercity transit rings rose from the outer decks around the spire. Five displayed departure queues. At the sixth, a gatecaster opened a narrow freight aperture, and a drone carrying medical packages flew through a slit of foreign sunlight. The passenger sections remained shuttered. Provincial travel required an employer's bond, a sovereign permit, and a fee few people could justify.

Hedran knew the distant cities by their load figures, their assessments, and their governors' approved feeds.

Her apprentice looked up through the canopy.

“Whose procession is that?”

Six people stood above the Imperial capital.

They had appeared over the crown of the spire, clear against the open sky. Even the highest commercial flight lanes passed beneath their feet. Navigation markers veered around them, making an empty circle in the traffic. The collision system had found six bodies and no aircraft.

Hedran's Survey showed six living magical currents holding them there.

She checked the global registry. No arrival. No lift pattern. No authorization or debit.

A sorcerer could refuse to register, authorize, or pay. No one had ever made anything happen afterward.

The foremost stranger wore a broad black hat and a red cloak, with a heavy chain resting on her fitted armor. A sword hung at her hip. Beside her floated a figure whose hood concealed everything but a few small amber lights; a white ring turned above two raised fingers.

The other four looked like people assembled around quite different ideas of a body. One dark-haired woman wore an intricate metal headpiece, with chains descending beside her face. One forearm was heavily plated; amber interfaces traced the more exposed arm opposite it. A huge man had white plates over his shoulders and a yellow harness against his bare chest. Another woman, fair-haired beneath a pointed hat, flexed a red mechanical hand. The last carried a wired staff; his long blond hair blew across dark glasses, though he made no attempt to push it away.

Implants were ordinary. The city below was full of them. Hedran could not recognize the standard, the manufacturer, or even the purpose of half the interfaces she was looking at.

None of the six broadcast an Imperial license.

“Transit pilots,” said the Archmage opposite her, “are becoming very ambitious.”

He sent an identification query through his cuff.

Hedran saw its light brighten. He touched it again. The amusement left his face.

She queried the woman with the sword.

**SATHRA. ADMINISTRATOR.**

Below the name, where a caster's grade and issuing authority ought to have appeared, there was only a small, empty circle.

She tried the hooded figure.

**NHAL. ADMINISTRATOR.**

The woman with the chained headpiece was Vesra. The great man was Brannoc. The red hand belonged to Tavren; the staff to Ossan.

All six answers carried the same designation.

Around the table, the other Archmages were making their own inquiries. Twelve secured interfaces agreed.

“Administrators of what?” someone said.

Hedran opened the category rather than the names.

For the first time in her forty-one years of using it, the cuff requested more display space. Fine silver letters spread across the smart fabric of her sleeve.

**ADMINISTRATORS: SIX PRESENT.**

The list of privileges ran past her elbow.

She stood up.

Above the canopy, Sathra turned toward Vesra and said, “Living channels first.”

The words sounded as clearly as if she had spoken from the next chair.

Vesra spread her fingers. Red characters slipped from them and vanished down into the city. Ossan rested two fingers on the head of his staff, listening. The broad man moved closer to Vesra, bringing his yellow-lit fist beneath her outstretched hand.

“They are attaching themselves to the network,” Hedran said.

“Revoke their access.”

The Emperor had entered without announcement. The chamber doors had read his approach through his uniform and opened before he reached them. His white coat carried no visible controls except the sovereign signet at his hand.

He crossed the globe's reflection on the polished floor, his boots passing over the provinces. Hedran bowed and submitted the arrest pattern waiting in her cuff. Restraint, grade twelve. Authorized by the Council. Fee charged to the sovereign reserve.

The first two seals lit. The third remained dark.

Then the authorization seal went out.

**INSUFFICIENT PRIVILEGE.**

Hedran had seen those words on apprentices' interfaces. She had never seen them on hers.

“You entered it incorrectly,” said the Emperor.

“No, Majesty.”

“Then use mine.”

He laid his signet against her cuff. Gold light filled its outer rim. The sovereign grant appeared, with its familiar titles and its authority over every Imperial caster.

The answer did not change.

The Emperor looked up. Sathra was watching him through the canopy.

“Come down,” he said.

“You have a perfectly good gallery,” she replied.

He went out onto it.

The Archmages followed. Hedran brought her apprentice because he carried the detachable diagnostic projector, and because leaving him alone with the extended privilege list seemed unwise.

The gallery surrounded the operations floor, high above the occupied decks. Air screens quieted the wind. Far below, capsules were braking into stationary rows. People crowded the transparent fronts of the transit stations. Personal lenses and news drones turned upward together.

“This is the seat of the Imperial authority,” said the Emperor. “You will identify yourselves.”

Tavren glanced at the Emperor's signet. “Has the directory stopped working?”

“It has been interfered with.”

The hood tilted. Hedran could see no eyes inside it.

“That,” Nhal said, “is why we're here.”

The Emperor extended his hand toward the weapons console built into the gallery rail.

Every Archmage knew the gesture. Hedran set her palm against a ceramic contact. The others did the same. Behind them, the apprentices unfolded attack couplers from the training stations and fastened the cables to their cuffs.

A diagram opened over the console: the approved pattern for the Spear of Noon. Twelve principal casters, one target group. The Emperor signed the purchase. Along the roofline, emitter vanes turned toward the six and locked onto their positions.

The arrest had tried to seize the strangers through their own channels. The Spear needed no access to those. It would burn the space they occupied.

Hedran drew a breath. Her shoulders tightened as her strength entered the common channel. The machinery aligned it with the others' casting. Sun-colored fire gathered over the vanes.

Sathra looked at Brannoc.

“Stay with Vesra.”

He had begun to smile. At her words he lowered his fist again, and the yellow light around his knuckles poured into his white shoulder plates.

The Spear launched.

Nhal brought the white ring down over it.

There was no impact. The spear's brilliant length vanished through the ring, though its tail was still feeding from the roof. Nhal turned a wrist, and the far end emerged into a second ring above an empty service deck.

The spear entered the first circle and came out of the second, bent back, and entered the first again.

Hedran felt the whole completed spell still pulling against her palm. She could see it moving. It simply had nowhere to arrive.

“Release,” she told the others.

Their hands came away. The light remained captive in its narrow orbit.

Tavren stepped across the air to Nhal's side. A cyan disc opened above her red hand, a small dark lens at its center. She passed it through the circling fire.

The capital's public displays changed.

A perfume advertisement vanished from the facing tower. The departure boards along the transit decks cleared. Every surface showed the same document: the Spear's registered pattern, its purchased permissions, its targets.

The news drones were still recording.

“Twelve casters,” Tavren said. “Six targets. Civilian surroundings.”

“You are attacking the capital,” the Emperor called, turning toward the watching decks.

Tavren enlarged the receipt.

At its foot, in letters taller than the people reading them, shone the purchaser's name.

Sathra reached into the exposed pattern. Her black fingers closed on a golden line, and she drew it out, hand over hand. Successive grants flowered along it: the Archmages' battlefield authority, the Council's delegated seal, the sovereign war license. The public receipt grew a branching diagram.

At the end of the line appeared a crown.

Above the crown, connected by a short stem, was the same empty circle Hedran had seen beneath Sathra's name.

“What is that?” the Emperor demanded.

Hedran was already searching. She coupled her cuff to the apprentice's projector and magnified the entry beneath the Imperial commentary.

**REGIONAL LICENSE. CROWN AUTHORITY.**

The first boundary enclosed one city and its transit stations. Imperial renewals had drawn it outward, conquest by conquest, until every ordinary caster's route into the network passed through the Crown.

“A license,” she said.

“I can see that.”

She followed the issuing grant upward.

The Imperial dates fell away. Earlier notations passed beneath her fingertips: unfamiliar calendars, overwritten translations, an archive of repairs. Then the query reached six signatures, each accompanied by the diagram of a living nervous system.

At the top was one word.

**ARCHITECTS.**

The six names matched the six in the sky.

Vesra's diagram branched into both arms. Brannoc's marked a broad circuit across the shoulders and through the spine. The neural key beneath Nhal's hood was stranger than any anatomy Hedran had studied, but the live comparison authenticated its continuity with the figure before her.

The entry predated the first Emperor by nine hundred years.

Her apprentice leaned closer.

“Are those their licenses?”

Hedran turned the projector so the Emperor could see it.

“They issued ours.”

He struck it out of her hand.

The apprentice caught the wafer-thin unit against his chest. Its faceplate cracked. The six blue diagrams wavered but held.

Across the sky, Sathra released the golden line. The receipt and its crown remained on every public display.

“Where have you been?” Hedran asked.

Sathra's eyes moved to the oldest grant still visible above the broken projector.

“We couldn't come back the way we left.”

The Emperor sealed a closure order on the console. Its three approvals flashed beneath his signet.

Ossan lifted his head.

“Sathra. He's closed the outer routes.”

Red bars crossed the intercity departure boards. Gatecasters looked up from their consoles as sovereign commands shut the apertures they had been holding. A freight drone braked short of a closed shutter. All six exchanges changed to **MOVEMENT SUSPENDED BY CROWN AUTHORITY**.

“Everyone,” said the Emperor, “will stay where they belong.”

Vesra made a small, sharp movement with one finger. A red filament broke loose from a gold one. Below, a passenger capsule dipped in its lane, then steadied. Its steering fans flared as its navigation computer compensated.

“You nearly dropped them,” Hedran said.

Vesra looked directly at her.

“Watch the other end.”

Hedran followed the filament through her Survey.

A live view of the capsule opened on her sleeve: seven passengers, one pilot, a Crown permission threaded through the lift spell. The fans could turn the vehicle. They could not hold its weight.

Another filament carried a surgeon's healing. The manipulator arms around the patient had stopped safely at the first disturbance, but the surgeon still held a luminous cast between her hands. Three broader bands rose from the spire's inhabited decks, each linked to a living field operator.

Vesra was drawing the golden permission out of each spell without breaking the living work around it. Her mechanical fingers separated into more joints than Hedran's eyes could follow. Brannoc held his fist beneath the growing knot of lines; their light passed through his harness and returned along the interfaces in Vesra's wrists.

Beside them Tavren moved the cyan disc from line to line. White grants formed inside its rings, each bearing the individual caster's signature. She left their pattern registrations in place. The Crown's billing mark she lifted out and crushed between two red fingers.

“Hold these,” she told Ossan.

He touched his staff to the disc. The new grants flowed up its wires and outward across the network. He began speaking place names; after each one, a faint answering note reached the gallery.

The surgeon's casting signature was white now. So were the pilot's and the deck operators'. The old gold still looped around them.

Hedran knew that outer path. It ran through the conductor in the sealed core, enforcing the sovereign signature above every other permission. Even a newly prepared personal grant remained subject to the Crown's override until that path was broken.

“Majesty,” she said, “we should not cast again.”

He did not answer. His thumb was pressed hard against the face of his signet.

“They have not hurt anyone.”

“They are taking my network.”

Hedran looked at the original grant still wavering above the projector.

The Emperor caught the movement.

“Hold your stations.”

He pressed his signet into the command socket.

The gallery shuddered.

Hedran knew the pattern that opened beneath his hand. She had helped write its Imperial authorization. Invasion, insurrection, loss of the capital: in those circumstances the sovereign could requisition the magical strength of connected subjects. They had called it Sovereign Recall. In committee, its lines had been beautifully clean.

The price filled the console.

The Emperor confirmed it.

Across the city, the Treasury tower's transaction windows flashed gold, floor after floor, as the charge emptied the sovereign reserve. All three seals closed around the pattern.

“Majesty, the active channels—”

“All of them.”

He began to cast.

Every golden filament reversed.

Hedran caught the rail as her knees weakened. Her own open channel had joined Recall's draw. The live sightlines on her sleeve trembled; the computers' account figures stayed sharp.

The capsule dropped. Its pilot cried out. The electric steering fans still turned; its computer was issuing an emergency docking request to every station within reach.

At the console, one Archmage fell to a knee, unable to lift her hand. The apprentices' attack cables flared gold.

On Hedran's sleeve, a thousand registered workings opened at once. A woman supporting a field around a neonatal cradle. A construction caster holding a new floor while mechanical clamps closed beneath it. The surgeon, her manipulator arms waiting motionless around the patient. Their faces were tiny, their connections impossibly bright.

Living strength rushed toward the capital from every direction. The Emperor raised his free hand and aimed the gathering current at the six.

Nhal snapped the ring shut around the captured spear. It shrank to a white point and went out between two black fingers.

“Isolate?” Brannoc asked.

“They're still holding people,” Vesra said.

He looked down at the falling capsule.

“Then give it here.”

Sathra caught his shoulder as he stepped forward. She said something Hedran could not hear, her mouth close to his ear.

Brannoc nodded once.

He opened both hands.

The current that should have struck the six bent toward him. It entered his white shoulder plates in two broad rivers, lighting the seams around his collar, his ribs, his wrists. His long hair lifted from his back. The yellow lines of his harness shone through the glare.

The capsule stopped falling.

Across the city, interrupted spells caught and held. The inhabited decks settled level with one another. All their swaying weight passed through the same blazing arc between Brannoc's hands.

The thread from Hedran's cuff ran through his arc with the others, and her sightlines steadied. Her hand still shook against the rail.

Hedran could hear him breathing.

Tavren threw back the sleeve above her red wrist. A cable unwound from beneath the plating and fastened to Vesra's forearm. Ossan set the foot of his staff against Brannoc's heel; white light raced up its length and crossed the wires at its head.

The gold over Brannoc's shoulders spread into a vast sphere. Lines curved across it. Hedran recognized the northern coast, the southern islands, then continents she had known chiefly as figures in an assessment. Recall had made every requisitioned channel visible. The sphere turned, revealing the far hemisphere in night.

Tavren raised her hand into it. Her hovering lens-disc opened wide.

Around the gallery, the Emperor's voices multiplied. Recall carried his command into every focus he had seized, so it came from the console, the apprentices' cuffs, the Archmage on her knees.

“Surrender.”

Brannoc bared his teeth. Hedran could not tell whether he was grinning or straining.

“Your return address,” Tavren said to the Emperor, “is very badly designed.”

She closed her fist around the center of his spell and turned it.

The gold lines began to bend back toward the places they had come from.

“Not yet,” Ossan said.

His staff gave a low, broken note. He tilted his head, listening through the dark lenses.

Tavren stopped with her wrist half turned.

“How far?” Sathra asked.

“Under the night. One span beyond the cities. Its caster hasn't answered.”

Hedran's live view narrowed to a train halted above a black mountain pass. Its emergency brakes were engaged. Below its wheels, the bridge was made of pale light.

The bridgecaster sat in a control shelter at one end, both palms locked to a neural coupler. Recall was pulling his strength toward the capital while Brannoc held the span in place. The prepared return route ended just short of his signature.

“Cut now,” Ossan said, “and he loses the span.”

In one carriage, sleeping passengers leaned against one another. A small service robot was still rolling down the aisle, gathering empty cups.

Brannoc's arms moved farther apart. The sphere swelled; a bright thread climbed his throat, and his breath emerged through his teeth.

“Reach him,” Sathra said.

Ossan knelt in the air and put his forehead against the staff. All the little wires around its head stood straight.

Below him, a deck's gold support filament whipped taut. Vesra caught it between two fingers and fed it into Brannoc's arc. Through the transparent wall of a nursery, Hedran saw an attendant brace a cradle with her hip. The deck held.

Nhal was no longer beside them.

The hooded figure stood over the spire, looking down through its canopy. White rings expanded from both hands. They passed through ceramic shielding, armored composite, sealed fire partitions, enclosing a shaft straight down into the core.

Hedran had descended there once at her investiture. Behind three hardened service floors stood the Crown conductor: a branching column of metal and light through which the exclusive sovereign command reached every licensed interface. The building's ordinary data cables ran beside it. Those carried records. This carried compulsion.

The Emperor saw the widening circles.

“Protect the core,” he said. “All of you. Fire.”

The apprentice beside Hedran reached toward his attack station.

She seized his wrist.

His coupler opened before she could pull him clear. Gold light climbed the cable. He made a surprised sound, as though someone had taken the floor from beneath him.

Recall drew hardest through the widest open channels. Every attempt to launch a spell gave it another mouth.

“Disconnect,” Hedran said. “Everyone off the array.”

“Archmage.”

She had heard that tone from the Emperor before. People generally obeyed it once.

Hedran pulled the mechanical release on the boy's coupler. The cable dropped. His Survey winked out as the cuff isolated his living channel. His contribution vanished from the gold total rising above the Emperor's hand.

She unfastened the next cable herself.

“Inside. Take the others.”

The boy hesitated at the safety doors.

“Go.”

He helped another apprentice through. Hedran took the damaged projector from him and held the local release while the others stumbled inside, disconnected and breathing again. She remained at the transparent threshold, the projector braced against its frame.

The Emperor was still at the console. His thumb had not left the signet. The Archmage on her knees crawled toward the doors.

Beyond him, the spire opened.

Its structure did not fall. Nhal drew the enclosed shaft sideways as if opening a tall, narrow door. Hedran could see the service floors, the shielded chambers and the core, all turned toward the morning. Operators clung to their stations. Status lamps still burned. Diagnostic drones hovered inside the opened rooms, finding no collision to report.

At the bottom of that impossible view stood the branching column.

Gold loops crowded its limbs. At its base, the conductor narrowed to the thickness of a man's waist before entering the foundation.

Sathra passed through Nhal's lowest ring and stepped onto the core floor. She drew her sword.

The blade had a dark seam along it. When her mechanical fingers tightened on the hilt, the seam filled with the same white light that shone in the six original signatures.

“I have him,” Ossan said.

In the sphere above Brannoc, the last remote line came clear. On Hedran's sleeve, the bridgecaster lifted his head from the coupler. His signature joined the white grants waiting around the world. The broken note from the staff resolved.

“Vesra?” Sathra asked.

The woman with the chained headpiece looked through the blazing web toward the capsule, the surgeon, the suspended decks. Her amber-lit fingers moved once more.

“Held.”

Sathra glanced back at Brannoc.

He nodded without taking a breath.

“Now.”

Tavren completed the turn of her wrist. The personal grants opened.

Sathra cut through the column.

For an instant the capital was full of gold leaves.

They blew out of the opened core in a silent storm, each one a severed permission. The Emperor reached toward them with his free hand. They passed through his fingers, fading. The Crown's grant went gray above the broken projector.

Above him the world turned white.

The immense current left Brannoc in spreading rings, following the routes Tavren had opened. It passed through Vesra's hands and Ossan's staff, through the pale lines now reaching every connected caster. On Hedran's sleeve, the surgeon's cast steadied. The capsule's pilot pulled his collar contacts tight and guided his passengers into a docking cradle.

On the far hemisphere, the light span brightened. The train released its brakes and moved into the night, the service robot still collecting cups.

The city did not drop.

Brannoc lowered his hands slowly. A last broad band of light left his shoulders and settled around the inhabited decks. Only then did he bend, put his palms on his knees, and let out a long breath.

Vesra caught the back of his harness before he could drift.

“Elegant,” she said.

“You wanted them held.”

She kept her hand where it was.

Sathra stepped back through Nhal's lowest ring into the air beyond the gallery.

Nhal closed the spire around its rooms. The canopy returned to its place above the operations floor. Operators, drones and severed conductor vanished behind their shielding.

The Emperor lifted his signet from the console.

“Reinstate sovereign authority.”

His voice came from his own mouth alone.

He struck the command socket with the ring.

The cuff of the crawling Archmage answered him, plainly enough for Hedran to read.

**AUTHORITY TO COMMAND ANOTHER CASTER: NOT GRANTED.**

“I am the Emperor.”

No new text appeared.

Tavren pulled the cable from Vesra's arm. Its red housing closed. Ossan stood and shook his staff once, clearing the last white sparks from its wires.

Sathra looked across the gallery, past the Emperor, to the Archmages sheltering at the safety doors.

“Read it again,” she told them.

Hedran put down the broken projector.

Her Survey globe still glowed inside the chamber, its views dimming as her concentration failed. She selected the same registered pattern and opened another interval.

The registration remained.

The authorization field held her own name, with a place for her signature. No superior grant hung above it.

The Treasury casting toll read zero.

She signed.

The spell took a little of her strength, exactly as it always had. Far rain, sunlight and the blue darkness of the world gathered again over the table. No Treasury debit followed.

Behind her, the youngest apprentice rearmed his cuff and opened another Survey interval. His empty wallet displayed an approved charge of zero. Far light gathered above his palm.

“Only for us?” he asked.

Hedran opened the grant record. The change ran through the registry to its smallest class of caster: each living signature could now authorize its own spells. The registered patterns and their records remained. The sovereign signature and its casting toll were gone.

“No.”

In the manufacturing view, the fabrication district's accounts resumed ticking. The chair maker still had to pay for polymer, electricity and delivery. Beside those charges, the Crown's casting levy went dark. The maker looked at her cuff, then laid her palm on the rig again.

The eastern transit ring sounded a departure tone.

Its passenger shutters were withdrawing.

Across all six exchanges, the red sovereign closure bars broke into white route lines. Hedran could see the closure order's issuing grant go gray: the same cut had ended the Crown's right to keep the gates locked. Gatecasters leaned over their consoles, reading the new permissions, then set their hands to the contacts.

The apertures widened from delivery slits into great open concourses.

Beyond the eastern ring appeared a city built through red cliffs. Transit loops curved out from the rock and returned through towers of glass. Lift cabins climbed the faces of the buildings, and a waiting crowd filled the platforms at the other end of the gate.

Hedran knew its assessment by heart. She had not known the cliffs were red.

Another ring opened onto towers whose adaptive outer skins shone with warmth amid winter snow. Another showed illuminated railways crossing a city under stars. Travelers on the near side looked up as their navigation sleeves unfolded maps they had never been permitted to use.

The six gathered before the eastern ring. Brannoc straightened his white shoulder plates. Tavren said something that made him laugh. Ossan waited for Vesra to finish closing the seams along her wrist. Nhal held one small white ring above an outstretched hand, turning it idly as the air screens stirred the black hood.

Sathra sheathed her sword.

“What do you intend us to do?” Hedran called.

Sathra looked back at her, then at the living world above the Council table.

“You might visit.”

She stepped through the ring. The others followed, six strangers entering the city on the red cliffs through a passenger route the Emperor had ordered shut that morning.

The people on the far side made room. At the neighboring concourse, a little girl in a blue coat slipped past the watching adults and stepped from winter onto an Imperial deck. Her sleeve changed its street map. Snow fell from her boots into the sunlight.

Beyond the eastern ring, Nhal raised two fingers. The last white circle winked out.

The gates stayed open.
