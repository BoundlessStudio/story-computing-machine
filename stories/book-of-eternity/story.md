The order said the book was to be burned unopened.

Ysabet had approved that precaution herself. She had seen what came out of the king’s other collections. Now she stood below his burning library with a lantern in one hand and the order in the other, while an old man told her he had guarded this particular volume for twenty-three years.

“I don’t get it. If this Book of Eternity is so dangerous, why do you have it under lock and key? Why couldn’t you simply have it destroyed right then and there?”

“We could have.”

The old man’s name was Orrel. He wore a grey coat with shiny elbows, and his white hair grew in two unruly wings above his ears. Nothing about him suggested the terror promised by the iron door, the three dead bolts, or the warning cut into the stone above them.

“Then why didn’t you?”

He looked at her lantern.

“Will you hear a witness first?”

Behind him, the book lay on a sloping stone stand. It was as wide as a paving slab and thick as Ysabet’s fist, bound in green leather worn almost black. Two brass clasps secured its covers. Somebody had polished the words BOOK OF ETERNITY so often that the corners of the letters were disappearing.

Above them, something heavy fell. The floor quivered beneath her boots. They had taken the palace by dawn; now the men were opening cupboards, bringing down portraits, discovering how much of a king could be made to burn.

Ysabet folded the order along its existing crease.

“One witness. I have people waiting upstairs.”

Orrel set a low stool before the stand, then placed her lantern on it. He opened the shutter enough to light the book.

“Stay behind this. Whatever you see, keep your hands to yourself.”

“Is that the danger?”

“Part of it.”

He unlocked the clasps. On the spine was a little brass rack. He raised the cover two inches and drove an iron pin through the rack to hold it there. The simple, practiced movement disturbed her more than a circle of candles would have done.

She expected writing.

There was a street.

It lay below the edge of the cover, farther down than the book was thick. Red roofs crowded one another above a steep cobbled slope. The light came from somewhere to the left: a late sun, although dawn had scarcely passed outside. A cart stood crooked across the street. Two men were arguing over which of them should move it.

Ysabet bent forward.

The men stopped arguing and looked up.

Behind the roofs rose a white bell tower with three clock faces. The fourth face had been bricked over. Ysabet knew the absurdity of that tower. As a child she had walked around it with her father, trying to find the clock that people on the north side were supposed to use.

“Harrowick,” she said.

Orrel watched her face.

“The army report said it was abandoned.”

“The army found an empty hill.”

She put the order down. She had carried it all morning without once wanting both hands free.

At the bottom of the slope, a yellow-coated woman came out of a doorway. She had close-cut black hair and something white tucked under one arm. As she hurried toward them, she kept looking over her shoulder and shouting. More people appeared at the windows.

“Orrel!”

The voice was small, but not faint. Ysabet heard the catch in it.

“What is that smell?”

“The palace library is burning, Edris.”

The woman stopped.

“Again?”

“Not here.”

“That’s what I was told last time.”

Orrel did not answer immediately. In the silence, Ysabet heard a child asking somebody to lift him high enough to see.

“Show her,” Edris said.

“I was going to.”

“Then do it.”

Orrel moved to the other side of the stand. Ysabet shifted the stool and followed, keeping it between herself and the opening.

A black crescent had been eaten out of the leaf. There were no houses in it. The city stopped in the middle of streets, at half a courtyard, along the straight, clean edge of a room whose wallpaper was still blue. Beyond those cuts was a blackness that the afternoon light did not enter.

The margin around it was brittle and brown.

Ysabet had seen enough burned towns to know what was missing. There should have been rubble, tiles, the stubborn shapes of fireplaces. Here there was nowhere for those things to lie.

“Who?” she asked.

“The keeper before me. He thought the same as you.”

“And the people?”

Orrel’s hand rested beside the black edge, careful not to touch it.

“The ones who were left told me where their families had been.”

Edris had climbed a narrow stair that rose along the edge of the city. From its top she could see them over a low parapet. The white thing under her arm was a folded shirt. She laid it down and flattened it with both palms, as if she had remembered she had been carrying it.

“My husband was at the south gate,” she said. “Ask him where the south gate is.”

Ysabet looked at Orrel. He pointed into the crescent.

She stepped back so sharply that her heel caught the stool.

“The king is dead.”

“Yes.”

“Then what is holding them?”

“They are. He laid the binding on their living bodies. Their hearts keep it.”

Ysabet looked at the tiny people crowding the windows. The thought had nowhere decent to go. Every sign that they were alive was also proof that the prison was working.

“Twenty-three years?”

“Since I came here. The city was taken five years before that.”

She was thirty-nine. She remembered the tower from a spring journey when she was nine. The army had come two years later.

“That woman can’t be more than thirty.”

“She was twenty-seven when they took the city.”

Edris heard him.

“Don’t tell it as though I haven’t been here.”

Orrel bowed his head.

The three clocks all showed the same time. Nothing moved in the sky above them. It had been that late afternoon while Ysabet grew tall, learned to ride, buried her father, took her first command. Somewhere in the city was the child who had asked to be lifted. She did not ask how old he was.

“You can open it,” she said. “There must be a way out.”

“There is a way to exchange.”

At the top of the stair, Edris had taken off her yellow coat. She laid it over the shirt. Without it, she looked narrower and older, though Ysabet could not have said why.

“For every person he released, the king required another,” Orrel said. “Sometimes a relative. Sometimes somebody who displeased him. Touch a prisoner through the opening and the passage begins. Let them come all the way through, and you take their place.”

Edris climbed onto the parapet.

“Get down,” Orrel said.

She did not look at him. She looked at Ysabet, and then her foot slipped.

Ysabet had spent the morning pulling people out of broken masonry. Her hand was already moving when she remembered the warning.

Edris’s hand came toward her, growing from a white speck into a woman’s open palm. Ysabet caught it.

For one instant it was only a hand: warm, with hard skin at the base of the fingers. Then Edris gripped her wrist and pulled.

Ysabet’s cuff went flat. Beneath it, her arm thinned into rows of black strokes. She could feel her fingers, but she could no longer see where one ended and the next began. The street rose toward her. Someone was shouting from below, a voice suddenly as large as her own.

She braced a boot against the stand and pulled back. Edris held on with both hands now. One of her elbows was outside the cover.

Orrel drove a steel ruler between their wrists.

“Let go!”

Ysabet did not know which of them he meant. She opened her fingers. Edris lost her grip, and the old man forced the ruler between their palms.

The street fell away. Ysabet struck the floor with her shoulder. Her arm was heavy and solid again, and she clutched it against her chest while Orrel pulled the pin and brought the cover down.

He locked both clasps before he helped her up.

She could hear herself breathing. She could hear the library burning overhead. The voices had stopped.

“Did you hurt her?”

“She was back on the stair.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“I don’t know.”

Ysabet looked at the book. Her wrist ached where Edris had held it. She remembered the coat and the folded shirt, set out on the parapet. Edris had left them where somebody else could find them.

Orrel stood with the ruler hanging at his side.

“I should have kept it shut.”

“Then I would have burned it.”

Boots sounded on the stair. Sergeant Pellan came through the doorway carrying an iron fire pan, followed by two soldiers with a sack of kindling between them. The sergeant’s sandy moustache was grey with ash.

He looked at Ysabet’s bare wrist, then at the old man.

“Trouble?”

“Put the pan down. We’re taking the book out intact.”

Pellan put it down, but he did not let go of the handle.

“That’s expressly forbidden.”

“There are people in it.”

He waited. She could see him making room for one more impossible thing. They had found a room upstairs in which every mirror was somebody else’s window.

“Alive,” she said. “Harrowick. The people who disappeared.”

“Can they come out?”

“By taking somebody else’s place.”

Pellan’s eyes returned to her wrist. The soldiers behind him shifted away from the stand.

“Then we don’t carry it through our lines.”

“Locked. Nobody touches it.”

“The order says every royal weapon is to be destroyed on recovery. No exceptions by field command.”

“I know what it says.”

“You helped write it.”

The two soldiers watched their boots. Orrel moved nearer the stand, as though his body would be useful between the fire pan and the book.

Pellan let go of the handle. “I can put a guard on this door while you take it to the council. But I can’t help you take it away against a sealed order.”

There was another crash overhead. Dust slid down the curve of the chamber ceiling. Waiting here while she rode across the city would leave the book under a burning building, and she had already seen what one singed corner meant.

Ysabet unbuckled her sword belt.

Pellan looked up.

“You can arrest an officer who refuses the order,” she said. “You can seize what she refused to destroy. It goes before the council with her.”

“As evidence.”

“Yes.”

He glanced at the order on the stand. “That isn’t a pardon for either of you.”

“I’m asking you to carry it out of the fire.”

She held the belt toward him. After a moment, he took it. The weight leaving her hands was worse than she had expected. She unfastened the red cord from her shoulder and laid that across the sword.

Pellan passed them to a soldier.

“You’ll come under guard. He comes too. I take the keys.”

Orrel nodded.

“Let us tell them first,” Ysabet said.

The sergeant looked at the book for a long time. Then he moved the fire pan against the wall and told his men to stand back.

Orrel unlocked the clasps, raised the cover to the same notch, and set the iron pin. This time Ysabet stayed well beyond an arm’s reach. Pellan stood beside her with one hand on his sword.

Edris was sitting on the stair. Her yellow coat lay across her knees. People had gathered below her, looking up. Ysabet could not see whether her hands were hurt.

“We’re taking the book out,” Ysabet said. “It will go to the council intact. I’ll tell them what I saw.”

Edris lifted her head.

“When do we get out?”

“I don’t know.”

“He’s been saying that for years.”

“I heard him.”

“And now you have the keys.”

Ysabet looked at Pellan. “No. I’m going with you under guard.”

The woman’s gaze moved from her face to the bare shoulder where her officer’s cord had been. She stood and put on her coat. Down in the street, somebody began passing her words back to the people who could not hear.

“What’s your name?” Edris asked.

“Ysabet.”

Edris said it twice. Ysabet waited while the name travelled down the stair.

“Put out that lamp, Ysabet.”

She knelt beside the stool and turned the wick down until the flame dwindled and went out. The little street remained bright with its own afternoon.

Orrel pulled the pin. Before the cover closed, Ysabet saw Edris pick up the folded shirt and take it down to someone waiting below.

The old man locked the book and gave Pellan the keys. The soldiers lifted the volume between them with the care they would have used for a wounded man.

Ysabet held out her wrists.
