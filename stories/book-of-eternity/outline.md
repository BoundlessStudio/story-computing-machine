# Provisional outline

## Promised experience

The request explicitly gives a dangerous Book of Eternity, custody under lock and key, and a questioner who sees destruction as the obvious solution. “I don’t get it” invites the reader to share that reasonable skepticism. I infer a pleasure in an answer that changes the meaning of the book and of keeping it, with the explanation made consequential in a choice. The imposing title permits wonder or dread, but the request specifies neither tone nor scale, character identities, relationship, nor ending. The direction below chooses them; they are not additional requirements.

## Provisional direction

Working title: **The People Between the Covers**. Close third person through Ysabet, a rebel officer sent to burn the defeated king’s notorious book. She and its elderly custodian, Orrel, are adults who have never met. The story takes place in the book’s underground chamber during the palace’s capture. Aim for accessible, ominous wonder with a decisive moral reversal, at roughly 1,800–2,500 words; non-graphic peril.

The book is the king’s portable prison: a real city and its living inhabitants have been folded into its pages. A narrow opening permits sight and speech; opening it flat permits one living person to step in while one steps out. The king built that exchange into his spell to demand replacements for prisoners he released. His living captives sustain the stored confinement; the volume is not an independently casting nonliving mind. Destroying the page destroys the place and people held there. The locks prevent casual reading from becoming an involuntary exchange and prevent executioners from burning witnesses. These are specific properties of this prison, not laws of all magic. The former king is dead; his spell has not conveniently died with him.

Ysabet initially sees Orrel as a collaborator preserving the royal instrument of terror. Orrel did preserve it, and has refused releases that would require new victims. He cannot claim that indefinite confinement is freedom or that his custody has solved it. Their disagreement concerns what they can actually do with this dangerous object now, not whether Ysabet is too foolish to ask the question.

## Candidate scenes and ending

1. Ysabet arrives with the revolution’s destruction warrant and a shuttered fire lantern. She speaks the prompt’s question, with the apparent typo rendered as “is” in dialogue. Orrel answers that they could have burned it, and asks her to hear one witness before doing so. Above them the victors are burning the royal archives; keeping the book will cost Ysabet more than a change of opinion.
2. Orrel shows a damaged leaf. A former attempt to burn the book left a black gap across its miniature city. Through a narrow opening Ysabet hears people calling at windows, sees a woman lean out to argue with the keeper about the promised release, and recognizes the city’s bell tower from before its unexplained disappearance. The inhabitants disagree with Orrel and display independent lives; this is evidence, not merely his assertion that marks on paper are people.
3. A desperate captive widens the opening from within. Ysabet catches an outstretched hand and begins to become lettering while the captive’s hand becomes full-sized. Orrel breaks the contact and closes the book before either finishes crossing. The immediate danger demonstrates why locks are necessary and why innocent prisoners can still make the volume dangerous. Neither person is made a villain for trying to escape.
4. Ysabet’s soldiers reach the chamber, expecting the public destruction the warrant promised. She must decide with the evidence she has: burn a city, conduct an exchange that supplies another captive, or defy the order and preserve people whose imprisonment remains unresolved. She orders her soldiers to carry the chained book out under her protection, and gives up the warrant as proof that the refusal is hers. They follow the officer who led them through the palace, though one warns that she will have to answer for it.
5. At the chamber threshold, Ysabet changes one concrete part of custody: Orrel must address the prisoners and tell them exactly what has been decided, including what has not been solved. Through the safe narrow gap she gives them her name so their accusations can reach the person responsible. A captive asks whether the burning has stopped. Ysabet closes the lantern’s shutter. The last image is the little city waiting under the book’s evening sky as the volume is carried toward real daylight: survival secured, liberty still a debt. Avoid a speech explaining the moral or pretending that better jailkeeping is liberation.

## Question for discussion

Does the refusal to burn provide an earned completion of the prompt’s question, or does the ending chiefly promise a later rescue? Protect the revelation that destruction would kill the people the officer means to liberate, and the actual exchange danger. If the political refusal is insufficiently decisive, change the ending’s action rather than add reassuring dialogue. The invented prisoner relationship must not displace the book’s contradiction as the dramatic center.
