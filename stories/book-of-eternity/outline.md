# Provisional revision outline — The Unburnt Hour

## Promised experience

The original request gives a dangerous Book of Eternity, custody under lock and key, and a reasonable question about destroying it. Its inferred pleasure is an answer that changes the meaning of the book and of keeping it. The user now explicitly finds the supplied candidate inadequate and asks to update the story description to make the Book “more important, old, gody, cosmic,” then check the story and redo the art. The earlier request for five book candidates and a cover using the user's choice remains part of the art direction.

I infer awe, immense antiquity, and significance beyond ordinary royal craftsmanship. Those qualities must be present in the Book's appearance, nature, and consequences; extra celestial decoration would repeat the reported failure. The user has not required a new cast, a universe-ending emergency, a literal present deity, or an ending that declares confinement the only possible solution forever. The inadequate reference's green leather and human brass fittings are not binding design features.

## Chosen direction and visual substance

Retain Ysabet, Orrel, Edris, Pellan, the palace's capture, the threatened burning, the contact exchange, and the costly preservation of lives without a false promise of liberation. The Book becomes an ancient sacred archive of arrested hours that the king appropriated. Its office is to preserve living hours against the passage that would otherwise end them: a surviving sanctuary invocation names the Book as the keeper of what time would take. Harrowick is its latest inhabited leaf, not the whole of its significance. An older inhabited hour retains the light of the Year Star, the vanished celestial source whose extinction began Ysabet's culture's calendar. This sky survives from before the first year of recorded time, although the star no longer shines outside. The held places can belong to vanished civilizations of the same deep-time world.

The paving-slab-sized volume has covers of night-dark material with depth behind their surface: the stars visible there are light from the held skies within, not painted decoration. Broad, eroded gold seals form a celestial instrument around a relief of a crowned, many-haloed figure. The figure and the sanctuary below the palace attest to ancient worship; who made the Book and whether those makers were gods remain uncertain. The king's iron cradle, clasps, and limited-opening rack are visibly later additions. The leaf margins resemble very thin silver-grey vellum; their luminous edges hold the colors of many horizons. It remains portable by two people in its cradle.

The Book preserves living places at their given hour, while people continue to experience what that preservation means. Its old exchange demands a replacement for anyone released: the entrusted hour is not permitted to surrender a life without receiving one. That protection can become captivity. The king learned the dedication that bound Harrowick to an existing leaf; he did not create the archive or its rule. The living inhabitants sustain its operation through the binding, and the artifact shapes that supplied power rather than casting independently. These are local properties of this object. Antiquity and arrested aging do not make its physical leaves invulnerable: the scorched margin and missing district demonstrate that fire destroys what a leaf holds. No invulnerable cover or star-hot page is proposed. The massive cover has endured; its tangible leaves can still burn, including within a closed volume heated by the palace fire. The archive holds the light of its lost skies, not active stellar furnaces beneath combustible covers.

## Complete revised scene direction

1. **The claimed royal weapon.** Ysabet arrives with the order she helped write: burn the king's book unopened. The royal inventory describes one captured city, Harrowick, giving her a reason initially to think the other leaves are machinery serving that conquest. The volume dominates the chamber before anybody describes it. Its deep stars cast unfamiliar configurations across older sanctuary stone beneath the palace masonry; the royal restraints are crude beside its ancient seals. The original question remains reasonable: extraordinary importance and danger make destruction seem more urgent, not less. Orrel's “We could have” and offer of a witness begin the answer.
2. **An inhabited hour.** Orrel opens the human restraint only to its pinned limit. Harrowick's afternoon enters the room; its familiar three-faced tower lets Ysabet recognize a real missing city. Edris contradicts the keeper and commands him to show the damage. The burned crescent, absent south gate, and husband lost there retain their human specificity. The charred physical margin cuts through the luminous edge: a flame can kill the people a divine-looking archive has kept from age. Surviving centuries is not protection against deliberate harm.
3. **A sky the outside universe has lost.** Given the inventory's single city, Ysabet asks whether Harrowick's leaf could be removed intact and the weapon around it destroyed; this is a proposed compromise, not an assertion that detachment is safe. Orrel lets her see another leaf within the same restrained opening. Above its inhabited city shines the Year Star, blue within a gold halo, recognizable from old almanac images although it went out before the calendar's first year. Its light falls on Ysabet's hand. People within this hour react to the opening and call to one another; these are present lives, not pictures of antiquity. The other leaf edges hold further horizons. The surviving sanctuary invocation gives this witnessed power its purpose: the Book keeps living hours that time would otherwise take away. The king turned that preservation into a weapon. Ysabet's plan would destroy the continuing people of the older hour along with light lost to the outside world; the inventory described the king's use, not the contents. The living-source answer explains why his death frees none of them. Unknown makers do not make the demonstrated cosmic function uncertain.
4. **The old bargain acts.** Return attention to Edris and the twenty-eight years she has lived through without aging. The king exploited the archive's exchange to demand replacements; the rule predates him. Edris pitches from the parapet at the fore-edge, Ysabet catches her by reflex, and direct contact draws Ysabet toward the leaf. Nearness accounts for Edris's apparent size before touch; passage starts only at contact. Ysabet releases her grip and Orrel's single ruler intervention breaks the remaining contact. The newer clasp belongs to the cradle, keeping the established handhold legible despite the changed cover. Divine scale has immediate force over her living body, rather than remaining a view on a lid.
5. **A limit to the victors' authority.** Pellan arrives with the means to burn the volume. Ysabet must acknowledge that her order mistook an ancient custody of inhabited hours for the defeated king's property. This is no automatic exemption: the dangerous archive still threatens anyone who opens it, and no one has authorized its preservation. The burning palace rules out delay. Ysabet proposes arrest and preservation of the whole Book as evidence; Pellan knowingly accepts responsibility rather than being compelled by a verbal loophole. She relinquishes sword and command. Her choice protects the unknown older inhabitants as well as the city she recognizes.
6. **What remains when their fire ends.** Under guard and beyond reach, Ysabet tells Edris the limited result without promising freedom or the council's decision. Edris demands the lamp extinguished. The lamp dies; Harrowick's held afternoon remains, and the Year Star's blue light still reaches the chamber through the deeper leaf edges. The soldiers close and lift the archive in its later iron cradle. Light from the sky that ended before their calendar continues within the ancient cover as mortal hands carry it out of their ruler's burning house. Ysabet offers her wrists. The Book's immense prior life, purpose, and continuing inhabitants remain present through the ending; neither human custody nor a costly rescue is mistaken for a solution to eternity.

## Revision question

Test whether the ancient archive governs the revelation, failed compromise, danger, and final choice, rather than merely ornamenting the existing city-prison. Preserve the original answer to destruction and locks, the captives' independent lives, and the immediate cost. The revised scope need not make an unsupported claim that all existence would end if this book burned.
