# Provisional outline

## Promised experience

The request explicitly gives a dangerous Book of Eternity, custody under lock and key, and a questioner who sees destruction as the obvious solution. “I don’t get it” invites the reader to share that reasonable skepticism. I infer a pleasure in an answer that changes the meaning of the book and of keeping it, with the explanation made consequential in a choice. The imposing title permits wonder or dread, but the request specifies neither tone nor scale, character identities, relationship, nor ending. The direction below chooses them; they are not additional requirements.

## Provisional direction

Working title: **The People Between the Covers**. Close third person through Ysabet, a rebel officer sent to burn the defeated king’s notorious book. She and its elderly custodian, Orrel, are adults who have never met. The story takes place in the book’s underground chamber during the palace’s capture. Aim for accessible, ominous wonder with a decisive moral reversal, at roughly 1,800–2,500 words; non-graphic peril.

The book is the king’s portable prison: a real city and its living inhabitants have been folded into its pages. Within it they live and think, but bodies do not age and the light remains at the hour when the city was taken. A narrow opening permits sight and speech, but is not safe to touch: direct contact between a captive and someone outside begins a one-for-one exchange. No fully-flat opening is required. The king built that exchange into his spell to demand replacements for prisoners he released. His living captives sustain the stored confinement; the volume is not an independently casting nonliving mind. Destroying the page destroys the place and people held there. The locked covers prevent anyone from making that contact. These are specific properties of this prison, not laws of all magic. The former king is dead; his spell has not conveniently died with him.

Ysabet initially sees Orrel as a collaborator preserving the royal instrument of terror. Orrel did preserve it, and has refused releases that would require new victims. He cannot claim that indefinite confinement is freedom or that his custody has solved it. Their disagreement concerns what they can actually do with this dangerous object now, not whether Ysabet is too foolish to ask the question.

## Candidate scenes and ending

1. Ysabet arrives with the revolution’s destruction warrant and a shuttered fire lantern. She speaks the prompt’s question, with the apparent typo rendered as “is” in dialogue. Orrel answers that they could have burned it, and asks her to hear one witness before doing so. Above them the victors are burning the royal archives; keeping the book will cost Ysabet more than a change of opinion.
2. Orrel shows a damaged leaf. A former attempt to burn the book left a black gap across its miniature city. Through a narrow opening Ysabet hears people calling at windows, sees a woman lean out to argue with the keeper about the promised release, and recognizes the city’s bell tower from before its unexplained disappearance. The inhabitants disagree with Orrel and display independent lives; this is evidence, not merely his assertion that marks on paper are people.
3. Orrel warns Ysabet to keep beyond reach of the page. A desperate captive leans through the narrow opening as if falling; Ysabet reflexively catches the outstretched hand. Her practical habit of catching people pulled from rubble briefly overcomes the warning. She begins to become lettering while the captive’s hand becomes full-sized. Orrel breaks the contact and closes the book before either finishes crossing. The cover stop has not failed and nobody widens the gap: contact caused the danger. Neither person is made a villain for trying to save a life or escape. The final conversation must happen with everyone out of the captive’s reach.
4. Ysabet’s soldiers reach the chamber, expecting the public destruction the warrant promised. Their senior sergeant refuses her countermand: the council expressly denied its officers discretion to preserve royal weapons. Burning is no longer morally tempting to Ysabet; the immediate obstacle is getting this real, dangerous evidence out of a burning palace without allowing the order to be carried out. She makes the sergeant a concrete offer: arrest her for defying the warrant, and take the locked book intact as evidence for her trial. His authority lets him seize and preserve evidence when arresting an officer; it does not let him accept an officer’s private repeal of the council’s order. He agrees to that route only when she gives up her sword and command. Her final address to the captives is a condition she secures before her hands are bound. Keep the negotiation brief and rooted in the book, not a legal subplot.
5. While the sergeant waits to take the keys and bind her, Orrel reopens the pinned gap and everyone stays beyond an arm’s reach. Ysabet tells the prisoners exactly what has been decided: the book will go intact to judgment, but they have not been freed and she cannot promise the outcome. She gives them her name so their accusations can reach their new external witness. A captive asks whether the burning has stopped. She closes the lantern’s shutter; Orrel shuts and locks the book; the sergeant takes the keys and binds Ysabet. Only then do the soldiers carry the sealed volume out. The last image is the captive city being carried past its new witness under guard. Survival from this threatened execution is secured, not guaranteed future safety. Avoid a speech explaining the moral or pretending that arrest or better custody has liberated anyone.

## Question for discussion

Both independent readers locate a thin climax in the earlier beat where every soldier simply followed Ysabet and her cost was postponed. The new refusal produces an immediate loss of freedom and authority. Full liberation is neither an explicit requirement nor a requirement inferred by either reader; the completed action is stopping the execution of the book’s captives. Test whether the arrest achieves that cleanly without transferring attention to Ysabet’s career. The unchanging hour and unaging inhabitants make the name Eternity specific without requiring a separate cosmology or subplot.
