# Three Nights at the Top

Elspeth Kedri signed the authorization because the man across her desk had already explained it twice, and on the third explanation his mouth had begun moving a fraction behind his voice.

The paper concerned two drivers, a van with a broken side door, and six crates of cooking oil that had arrived at the wrong warehouse. Ordinary trouble. Expensive only if neglected. She drew her private hook beneath her name, blotted the wet ink with the edge of her hand, and pushed the sheet away before the trembling in her fingers became visible.

“Anything else?” she asked.

The man gathered the paper with the reverence people had lately mistaken for respect. At twenty-three, Elspeth was the youngest boss the city had seen in anyone’s useful memory. Men twice her age lowered their voices outside her office. Restaurant owners sent fruit in winter. Policemen returned her calls. In the street below, the all-night bakery sent warm yeast through the floorboards, and two guards stood at the foot of the stairs to make sure fear climbed them one person at a time.

Nobody saw that she had read the same line four times.

She had not slept since three mornings ago. A market stabbing had threatened the truce; Axholm had raised his monthly price; a missed payroll had put two captains outside her door. Whenever she lay down, another phone began. By now the crises could be sorted only by whichever was speaking.

“No, boss.”

“Then go.”

He went. Rain fretted the windows. Somewhere below, metal racks clattered and the bakery’s first wholesale loaves rolled toward ovens hot enough to make the office warm even in the wet hour after midnight.

An envelope lay beside Elspeth’s fresh signature. The driver’s folder had covered that patch of desk until he gathered it.

It was cheap gray paper, unsealed, with no name on it. It had not been there when the driver came in. Her desk stood six paces from the only door, and Elspeth had watched that door the entire meeting except for the instant when she had bent to retrieve her pen.

She did not call the guards.

Inside were seven carbon copies and a municipal document printed on paper the color of old teeth. Four copies carried her signature, her private hook, and the tiny dot she placed inside the first letter of Kedri when she meant an order to take immediate effect. One reassigned the crews who watched the east docks. One released emergency wages. One put three refrigerated trucks under Olek Velcor’s direct authority. One changed the night access at the cold-storage building.

The other sheets were a payroll she had never seen. Eleven men, all paid from the emergency release, none assigned where her copy said they were assigned. At the bottom was a loading time: 4:40.

The municipal document listed the locations of a predawn sweep. Every place contained people still loyal to Elspeth. None contained Olek’s eleven men.

She looked again at the first order. Three evenings ago, after a stabbing outside a fish market, Olek had brought her a shift proposal. He had said the east-dock watchers were too angry to face the rival crews without starting a war. Move them north, he had advised. Put calmer people near the water.

Had the list beneath that order named the north warehouses?

Elspeth remembered the cut on Olek’s thumb. She remembered the bitter coffee. She remembered signing while two callers argued into separate phones. The page below the signature had become a white rectangle in her mind.

She laid the envelope beneath the authorization for cooking oil and called Olek.

He answered before the first ring had finished. “You should be asleep.”

“So should you.”

“I get paid to worry.”

“Not as much as you think.” She leaned back and closed her eyes, because without the room to manage she could hold his voice in one place. “The south crews say their wages are short.”

“They always say that.”

“This time they have arithmetic.”

A pause. Rain ticked against the glass. “If Odette has been showing them the emergency column, she’s comparing the wrong totals. The warehouse retention comes off first.”

Elspeth opened her eyes.

Warehouse retention appeared only on the parallel payroll.

“Does it?” she said.

“You approved it.” His voice had changed by less than a step. “Elspeth, we’ve had rival cars near the east route all evening. Let me put the cold-store team on defensive readiness. We can settle the books after morning.”

“No movement until I call.”

“They’re already in place. You gave me night access.”

She looked at her hook, copied in black carbon. “Then they can enjoy the shelter.”

“You sound tired.”

“You sound concerned.”

“I am concerned. A boss who keeps peace by answering every knock eventually becomes the door.”

It was good enough to have been prepared. Elspeth almost admired it. “Wait for my call.”

She ended the connection before he could ask where she was going.

The two guards downstairs watched her descend with the careful blankness of men who had never seen her stumble. She told one to stay with the office and took the other as far as the car. Then she sent him back too. If the envelope was bait, bringing loyal muscle would only show whoever had set it which muscle remained hers.

She drove three blocks before the traffic lights began arranging themselves into the route she used for Councillor Dastin Axholm. By the sixth block she had forgotten whether she had checked the rear mirror. She checked it three times in quick succession, furious at the uncertainty rather than the empty street.

Axholm met her in a municipal service stairwell that smelled of wet stone and industrial soap. The public entrance had closed hours ago, but a side door admitted cleaners, councillors, and anyone whose business improved without witnesses. He wore a raincoat over evening clothes and looked rested enough to be indecent.

“This is becoming a costly habit,” he said.

“You raised your price yesterday.”

“Costs rise.”

“You’re sweeping six of my locations before dawn.”

Axholm’s expression remained composed. He touched the knot of his tie as if testing whether the accusation had wrinkled it. “The municipality periodically remembers it has duties.”

“Not this precisely.”

“If you’ve heard a rumor, I advise you to move anything embarrassing.”

Elspeth took out her phone. She had photographed only one section of the raid list and one section of the payroll, enough to show the same coded warehouse reference on both. She held the screen where he could see and not touch.

His eyes stopped at the reference.

“I don’t know what that is,” he said.

“Then you won’t mind if the police chief sees it beside the messages where you sold me his schedules.”

“That would create questions for you as well.”

“Yes.” Elspeth braced one hand against the rail. “Mine are asked in rooms without minutes.”

He looked at her hand, then at her face. “Who else has this?”

“The person who put it inside my guarded office.”

Axholm’s polished tone vanished. “You’ve lost control of your own floor.”

“And you’ve lost control of your list.”

The cleaner’s lift groaned somewhere behind the wall. Axholm considered the image again. “I have never met Olek Velcor privately.”

Elspeth had not said Olek’s name.

“Of course not.”

“A sweep can be delayed because of staffing concerns. Until seven.”

“Eight.”

“Seven. After that, I will need a reason with paperwork under it.” He raised his own phone and photographed her screen before she could turn it away. “And this conversation will affect the price of everything.”

She let him have the picture. His fear had bought less than his cooperation would have, but fear was what he sold most honestly.

Back in the car, Elspeth watched rain gather in the orange light over the empty passenger seat. The clock said 1:53. She counted backward from the loading time and got two different answers.

She called the rival boss instead.

No greeting came from the other end. Elspeth could hear glasses being stacked and a woman laughing somewhere far from the receiver. The rival boss did business in rooms that pretended not to be offices.

“Your convoy at 4:40,” Elspeth said. “Two produce trucks and the gray car. Don’t send it through the east docks.”

“You’ve mistaken me for someone who accepts directions.”

“The cold store has eleven people waiting for it.”

“Your people?”

“Not by sunrise.”

The laughter on the other end ceased as a door closed. “This sounds like a clumsy way to move my convoy somewhere worse.”

“Then leave it where it is and count the shooters yourself.”

“If I divert, you follow. If I don’t, you claim you warned me. Either way, you make your quarrel my expense.”

Elspeth pressed two fingers to the ache above her right eye. Nobody in the truce confused information with kindness. “Name the expense.”

“The east-dock route. One month. No fees to you, no inspections by your crews, and no pursuit tonight.”

That route paid for the peace. Smuggled cigarettes, stolen electronics, untaxed liquor: small ugly rivers that pooled into wages, bribes, and compensation when some frightened boy broke a shop window while proving his courage. Without it, she would have to close two operations or leave half her people unpaid.

Olek knew that. His new war did not merely promise territory. It made insolvency look like cowardice.

“Two weeks,” she said.

“Six.”

“One month.”

“Agreed. I’ll move the convoy when I hear you say the route is mine.”

Elspeth watched a bead of rain travel down the windshield and divide itself around a fleck of dirt. “The route is yours for one month.”

“You sound terrible, Kedri.”

“Move your trucks.”

The line went dead. At 2:07, Elspeth’s largest reliable income belonged to a woman who did not like her, and Olek’s war had no target.

She returned to the bakery office expecting armed men. She found Odette Naverin sitting in the chair before her desk with her damp coat folded across her knees.

Odette kept the crew’s official accounts and looked like the sort of person a room forgot as soon as she stopped speaking. Olek called that useful. Elspeth had called it safe. Tonight Odette’s hair was plastered to her cheeks, and the skin around her thumbnail was torn raw.

“Did the envelope change anything?” Odette asked.

Elspeth closed the door. “You had one chance to answer before I asked. That was it.”

Odette looked toward the desk, where the cooking-oil authorization still covered the papers. “Then ask.”

“Who helped you get into this room?”

“No one. I put it inside the driver’s folder before he came up. You sign his authorizations without checking the back because he smells like fryer grease and stays too long.”

The answer struck harder because it was accurate. Elspeth stood between Odette and the door. Her body knew this part despite the sleeplessness: narrow the distance, make the other person decide whether the next movement was an attack. Odette clutched the folded coat but did not rise.

“You copied my private mark,” Elspeth said.

“Carbon copied. I didn’t invent anything.”

“You hid accounts.”

“Olek ordered me to separate warehouse retention from the ordinary payroll.”

“You could have come to me.”

Odette’s gaze sharpened. “I did. Tuesday afternoon. I said the emergency release and the warehouse totals did not reconcile. You said if I couldn’t add two columns after lunch, I should find different work.”

Elspeth remembered Odette standing at the edge of a room full of angry crew captains. She remembered wanting one person to bring her a problem small enough to dismiss. She did not remember looking at the figures.

“Show me,” she said.

Odette’s shoulders lowered by a degree. She uncovered the papers and arranged them by consequence rather than date. The first order moved Elspeth’s most dependable crews into the six locations on Axholm’s list. The wage release paid Olek’s chosen eleven while everyone else waited. The vehicle order gave them a way to reach the cold store without using cars associated with Olek. The access change put them inside before the rival convoy was due.

Separately, each order answered an emergency Elspeth could recall. A stabbing. A wage threat. Spoiled cargo blocking three trucks. A lock damaged in an attempted theft. Olek had not forged her name. He had curated her attention.

“How many payments went out?” Elspeth asked.

“Three. The rest are queued for morning.”

“Stop them.”

“On whose authority?”

The question had no fear in it now, only accounts. Odette needed an instruction that would survive Elspeth changing her mind, Olek claiming precedence, or a frightened clerk choosing whichever voice sounded older.

Elspeth pulled the unsigned authorization pad toward her, then pushed it away. “Yours. Reconciliation hold. Two people verify every release, and neither can be me or Olek.”

“For how long?”

“Until noon.”

Odette wrote it in her own notebook. “And after noon?”

“Ask me when I’ve slept.”

Together they called the crews assigned to Axholm’s six targets. Elspeth did not announce a betrayal. She told each captain that the municipal sweep had shifted and ordered them out before seven. Then Odette called a second number from the ledger and made someone else repeat the instruction. Two captains complied. One demanded Olek’s confirmation. One said half his people had already left for the cold store. At another location, the phone rang unanswered. The last captain listened, swore at both Elspeth and Olek, and announced that everyone there was going home.

No one pledged loyalty. By three-thirty, Elspeth had fewer people, less money, and a better idea where each stood.

The cold-storage loading bay occupied the back of a windowless building near the water. Three refrigerated trucks waited under white security lamps, their engines off. Meltwater crept from beneath a loading door and shone on the concrete. Elspeth entered alone, leaving Odette at the office with the accounts and instructions to send the complete reconciliation to every crew captain if Elspeth did not call by five.

Olek stood at a folding table with the four original orders laid out before him.

He was forty-six, broad without vanity, dressed in the same dark work coat he wore when inspecting warehouses. Seven of the eleven names on the parallel payroll waited around the bay. Two held guns low against their legs. The others had the uncertain posture of men attending a meeting that might later be described as a battle.

“You came without a guard,” Olek said.

“You moved my guards into a police list.”

“Your guards. Your order.” He touched the papers. “Your signature.”

Elspeth stopped on the opposite side of the table. Close up, the originals were worse than the copies. Her pen had scored the paper where exhaustion made her press too hard. The private dots sat inside each K.

Olek addressed the room. “She’ll say they’re forged.”

Nobody looked convinced enough for him.

“I signed all four,” Elspeth said.

One of the gunmen shifted his weight. Olek went very still, denied the denial he had prepared to defeat.

Elspeth placed a fingertip on the first order. “You told me this moved hot heads away from the rival crews. It moved my captains onto Axholm’s raid list. You said this money covered late warehouse wages. It bought the people in this room. You said the trucks were trapped behind spoiled stock. You needed clean vehicles. You said thieves damaged the cold-store lock. You were reserving the bay.”

“I used what you approved,” Olek said. “That is what operations means when the boss can’t stay awake long enough to operate.”

He had named the part she could not dismiss. The crew was cash-poor. Axholm charged more every month. The truce preserved lives and storefronts, but peace did not pay as quickly as taking a route from someone weaker. Every captain waited for Elspeth’s answer because she had trained them to believe that sharing knowledge was the first stage of stealing power.

Olek leaned both hands on the table. “You’re finished whether you admit it or not. Let the convoy come. We take it, we take back the docks, and the city remembers there is a cost to leaning on us. Afterward you stay in the front office. Your name still settles people. I handle the balance sheet.”

He thought he was offering dignity. In his accounting, it might even have been generous.

“The convoy changed course an hour ago,” Elspeth said.

The bay seemed to widen around the silent trucks.

“No,” Olek said.

“Axholm delayed the sweep. The captains know which locations you sold him. Some are leaving. Some are waiting to see who pays them. And the rival boss owns the east-dock route for the next month.”

Olek’s face tightened. “You gave away our best route.”

“I gave away your target and the income you promised these people.”

One of the men near the wall muttered a curse. Another asked, “What about the morning payments?”

“Held,” Elspeth said. “The official payroll will be reconciled by Odette. The parallel one goes to every captain if anyone gives an order in my name after tonight.”

Olek stared at her as if looking for the vanished hours in her face. “So this is your victory? Half a crew and no east route?”

“No.” Her tongue felt thick, and she chose the words with the same care she should have given the papers. “It is what remains after I signed what I did not read and you spent it on a war.”

She took an access card from her pocket and dropped it over his originals. “Your authority is revoked. You can leave with your clothes and what is personally yours. You do not take the trucks. You do not take the queued money. Anyone who wants to leave with you may go.”

One of the gunmen laughed once, without humor. “And if he says no?”

Elspeth looked at the weapon, then at him. “Then he owns a loading bay, seven people, no target, no payroll, and a raid delayed until seven. Ask him what that is worth.”

Olek looked toward the two guns. Neither man raised his. Elspeth had counted on the signatures and schedules: he needed his takeover to look like experienced hands restoring order. An unarmed body on the concrete would leave him the violence he claimed was necessary and none of the income or followers that made him a boss.

He swept the originals from the table. “You think making yourself smaller makes you harder to hit.”

“Tonight it does.”

“The rival will keep the route. Axholm will eat you alive. The people who stayed will leave when wages don’t come.”

“Probably some of them.”

He searched her face for bravado and found too much fatigue to mistake. “You won’t last the month.”

“Then you can wait a month.”

Olek put the papers inside his coat. Elspeth let him. Odette had clean copies and the account trail; the originals no longer controlled who knew what.

Five people left with him. One of the gunmen set his weapon on the folding table before following. The other carried his out, pointed at the ground. Two men remained by the wall. After the outer door shut, one asked whether they were still being paid.

“Late,” Elspeth said. “And less if you lie to me about the hours.”

The man looked toward the door Olek had used. “Honest, at least.”

“Don’t spread that around.”

She did not follow Olek. Pursuit would break the stand-down she had bought and turn his failed war into a smaller successful one. She called Odette at 4:51 and told her which people had left. Then she sat on the edge of the loading platform until the concrete stopped tilting beneath her feet.

By the time she returned above the bakery, dawn had thinned the windows from black to gray. The ovens below were between batches, and for a few minutes the building no longer trembled.

Axholm called to say seven o’clock was approaching and his future cooperation would require “a more mature arrangement.” The rival boss sent a picture of two trucks parked safely behind a locked gate, followed by a reminder that the month had already begun. Three captains reported empty posts. One wanted fuel. Another wanted to know whether Olek’s departure meant old debts were still valid. The guard at the stairs asked who was authorized to enter.

Elspeth answered the last question and none of the others.

Odette arrived with a stack of authorizations held against her chest. “These need decisions.”

Elspeth looked at the top sheet. The words were legible. Their order was not.

“I don’t know yet,” she said.

Odette waited, pencil ready.

“Hold every payment except food, fuel, and the ordinary wages already verified by two people. Nobody moves crews without a second voice confirming the destination. Wake me if Olek comes back, if Axholm moves early, or if the rival convoy turns toward our streets.”

“How long?”

Elspeth lowered herself onto the office sofa, shoes on, coat still wet. “Ninety minutes.”

“You said noon on the reconciliation hold.”

“That was before I remembered morning exists.” She closed her eyes. “Make it ninety minutes. Then bring me the things I don’t know.”

The next question never came. Sleep took her while Odette was still writing.
