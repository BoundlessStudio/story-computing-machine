# The Wrong Side of the Part

By three fifteen on the last day of the shop, I had made seventy-two decisions and none of them was the one that mattered.

SELL went on the acrylic heads and the hydraulic styling chair. DONATE went on three boxes of synthetic bobs that had survived two decades of fashion by never having participated in it. DISCARD went on dried adhesive, moth-nibbled switches, and a practice head whose remaining hair gave it the expression of a betrayed turnip.

I printed the labels in twelve-point type, centered. Grief was no excuse for crooked stationery.

The unsigned purchase contract lay beneath the appointment ledger, where I had put it while searching for a cloth to polish the mirrors. The mirrors did not need polishing. They were already honest enough to show the stripped shelves, pale rectangles on the walls, and me in my gray cardigan, reducing our mother's life's work to three verbs.

My phone vibrated.

**Four sharp,** Petra had written. **The buyer arrives at five. Please tell me we are still having a surprise-free afternoon.**

I typed, **Of course.**

Then, **Though I wondered if we might discuss—**

Then, **There are a few figures I don't quite—**

I deleted everything except **Of course**, added a smiling face so it wouldn't look hostile, and hated Petra for making me do it.

This was unfair, since Petra had not made me do anything. That was the useful feature of never saying what I wanted: other people could be guilty without the inconvenience of a trial.

Below her text sat a voice message from six months earlier. Seven minutes and twelve seconds. I had listened once, in the dark, after our mother was asleep upstairs. I had never played it again. I did not need to. Petra's voice had worn a groove through me.

I turned the phone facedown and chose a new task.

Our mother's oldest travelling case had escaped the labels because its brass catches were jammed. I worked them loose with the flat end of a tail comb. The case smelled of old powder, canvas, and that faint animal warmth human hair kept long after it belonged to no one. Inside rested a row of setting pins, a stained veil, and nothing worth saving.

The bottom sounded hollow when I knocked it.

Beneath a false panel lay a wig wrapped in yellowing tissue. It was an ash-brown bob, center-parted, blunt at the jaw. Good hair, hand-knotted, wasted on a style so obedient it seemed to apologize for occupying the box.

A card had been tucked beneath the crown.

*Wear it and meet the person you are not.*

*Heat wakes it.*

*To take it off, tell the truth neither of you wants.*

I read it twice, which was one more time than it deserved.

Our mother had loved theatre without requiring a stage. She once told a nervous bride that the right hairpiece would frighten rain away, and it had not rained until after the photographs. This proved nothing except that weather occasionally cooperated with salesmanship.

“Magical wig,” I said to the empty shop. “Naturally. Very useful inventory note.”

Still, human-hair work had to be tried before it was donated. A warped foundation could pinch, and old elastic sometimes failed without warning. I could picture Petra asking whether I had checked it. I could picture myself saying no. The second image was less plausible.

I pinned up my own hair, loosened the adjustment tabs, and lowered the wig onto my head.

I did it voluntarily. I did it while disbelieving every word on the card. Later, those facts mattered to me, though they did not make what followed less mine.

The crown settled cool against my scalp. I slipped two fingers beneath the nape and tested the tension. The foundation was exquisite: fine lace, tiny knots, a spiral of denser work around the crown as deliberate as a fingerprint. Then my skin warmed it.

My next heartbeat struck the net like a plucked string.

Heat ran outward through the knots. Not heat arriving from the wig—mine, drawn from my scalp and pulse, distributed in bright little paths I could feel but not see. The lace contracted to my head. My attention caught on it, and the whole spiral seemed to fill with everything I knew I was: quiet, careful, accommodating, tidy. The list became a cage only because I supplied the bars.

The hair brushed my jaw.

Then it brushed my shoulders.

I grabbed the styling chair as the bob poured down and out. Ash brown deepened to copper-black, bright as a banked fire wherever the light struck. The center part slewed hard to the left and bleached bone-white in a line so straight it looked cut with a razor. Sleek lengths buckled into enormous coils. They crowded my shoulders, sprang halfway down my back, and tossed after every startled movement with the momentum of an entrance.

I stared into the salon mirror.

The woman in it stared back with my face and none of my apologies.

Nothing inside me went dark. I remembered the labels. I remembered the contract. I remembered why Petra wanted the sale and why I had said yes. I simply found all those reasons unbearably boring.

“Oh,” I said, and my voice came out rich with amusement. “There you are.”

I yanked off the gray cardigan and dropped it on the DISCARD pile.

The labels offended me next. Seventy-two tiny white surrender flags. I swept them from the counter, peeled SELL off the styling chair, slapped it onto the contract, and laughed so hard the copper-black coils bounced around my face.

My phone gave me the buyer's number from the email thread. I called.

“This is Tilda Bristow,” I said when a cautious voice answered. “A small correction to today's arrangements. The shop is not for sale.”

There was a silence filled with paper and alarm. “But the appointment—”

“Is an excellent opportunity for you to discover resilience.”

I ended the call.

Before I could lower the phone, Petra rang.

“I'm stuck at the lender,” she said. “The buyer asked for a corrected debt statement before the signing. Their system is down. I'll be there by quarter to five. Do not start without me.”

“Take your time,” I said, and heard how easily the new voice could make sabotage sound considerate.

Old me would have vomited from shame. I could remember that. I could even anticipate the practical consequences with perfect clarity: breach, delay, debt, Petra's fury. Yet relief fizzed through my blood. I had spent two years making grief presentable for other people. For once, I wanted witnesses to the mess.

The shop account was still signed in on my phone. I propped it against a canister of combs and recorded myself beneath the faded awning, framed by bare display hooks.

“At four thirty,” I told the lens, “we are holding a funeral for being agreeable. There will be free hair, poor judgment, and the public burial of a terrible property sale. Dress code: something you were told was too much.”

I posted it before prudence could crawl back into fashion.

Then I tore apart the sorting scheme. SELL, DONATE, and DISCARD fluttered together across the floor. I put a silver bouffant on the betrayed turnip and arranged the heads in the window like a jury of glamorous widows. I opened every box Petra had taped. Each rip of packing tape felt like taking a full breath after years in a tight collar.

People began arriving at four twenty-six.

There were only eight at first: nearby shopkeepers, former customers, two teenagers still in school clothes, and a man who claimed he had come because his partner had sent him for groceries. By four thirty-five there were perhaps fifteen, which was enough to fill the narrow shop and make disaster feel curated.

I became magnificent.

I fitted a violet crop onto a woman who had once returned a custom piece because her husband called it “attention-seeking.”

“He was right,” I told her, turning her toward the mirror. “Seek better attention.”

The room applauded.

I gave away a reserved lace-front to someone it had not been reserved for. I knew this was theft from tomorrow's obligations. I also knew exactly which drawer held the deposit slip. The knowledge did not stop my hands. That was the horror of it, hidden inside the champagne sparkle of being watched.

I modelled three pieces at once over the impossible coils and told the crowd which customers lied about cutting their own bangs. I described our most expensive stock as “mourning hair for rich people who expect grief to have a natural-looking hairline.” A few people looked offended; more bought things. The shop account's viewer count climbed.

Every suppressed observation came out sharpened for entertainment. My jokes were accurate enough to draw blood and funny enough that the wounded laughed first.

At four forty-three, Petra pushed through the door.

She wore her good coat and carried a document folder against her ribs. Her gaze went from the crowd to the open boxes, to the wig on my head, to the SELL sticker across the contract I had pinned to the wall.

“Tilda,” she said. “What have you done?”

“Improved the closing party.”

“There wasn't supposed to be a closing party.”

“Then I've improved it infinitely.”

The crowd laughed. Petra did not. She was three years younger than I was, but exasperation had always made her look like the elder sister appointed by a stern committee.

She came behind the counter and lowered her voice. “The buyer sent me your video.”

“Then we saved on invitations.”

“Take it down. Call back. Say you're ill.”

“At last, a diagnosis for owning an opinion.”

“This isn't an opinion. It's a jointly owned building under debt, and you just announced to the neighborhood that you can make unilateral decisions about it.”

The delight in me sharpened. She had found the point that hurt and mistaken it for a warning.

I climbed onto the low fitting platform. The coils rose and settled around me, too large for modesty. “Petra would like everyone to know the shop is jointly owned. This is legally true. It was jointly inherited. It was not jointly kept open. It was not jointly cleaned at midnight, or stocked, or defended to creditors. And when our mother got sick, she was not jointly lifted from bed or washed or persuaded to eat.”

The room changed temperature.

“Stop,” Petra said.

For years I had wanted her to say I was right. Wearing the wig, I wanted something sweeter: I wanted her unable to survive my rightness.

Petra looked at the people crowded between the wig stands. “Could everyone please leave?”

“No,” I said. “They came for the funeral.”

Her mouth tightened. “You told me you could manage. Every time I offered to come, you told me not to disrupt my life. You made every decision, Tilda, and then presented me with the invoice in a language I was supposed to guess.”

It landed because it was true.

Truth did not make me gentler. It made me reach for a larger weapon.

I picked up my phone. The old voice message waited in the thread, still seven minutes and twelve seconds, still private.

Petra saw the screen. Her face emptied.

“Don't.”

Ordinarily, that one word would have stopped me. I knew it should stop me now. I remembered sitting in the dark while her recorded voice broke, remembered deciding I would never use her confession against her. The promise was present in my mind, intact.

I pressed play.

Petra's voice filled the shop.

*I couldn't come back because every time I saw her like that, I was terrified. You made it look as if I was busy, and I let you, because cowardice sounded worse. I knew you were drowning. I knew, and I still—*

Petra knocked the phone from my hand. It skidded beneath the styling chair. The message kept playing.

No one laughed.

She crouched, but I was faster. I snatched up the phone, clipped the ugliest thirty seconds, and posted them with one line about the co-owner who had inherited half the shop and none of the sickroom.

The act took six taps.

Petra stared at me as if six taps were a distance she could not cross.

“The wig didn't invent that cruelty,” she said. “It just made you enjoy using it.”

My screen lit with a new email before I could answer.

The buyer had withdrawn the offer.

There it was: the thing I had wanted. The five-o'clock signature evaporated. The shop remained ours, along with its debt, its emptied shelves, and the echo of Petra's voice dying in the sudden quiet.

Petra picked up her folder.

“You wanted me punished,” she said. “You were just too polite to ask.”

Then she left.

The crowd followed in embarrassed pieces. One person set a free wig back on the counter. Another avoided my eyes while continuing to record. Within minutes I stood alone among ripped boxes and overturned categories.

The copper-black mass in the mirror was glorious. Its white part divided my head like a verdict.

I opened a live broadcast.

The shop account still had hundreds of viewers. Reactions climbed the screen: flames, question marks, hearts, demands for context. I angled the phone so the empty shop stretched behind me like conquered territory.

“Since everyone is suddenly interested in inheritance,” I began, “let's discuss who morally owns this place.”

I knew Petra owned half. I knew care could not transmute signatures. I knew I had hidden my resentment inside obedience, then called the hiding generosity. None of that knowledge had vanished. It merely stood at a distance while self-interest wore my skin beautifully.

My thumb hovered over the button that would send me live.

I wanted to press it.

I wanted the shop. I wanted the watching faces. I wanted Petra to hurt enough that my hurt would finally look proportionate. And beneath all of it, humiliating in its smallness, I wanted my sister to have noticed without being told.

At my temples, the crown pulsed.

Craft lived in my fingers deeper than temperament. I slid them beneath the coils and felt the lace. Each beat of my heart sent warmth along the radiating knots. The foundation was not a mind wrestling me. It was a focus, exquisitely worked and utterly inert without the living pattern I kept feeding it: meek against monstrous, silence against spectacle, one self defined by the rejection of the other.

The card had not said which truth, only that neither of us wanted it. There was no *us*. There were two postures I had used to avoid the same naked sentence.

I ended the broadcast before it began.

The empty shop offered no applause.

“I want the shop,” I said, “and I wanted Petra to know without ever making myself ask.”

The sentence hurt more than any accusation. It blamed no one. It guaranteed nothing. It made my desire visible where it could be refused.

The white part blurred.

Heat drained from the spiral of knots. The huge coils lost their spring and collapsed against my shoulders, copper-black fading through brown. The crown slackened under my fingers. I lifted the wig free.

By the time it reached the counter, it was the same drab, symmetrical bob I had taken from the case.

My gray cardigan waited on the rubbish pile.

I put it on, then took it off again.

Deleting the post did not delete the copies. A clipped version already had more views than the shop account had followers. The buyer's withdrawal remained politely final. Three messages demanded refunds for things I had given away. One asked whether the funeral would continue tomorrow.

I typed a public statement that said the disclosure had been mine, made without Petra's consent, and that no circumstance excused it. I did not mention the wig. “Magical hair made me do it” had the structure of a joke and the ethics of a trapdoor.

Then I sent Petra a message.

**I removed it. What I did is still mine. I am sorry I played and posted your message. I won't ask you to forgive me. Please come back only if you want to watch me make sure it cannot happen again. After that, I want to propose a fair process for the shop. You can say no.**

She did not answer.

At five twenty, she returned.

She stayed inside the locked door, coat still buttoned, while I set the ash-brown wig on a canvas block at the workbench. I turned the styling chair away from the mirror. For once I did not want to see myself as a client whose discomfort could be managed.

“Is that it?” Petra asked.

“Yes.”

“Can it do it again?”

“If someone wears it and supplies what it needs, I think so.”

“You think.”

“I only know what it did with me.”

I showed her the spiral construction at the crown: the tiny central knot from which the tension spread, the pathways that had carried my heat and pulse. Then, with a blunt needle and tweezers, I began to unwork it.

The first knot resisted because it was old and fine, not because it wished to remain. I teased it apart. Hair loosened across the block. I took out the next, then the next, destroying in minutes the skill of hands I would never identify.

Petra watched until the foundation opened and the wig became ordinary loose hair sliding onto the worktable.

“I'm sorry,” I said again. “The grievance was real. The cruelty was a choice I made while cruelty felt good. Both are true.”

“That doesn't fix it.”

“No.”

“People heard me.”

“Yes.”

“I don't know if I can forgive you.”

My old instinct rose at once: reassure her, release her, punish myself so thoroughly she would have to comfort me. I let the instinct pass without obeying it.

“You don't have to decide that today,” I said.

I put the loose hair in the DISCARD box. Then I peeled the label off.

On a fresh label I wrote DECIDE TOGETHER and stuck it over the torn cardboard.

Petra gave it a tired, suspicious look. “Stationery. Bold.”

“I'm starting within my range.”

Her mouth moved, almost a smile and not forgiveness.

I took the unsigned sale contract from the wall. On its blank back, I wrote what I should have written before the first box was packed: a formal accounting of the shop's debt, both our ownership, and the care work I had performed; an independent appraisal; thirty days for me to seek financing to buy Petra's share at a fair value; a sale after that if I could not. I added that I would make every possible correction to the public harm without repeating her private words.

“I will agree to the accounting and the appraisal,” Petra said. “That's all I'm agreeing to.”

“All right.”

“I am not agreeing to thirty days.”

“All right.”

“And I am not agreeing to save this place because you finally admitted you want it.”

The shop around us looked smaller without the wigs: just scuffed floorboards, shelves, two sisters, and a future that had declined to label itself.

“All right,” I said.

Then I read her the rest of my terms aloud.
