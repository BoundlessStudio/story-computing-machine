# Provisional direction

The explicit promise is a goth teenage boy beginning to study witchcraft, actively concealing it from parents who remain completely oblivious. I infer the pleasure of secret, genuinely strange power behind conspicuous but harmless-looking goth style, with comic near discoveries. The intended tone is affectionate, eerie, and increasingly breathless; the scale is one bedroom opening into a dangerous nocturnal wood. The central relationship is a boy whose loving parents enthusiastically make every hobby public, and who badly wants this new fascination to belong to him. The payoff is an earned first piece of witchcraft, a private glimpse of a larger world, and a thoroughly mistaken parental account of the evening. These are interpretations, not added requirements; no secret parental expertise or knowing wink is planned.

Orson Dacre, sixteen, has hidden a battered witchcraft primer inside an ordinary schoolbook. His parents accept the black clothes, eyeliner, and skull collection so eagerly that his last attempt at a private interest became a family-chat slideshow. He wants witchcraft to be real, and wants time to be bad at it without an audience.

- Alone before dinner, he studies rather than improvises, and succeeds at the primer's first exercise: calling a living witchlight, a luminous moth from the wood between nights. The moth is beautiful. Orson's overlooked closing instruction leaves the little passage open.
- Something with antlers and a hound's body follows the moth. Orson initially hides it among his room's black hanging clothes while his father brings an unsolicited improvement for his "spooky lighting." The hound can travel through connected shadows; the father's moved lamp gives it a route toward the landing. Orson has to learn this through an attempted containment that fails.
- His mother's dinner summons forces competing conversations: ordinary household questions outside the door, a very real pursuit inside. He keeps the door mostly closed, changes the light, and uses the black fabric he actually wears to lure and block the hunter. The parents' interruptions remain reasonable; he does the work of preventing them from seeing the evidence.
- The book's return passage gives him a possible reversal, but he must draw the hunter back across the threshold. He takes the witchlight and steps into the wood himself. The familiar light and his newly understood control of its shadow let him trick the hound into following a false silhouette, get home, and close the passage. His beginner's study and a risky decision both matter.
- He reaches dinner late, windblown and carrying a secret living moth. His parents conclude that he has taken up nature photography or astronomy, with their usual delighted specificity; he lets one ordinary explanation stand. Later he deliberately opens a pinhole of the wood's stars, safely this time. He is still a beginner, but the next lesson is his choice. A banal parental reminder interrupts a final impossible view, preserving both wonder and the secret.

Main question for discussion: can the first-act parental enthusiasm make his need for privacy immediate enough without turning affection into a problem the story must therapeutically resolve? I intend to keep that pressure comic and concrete, and let the real challenge be hiding and learning witchcraft.
