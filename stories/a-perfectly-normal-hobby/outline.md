# Provisional direction

The explicit promise is a goth teenage boy beginning to study witchcraft, actively concealing it from parents who remain completely oblivious. I infer the pleasure of secret, genuinely strange power behind conspicuous but harmless-looking goth style, with comic near discoveries. The intended tone is affectionate, eerie, and increasingly breathless; the scale is one bedroom opening into a dangerous nocturnal wood. The central relationship is a boy whose loving parents enthusiastically make every hobby public, and who badly wants this new fascination to belong to him. The payoff is an earned first piece of witchcraft, a private glimpse of a larger world, and a thoroughly mistaken parental account of the evening. These are interpretations, not added requirements; no secret parental expertise or knowing wink is planned.

Orson Dacre, sixteen, has hidden a battered witchcraft primer inside an ordinary schoolbook. His parents accept the black clothes, eyeliner, and skull collection so eagerly that his last attempt at a private interest became a family-chat slideshow. He wants witchcraft to be real, and wants time to be bad at it without an audience.

- Alone before dinner, he studies and succeeds at the primer's first exercise: calling a living witchlight, a luminous moth from the wood between nights. He learns to guide its flight and observes his shadow moving with its light. A parental interruption distracts him from closing the little passage.
- Something with antlers and a hound's body follows the moth. A failed attempt to contain it with a thrown shirt reveals that it follows a moving silhouette rather than a motionless body. His father's moved lamp then gives the hound a particular shadow route toward the landing; Orson restores the light and asks his father to keep outside while he deals with something breakable. Privacy no longer outweighs safety. He will warn them if he cannot contain it.
- His mother's dinner summons forces competing conversations: ordinary household questions outside the closed door, a real pursuit inside. He keeps the parents out of the dangerous room while devising a return route. Their interruptions remain reasonable; he prevents their seeing the evidence. Sound crosses normally: wind, leaves, and a bump have ordinary explanations; he cannot rely on an inaudible monster.
- He takes the witchlight just across the open passage and deliberately draws the hunter after him. Bedroom and parental voices remain present, with his mother intending to bring dinner upstairs in one minute. In the wind of the wood he uses his coat, the moth's directed light, and a broad pale standing stone to make a moving false silhouette. The hound pursues it; he gets home and shuts the passage. The trick uses previously observed behavior, not a newly acquired shadow power.
- He conceals the moth in an opaque black sleeve before his mother enters with dinner. Her mistaken new-hobby account follows only the ordinary equipment and residue she can see. Later he deliberately opens and closes a pinhole of the wood's stars, safely this time, before another parental interruption. He has kept a secret living companion and real access to a larger world. He is still a beginner, but the next lesson is his choice.

Main question for discussion: can the first-act parental enthusiasm make his need for privacy immediate enough without turning affection into a problem the story must therapeutically resolve? I intend to keep that pressure comic and concrete, and let the real challenge be hiding and learning witchcraft.
