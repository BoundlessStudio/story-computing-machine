# A Seat by the Door

The explicit promise is a person who repeatedly escapes an afterlife where reincarnation is not supposed to happen, dies again, and receives an offer of godhood because the gods want to keep track of them. The distinctive pleasure I infer is an escape artist making death's authorities change tactics, with the divine invitation itself becoming the next contest of ingenuity. Comic exasperation is suggested by “if only so they can keep track of you”; tone, scale, mechanism, motive, personal relationships, acceptance, and final outcome remain open. This direction chooses an irreverent first-person caper with real wonder, an actual apotheosis, and an escape that changes the arrangement rather than simply repeating the opening.

## Provisional direction

Orra has returned seventeen times, entering a newly born body with no previous occupant and gradually recovering her memories. Within this pantheon's jurisdiction, the dead are meant to remain dead; neither they nor Orra can establish anything universal about every people's continuation. She enjoys living, including its unfinished, surprising particulars, rather than pursuing one lost person. Her faces, trades, appetites, mistakes, and attachments have differed across lives. She has also helped living people escape captivity in several of them. Those deeds have left a small, patchwork mortal devotion to “the One Who Got Out,” which she has mistaken for lucky folklore.

The principal opposing presence is Lethra, the death goddess: formidable, observant, personally tired of fetching Orra, and sufficiently honest to state the offer's purpose. Sarro, a war god, supplies a few contrasting reactions rather than a second governing conflict. This local council's gods are living beings; their thrones are offices rather than animate judges. Godhood here joins power, authority, and worship. They can voluntarily embody themselves in the living world at shrines where mortals invite them, while their council peers can follow their whereabouts by a light in the council's map of the sky. The offer confers that same right: their intended achievement is permanent, reliable knowledge of where Orra is, not an impossible guarantee that a colleague will never leave the hall. Orra initially mistakes it for comfortable imprisonment. The negotiation discovers a bargain both sides can actually keep.

1. **The eighteenth arrival.** Orra appears in the council hall expecting another escape problem. There is an empty throne instead of an escort to the ordinary afterlife. Lethra states that she is being offered divinity, then admits why. Orra tests the distance to a window while ostensibly admiring the room. Lethra moves without looking to block it: this time the gods have studied her.
2. **The evidence of nuisance.** The hall's views show three brief, concrete escapes and their differing methods: Orra returning to a departing festival procession by putting on the mask of its missing dancer; persuading an underworld beast to carry her out by joining its fight instead of fleeing its mouth; using her own funeral song as a route back into a living voice. These are remembered deeds, not proof that the props use magic independently. The returned life then begins through her own exceptional passage into new, previously unoccupied flesh. Lethra has closed those particular routes. The memories establish inventiveness, genuine previous returns, the difficulty of keeping her, and her appetite for the world without becoming a full chronological biography.
3. **The attractive cage.** The gods demonstrate what a god can taste and touch, grant, lose, and do. Orra asks specific questions about leaving; Lethra states the shrine-visit right and the tracking light plainly. Orra refuses the first offered title, an ornamental “Patron of Rest,” because no one would pray to her for that. She recognizes the offer would provide a living divine body and release her from having to die and start at infancy to get another lifetime. Lethra is offering a colleague's freedom under continual observation; Orra wants to know whether the freedom is real.
4. **Choosing the office.** A living prisoner prays to the One Who Got Out, using the same rough charm Orra once taught fellow captives. That prayer is not a new public crisis or a replacement protagonist: it reveals the worship her deeds have already earned. Other small invocations arrive under different names. Orra recognizes their common practices and claims the office they actually sustain: escapes. Lethra recognizes a genuine grant of authority backed by existing power and worship, rather than making belief create arbitrary powers. Orra insists that the council explicitly grant the same embodied visiting right as every seated god. Lethra agrees, knowing that she can finally follow her.
5. **An escape under their eyes.** Orra accepts and receives living divine breath, office, and the tracking light. She answers the prisoner's invitation immediately, using the tiny scratched shrine in his cell as a place to embody herself. Her first divine act makes an impossible way out of that cell, using the escape capacity she already possessed at a newly granted scale. She goes through with the prisoner. In the hall, the new light moves across the star map; Lethra can at last point exactly to her. The promotion genuinely works for both parties, while its first practical consequence exhausts the war god.
6. **The seat.** A short aftermath lets the prisoner choose his own direction once free, then returns attention to Orra in the living city: hungry, newly powerful, able to hear the next appeal. Lethra appears beside her, having followed the light, and sets down an ordinary stool at a food stall. Orra's promised place among the gods now includes this god beside her in the world she kept escaping toward. Their exchange earns the title without converting the caper into duty, maintenance, or a lesson in accepting death. The last action opens another invitation or escape rather than explaining a moral.

The exact escape memories, mortal setting, title of the office, final exchange, and length remain available to improve in discussion and prose. Protect the central negotiation, genuine repeat returns, Orra's active choices, the gods' candid containment motive, and the two-sided payoff: they finally know where she is, and she has never been so difficult to keep at home.
