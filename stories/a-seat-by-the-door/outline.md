# A Seat by the Door

The explicit promise is a person who repeatedly escapes an afterlife where reincarnation is not supposed to happen, dies again, and receives an offer of godhood because the gods want to keep track of them. The distinctive pleasure I infer is an escape artist making death's authorities change tactics, with the divine invitation itself becoming the next contest of ingenuity. Comic exasperation is suggested by “if only so they can keep track of you”; tone, scale, mechanism, motive, personal relationships, acceptance, and final outcome remain open. This direction chooses an irreverent first-person caper with real wonder, an actual apotheosis, and an escape that changes the arrangement rather than simply repeating the opening.

## Provisional direction

Orra has returned seventeen times, entering a newly born body with no previous occupant and gradually recovering her memories. Within this pantheon's jurisdiction, the dead are meant to remain dead; neither they nor Orra can establish anything universal about every people's continuation. She enjoys living, including its unfinished, surprising particulars, rather than pursuing one lost person. Her faces, trades, appetites, mistakes, and attachments have differed across lives. She has also helped living people escape captivity in several of them. Those deeds have left a small, patchwork mortal devotion to “the One Who Got Out.” The gods identify this early as one reason a real promotion is possible: it is evidence of eligibility, not a late revelation that she was destined to serve petitioners.

The principal opposing presence is Lethra, the death goddess: formidable, observant, personally tired of fetching Orra, and sufficiently honest to state the offer's purpose. Other gods remain a reacting council rather than a third named protagonist. This local council's gods are living beings; their thrones are offices rather than animate judges. Godhood here joins power, authority, and worship. They can voluntarily take their living divine bodies to the mortal world through places where people worship them; their council peers can follow their whereabouts by a light in the council's map of the sky. Orra's old charms have been made into tiny shrines, including one scratched into a market arch in the city where she most recently lived. The new office would confer that same travel right, but Lethra initially proposes a year's residence to learn the office. Their intended achievement is permanent, reliable knowledge of where Orra is, not an impossible guarantee that a colleague will never leave the hall. Orra initially suspects comfortable imprisonment. The negotiation discovers a bargain both sides can actually keep, at the decisive moment rather than before it.

1. **The eighteenth arrival.** Orra appears in the council hall expecting another escape problem. There is an empty throne instead of an escort to the ordinary afterlife. Lethra states that she is being offered divinity, then admits why. Orra tests the distance to a window while ostensibly admiring the room. Lethra moves without looking to block it: this time the gods have studied her.
2. **The evidence of nuisance.** Two brief, concrete remembered escapes establish different methods: Orra returning to a departing festival procession by putting on the mask of its missing dancer; and using her own funeral song as a route back into a living voice. These are remembered deeds, not proof that props use magic independently. The return then begins her own exceptional passage into new, previously unoccupied flesh. Lethra has closed those particular routes. She also points out the mortal escape charms bearing Orra's old signs: genuine, limited worship of an already powerful escape artist. The memories and charms establish eligibility, inventiveness, previous returns, and an appetite for the world without becoming a chronological biography or a calling.
3. **The attractive cage.** Lethra gives Orra a divine fruit to taste and shows the trackable light of an absent colleague: godhood has material attractions and an enforceable location. Orra asks specific questions about leaving. Lethra explains the shrine-travel right but proposes a year here to learn her place. Orra refuses “Patron of Rest” as the offered title and refuses the year. The office she chooses is escapes. She names what she actually wants now: to return today, in a body old enough to eat the hot street food she missed by dying, rather than spend another childhood remembering how to speak. The point is Orra's existing desire, not discovering other people's needs.
4. **The consequential refusal.** Lethra insists that this is a substantial offer, not another door to run through. Orra leaves the empty throne untouched. She will accept the living divine body, the office, and perpetual tracking only if her place among the gods includes the same freedom to leave that an established colleague has, beginning immediately. Otherwise she will attempt a nineteenth life by her old means, despite Lethra having closed the known routes. This risks a genuinely comfortable and powerful future rather than making the bargaining costless. Lethra must choose whether she wants a captive she still has to outwit or a colleague she can always find. Orra points to the moving light: the gods' own proposed solution makes a compulsory residence unnecessary.
5. **Agreement tested in the same action.** Lethra concedes immediate embodied travel; Orra accepts, receives living divine breath and authority, and tests that concession before anyone can add a welcoming speech. She deliberately uses the market-arch charm she has already identified, makes it the first exit of her office, and steps straight from the council hall into the living city's market. Her first divine act serves her own escape. The council's newly lit point moves to the market and stays plainly visible as she walks away from the arch: Lethra's tracking has succeeded, not been tricked or broken. The departure is the climax of the negotiation, not a subsequent rescue plot illustrating terms already settled.
6. **The seat.** Orra sits down at the street-food stall in a living body with her memories intact and begins eating the thing she wanted before the gods made their offer. Lethra appears, having followed the light, and sets down an ordinary stool. Their exchange maintains their old contest: Lethra can prove exactly where Orra is; Orra can point out that a place among the gods need not mean staying in their hall. Orra then proposes going somewhere much farther away than this market. End with movement or an appetite for the next journey, not a new prayer, a vocational lesson, or a cure for invented loneliness. Lethra's company is the concrete consequence of tracking a restless colleague, with warmth available inside the exasperation.

The exact escape memories, mortal setting, title of the office, final exchange, and length remain available to improve in discussion and prose. Protect the central negotiation, genuine repeat returns, Orra's consequential refusal and active escape, the gods' candid containment motive, and the two-sided payoff: they finally know where she is, and she has never been so difficult to keep at home.
