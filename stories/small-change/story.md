# Small Change

The first coin hatched on the baker's oven ledge. It came out of its gold shell with two wet wings, a tail no longer than a needle, and an appetite for the rye cooling beside it.

Sella stared at it. The baker stared at Sella.

"You paid me with that."

"It was round," Sella said.

The little creature sneezed. A blue flame no bigger than a candle lit the flour on the baker's sleeve. Sella slapped it out. The creature ate another piece of crust.

Outside, the guild bell began to ring. It rang for fires, invasions, and meetings about unpaid dues. Sella had a feeling this was all three.

Yesterday she had found a bowl-shaped hollow of smooth gold pieces at the back of a dragon's cave. No dragon, no traps, no bloodshed: the easiest haul of her career. She had carried the coins home in a leather purse that was now, she realized, moving against her hip.

She opened it. Nineteen bright discs shifted against one another. One had a hairline crack. Something knocked from inside.

"How many did you take?" the baker asked.

Sella counted again. "Twenty-one."

"Where are the other two?"

"One is on your oven ledge."

"I can see that one."

Sella thought of the toll she had paid at the north bridge. A single gold piece for herself and her mule. The keeper had bitten it, weighed it, and dropped it into his tin box.

The guild bell rang faster. Over the roof came a shadow wide enough to cover the street. The baker looked up. The new dragon looked up too, and gave a sound like a kettle trying to sing.

"Don't let them shoot her," Sella said.

The baker took off his apron, wrapped the little dragon in it, and thrust the bundle into her hands. "Then show them why."

In the square, the watch had rolled out its bolt thrower. Guildmaster Hollis stood beside it with a ledger open against his chest, though there was little in the safety regulations about a dragon that had followed its treasure into town.

Sella climbed onto the machine's wooden frame. "Wait!"

"Get down," Hollis called. "That thing is circling."

"She has children here."

The watch captain squinted at the apron. A gold head poked through the cloth and bit Sella's thumb. Behind them, the great dragon crossed the sun again. She was not diving. She was searching, sweeping each street with her eyes.

Sella held up her purse. It rattled and chirped.

Hollis shut his ledger. "How many?"

"Twenty-one. I have nineteen. This one was at the bakery. The last is at the north bridge."

The watch captain lowered his hand. His crew eased the bolt thrower's rope. Sella climbed down before anyone could change their mind.

The dragon landed in the square so gently that only the fountain water shook. Her scales were the color of old coins. She looked at the purse, then at the bundle in Sella's arms.

"They need me," the dragon said. Her voice was low and rough, as though she had flown a long way without stopping. "Not your ovens."

Sella emptied the purse onto the cobbles. The dragon curved one great claw around the little pile without touching it. Gold shells split. Tiny wings unfolded. The baker's hatchling wriggled free of the apron and hurried toward its mother on four uncertain feet.

The watch captain smiled before he could stop himself. Hollis did not. He had found a blank page in his ledger.

"The bridge," the dragon said.

"I know. I paid the toll with it." Sella looked at the northern road. By mule, the journey would take half a day. The coin had a crack when the keeper bit it. She had heard the faint sound then and called it a flaw in the gold.

The dragon lowered her shoulder to the cobbles. Sella stared at the ridge of scales along it.

"I don't have a saddle."

"I don't have half a day."

Sella climbed. Hollis caught her boot.

"Wait," he said. "There are rules for this."

"There weren't rules for any of it," Sella said.

The dragon rose. Wind snatched Hollis's ledger open and carried three loose sheets over the square. Sella held on with both hands as the town shrank beneath her.

At the north bridge, the keeper was standing outside his booth with a tin box in his hands. He had pried off the lid. A thin gold head peered over the rim, blinking at the sky.

"Thought it was counterfeit," he said when Sella and the dragon landed. "Then it started calling for its mother."

He held the box out. Sella took it and offered it to the dragon. The little one climbed from the box to the enormous waiting claw.

The keeper looked from the mother to Sella. "Do I still get the toll?"

Sella searched her pockets. She found two copper pennies, a button, and the dry heel of the baker's loaf. She gave him the pennies. He considered them, then waved her through the gate.

When Sella returned to the guild, Hollis had nailed a fresh sheet above the door. It read:

**SAFETY REGULATION 32: BEFORE DIVIDING RECOVERED TREASURE, LISTEN TO IT.**

He was standing beneath the notice, writing something else in the margin.

"What now?" Sella asked.

"Regulation thirty-three. No riding dragons without a saddle."

Sella looked up. The mother dragon was already climbing toward the mountains, twenty-one small shapes tucked beneath her wings.

"That one," Sella said, "is for my safety."

Hollis followed her gaze. He crossed out the sentence and began again.
