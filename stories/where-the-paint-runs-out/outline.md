# Where the Paint Runs Out

## Promised experience

The request explicitly gives a free-spirited ranch girl, apparently ordinary life inside artist-created landscapes, three material anomalies, discovery of an unfinished painting abandoned centuries ago, and the question of becoming real without destroying her originating world. Its pleasure, as I read it, is beautiful ranch-country adventure turning into a tangible encounter with the substance of existence: each strange sight should let her discover or attempt something previously impossible. “Free-spirited” suggests appetite and initiative; “without destroying the world” gives her freedom an ethical obstacle rather than making home disposable. Wonder, danger, and an earned engagement with both halves of the question are my inferred direction. The request specifies no companion relationship, antagonist, scale beyond the threatened world, or required answer. The supplied image guides her dark hair, red tied top, weathered denim, horses, sunset mountains, gold, and visibly unfinished edges; it is visual guidance, not a second set of prose instructions.

## Provisional direction

Close third person, approximately 4,000 words. Dessa, an adult ranch daughter, wants to ride beyond the mountains everyone calls the end of the country. Her principal attachment is to her mare, Calico; her father and the ranch make the world she might lose specific. These relationships and the following rules are inventions, not user requirements.

The painting is deteriorating in a long-abandoned painter's room. Its painted matter is shared: pulling a painted person into the exterior also pulls apart the landscape that supplies her substance. However, new matter can enter the work. Dessa discovers that she can use the artists' power herself. Rather than extract her existing paint, she must gradually give her continuously living body new substance from outside. The crossing will be one way: a flesh-and-weight body cannot fold back into the canvas. The intended ending answers yes to physical emergence and preservation, with the cost of a real departure from home rather than home’s annihilation.

## Concrete movement

- Riding an unapproved shortcut, Dessa catches a falling gold flake. It has taken a patch of sunset with it. At the ranch fence, the fringe of her red shirt comes apart into wet-looking strokes. She leaves before anyone can persuade her to wait for safer weather, taking Calico toward a new crack through the mountains.
- The mountain split exposes woven threads under the rock. Beyond it, the apparent distance becomes a narrowing strip of unfinished ground. Calico refuses a blank drop; Dessa dismounts and climbs the exposed weave. From the rim she sees their entire sunset country below her, stretched on a frame in an immense, dusty room. A dated unfinished-work record and the painter's decayed possessions establish that its maker abandoned it centuries ago. Other canvases hold landscapes whose moving people also do not know they are pictures.
- She tries to climb out. Her hand acquires weight, warmth, and an unfamiliar pulse, but the red of the far mesas begins peeling toward her arm. A second pull lifts the ranch sky; she sees Calico running beneath its curling edge. She goes back before the wound takes the ranch. Becoming real is possible, and extraction can ruin everything she loves.
- Retreating through the unfinished ground, she discovers that a loose stroke she drags into a gap becomes a foothold. She tries making ground rather than stealing it. Her first attempts simply move existing color and cost something elsewhere. Then exterior rust, loosened from the frame by rain through the broken roof, lands on her sleeve. Shaped by her intention, this genuinely new color gives cloth substance without draining the mountains. She tests that distinction on a small object before staking herself on it.
- She returns to the rim, gathers exterior earth, rain, and fallen plaster, and makes the first brush from her unraveling cuff. Her working knowledge of balance, knots, and a frightened horse helps her negotiate the edge; she is learning to paint through consequential experiments, not inheriting a convenient finished spell. She gradually paints new substance into her own moving body. The original landscape stops lifting as her growing exterior hand gains the ability to hold the brush. She must finish while decay widens the mountain tear and while she can still retreat; an incomplete crossing would leave her trapped between kinds of matter.
- Before the last step, she sends Calico back with her father, who has followed the mare's tracks to the unfinished ground. He cannot promise that either side will last forever. He can ask whether she still wants to go. She does. The final stroke makes her heavy enough to stand outside; the world behind her remains alive. She cannot ride back into it. In the painter's ruined doorway she meets wind with no visible brushwork and looks toward an actual horizon. Behind her, Calico and her father are already moving home through the intact mountains. Her freedom is neither a rescue by the original maker nor a declaration that painted lives never mattered.
