# Where the Paint Runs Out

## Promised experience

The request explicitly gives a free-spirited ranch girl, apparently ordinary life inside artist-created landscapes, three material anomalies, discovery of an unfinished painting abandoned centuries ago, and the question of becoming real without destroying her originating world. Its pleasure, as I read it, is beautiful ranch-country adventure turning into a tangible encounter with the substance of existence: each strange sight should let her discover or attempt something previously impossible. “Free-spirited” suggests appetite and initiative; “without destroying the world” gives her freedom an ethical obstacle rather than making home disposable. Wonder, danger, and an earned engagement with both halves of the question are my inferred direction. The request specifies no companion relationship, antagonist, scale beyond the threatened world, or required answer. The supplied image guides her dark hair, red tied top, weathered denim, horses, sunset mountains, gold, and visibly unfinished edges; it is visual guidance, not a second set of prose instructions.

## Provisional direction

Close third person, approximately 4,000 words. Dessa, an adult ranch daughter, wants to ride beyond the mountains everyone calls the end of the country. Her principal attachment is to her mare, Calico; her father and the ranch make the world she might lose specific. These relationships and the following rules are inventions, not user requirements.

The painting is deteriorating in a long-abandoned painter's room. Its painted matter is shared: pulling a painted person into the exterior also pulls apart the landscape that supplies her substance. New matter can enter through the exposed, unfinished ground. Dessa discovers through experiments that she can shape it as the artists did. Rather than extract her existing paint, she must gradually give her continuously living body new substance from outside.

Her first extraction attempt also starts an advancing peel. Preserving home requires physically supporting the split and painting over the exposed ground, closing the only passage she has found. There is no universal rule that real bodies cannot return: she chooses which side to inhabit before sealing the route. The intended ending answers yes to emergence and immediate preservation, with a demonstrated loss of access to home rather than its annihilation or a promise that it will last forever.

## Concrete movement

- Dessa and her father race their horses to the far ranch gate, a familiar contest she still wants to win. She wants to know what lies beyond the mountains; he wants her back for the next race. A falling gold flake has taken a patch of sunset with it, and the fringe of her red shirt comes apart against the fence into wet-looking strokes. Instead of waiting for the strange weather to pass, she takes Calico toward a new mountain crack.
- The mountain split exposes woven threads under the rock. Beyond it, the apparent distance becomes a narrowing strip of unfinished ground. Calico refuses a blank drop; Dessa dismounts and climbs the exposed weave. From the rim she sees their entire sunset country below her, stretched on a frame in an immense, dusty room. A dated unfinished-work record and the painter's decayed possessions establish that its maker abandoned it centuries ago. Other canvases hold landscapes whose moving people also do not know they are pictures.
- She tries to climb out. Her hand acquires weight, warmth, and an unfamiliar pulse, but the red of the far mesas begins peeling toward her arm. A second pull lifts the ranch sky; she sees Calico running beneath its curling edge. She goes back before the wound takes the ranch. The stolen color settles back, but the split she enlarged continues peeling under the old canvas's tension. Retreat has stopped extraction, not the danger she has set moving.
- In the unfinished ground, a loose stroke she drags into a gap becomes a foothold, leaving another place bare. Then rain stains the raw margin with rust from the exterior frame without costing any mesa its red. She recognizes a possible distinction and deliberately gathers more. She works this new color into the end of her lasso. The same knot stays tied throughout, but the treated end can now lift a real fallen nail beyond the rim without fading the landscape; the untreated end starts lifting color from distant rock when she makes the same attempt, and she eases off. Next she transforms a moving fingertip while it remains attached to her living hand. She feels the nail's cold, chooses to let it go, and draws her finger back without any sky following. There is no second Dessa, shed body, or transferred mind.
- With a brush made from her unraveling cuff, she gathers exterior earth, plaster, rain, and loose fibers. She uses her gradually strengthening exterior hand to brace the split with those fibers, then paints new supporting ground over it. Her horse-handling and rope skills help her work at the edge. She spends material and time on the endangered world before completing herself. The braced ground holds; lifted sky lies flat; Calico can stand on the restored approach. But the remaining opening concentrates the canvas's tension: when Dessa lets its lips gape, the new ridge begins lifting at that edge. She pinches the lips together and the ridge settles; releasing them starts the lift again. She must close this last gap to keep the repair from peeling away. That will also remove her only demonstrated route between worlds.
- Her father arrives along her tracks. Dessa can still withdraw into the country with him, retain their races and Calico's warm weight against her, and close the opening from within. Instead she takes a last unmediated touch of them and completes her body outside. She closes the remaining gap from that side. No mountain color follows her. Her father's hand against hers becomes a painted hand against a solid surface; Calico's mane can no longer brush her palm. Father and mare go home over the restored ground, the stable repair still visible as a new pale ridge. The country is inhabited and moving, not frozen by her finishing a portion of it. Dessa stands heavy and bodily in the ruined doorway and meets wind with no visible brushwork. She gets an actual horizon and loses the next race with her father; the ending should allow both feelings to remain.
