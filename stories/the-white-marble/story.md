# The White Marble

The king was going to the palace at supper. I was given a rag to wash him with and told to leave the rest of him alone. There was chalk on his nose and a great shelf of marble on the back of his neck. I put one finger behind my own ear and felt how far down the bone came. You could hardly turn your head on a neck like the one my master had given the king. He turned his own to see where a noise came from, like anybody else.

My mother used to leave her dinner until the light went, because she could see to eat in the dark. I was living at home then. Some days I minded waiting for her and would give her the piece of bread with the heel on purpose, and some days I watched her work and wanted the sun to stay where it was. She did a great deal of carving after hours for people who wanted a likeness and could do without a guild seal. She had a way of working round an ear from the front, so she could see the instant it stopped looking stuck on. At the Carving House she polished the work. The old master used to send her home with the cloths to wash, which was counted as a kindness because she was allowed to keep the soap.

I hadn't meant to take the whole shelf off. Once I got round the king's ear I wanted to see his head meet his shoulders. I put a folded rag over the chisel to quiet it, which also made it difficult to see what I was doing, and kept running round the bed to see how he looked from the front. By the time his head sat right, I was hungry enough to taste the dust. The shim under one leg of the bed had worked loose and I could feel the whole thing rock when I leant on it.

The master had gone to meet the men from the palace below the yard. With a cart to bring up the hill, they'd be coming at a walk. I'd grown to rely on the sliver of light under the big doors to show me when anybody was on the step. So far there hadn't been so much as a shadow of a boot. From the step you could see all the way through the shop to the old wagon threshold at the far end. I always started sweeping back there because if you went the other way the broom packed the dust into two deep wheel ruts that went straight into a wall. You could get out beside them by the hoist door. You could hardly hear a man out in the yard till he put his foot on the first tread, and then you could count him all the way up.

I pushed the king back level with my hip and tried the heel of a sandal under the bed. That was the better sandal. When the leather began to give I pulled it out again. There was a spare oak wedge just across the chalk. The master drew that line every morning along the whole length of the white marble. The slab ran from the rough block beside the king's bed nearly to the back of the shop, where it had been carved at the far end. We could walk its length without crossing the line. He would click his teeth when any of us put so much as the end of a broom over it. When the Queen had brought her guests through to see the founding relief at the far end of the slab, he made her stand behind the chalk, too. For three hundred years the greatest carvers of Vennor had been kept from setting a finger on the rough white block at my end. My mother had told me to mind my breathing when I went past it. The old gods had put something inside they were afraid to have loose, and for her the matter ended there.

I took off the other sandal and set them together before I went for the wedge. I was going to come out of this with at least one pair of something I owned.

It took me three tries to slide the wedge under the king's bed. When I swung, my hammer glanced off the wedge into the white marble beside me. I felt the blow all the way to my elbow. I held the hammer out as far from me as my arm would go, as if the mark would be smaller if I did. With my other hand I felt for a chip. My red sleeve slipped away from my wrist. The stone was cold against the skin there. Then there were three faint knocks from inside the marble. I'd felt something as faint once when an egg began to hatch in my hand. I found I hadn't taken a breath since the blow. I was trying to let it go quietly when the woman spoke.

“Give it one here,” she whispered. “Where you've got your hand.”

I set the hammer down. I could see as far as the dark hoist door. There was nobody between it and me. The voice had come from level with my hand and when I bent to hear it again my braid fell across the chalk. It was yellow when I set out for the shop in the morning. By supper I usually had to beat the dust out of it against a step.

“Is there a hollow behind this?” I asked.

“There’s me. The name is Sevra. I’d come and tell you what you’re doing wrong, only I can’t get my fingers through. There’s a bit of stone on my wrist that I can’t get past. Put the hammer back there and you’ll save me trying another three hundred years.”

I took the point chisel out of my black apron and set it where my hammer had landed. I could face the master with one chip to my name. I did not much like the thought of having to tell him I'd been asked to make two. I took the point away.

“Wait,” she said. “Go and look at the carving at the other end. I did it. I could put one mark in this marble and see a change by the time I went out into the street. It’s a trick your masters would give two good fingers to learn. Finish carving me out and I’ll teach it to you. You can make a thing that happened go a different way.”

I knew most of the carving without having to trespass to see it. At the bottom were the six signs the gods had given the families who would have the keeping of the royal guild. A feather, a white hare, a dog with its head turned and the broken rim of a pot for our master’s family. Over the years there had been a number of small commissions I’d finished for him. I'd put the little rim on them myself. Above the six signs was the surrender. The whole of one god's hand, from wrist to fingernails, lay over a gate. In school they had told us the ward beyond had given up its pride to become part of Vennor. When I was very small I thought the god must be pressing down hard in case the people changed their minds.

“What if I cut a seventh sign with the others?” I asked, back at the unworked end of the slab. “My mother draws a little shoe on the things she makes. Could I put her shoe in with the feather and have men come down here to ask for her work, the way they ask for the master’s?”

“Is she good at it?”

“There’s a boy across the yard with a shaking hand who will put his sign on a princess before they'll let my mother cut a button on the princess's dress. Go and ask anyone which of them can carve the better likeness.”

“I only meant, can she put a likeness of you in a stone and make you want to look at it? I'd have asked to see her work, when there was still a shop I could ask to see it in. You saw where the six families got their place. I can teach you to put a shoe there just as easily. Do you think you could give me that first blow now? You’ll have a much better chance of getting what you want if I have a finger to show you with.”

“I’ll do your wrist if I can make the first cut myself after you teach me,” I said. “Then I'll finish.”

I put the point where the hammer had landed. I could hear the woman breathing for the first few knocks, because she was asking me to wait when she breathed in and hit when she breathed out. The stone gave way in flakes thin enough to see light through. A knuckle came to the surface, and then I had to follow the fingers back to the wrist and chip out the places between them so the hand could move. She made a little fist on the marble. Sevra had gone quiet. I put a finger under the wrist. I had expected the dead weight of a cast. Her pulse beat so fast against the pad of my finger that I kept my hand there counting till it slowed.

I was still standing with my weight against the block. Sevra picked grit off my sleeve with the hand I’d freed. I thought she wanted to hold my hand when she caught a fold of the red cloth. Then she drew the sleeve away from the edge of a flake and I saw how close I’d come to tearing it.

“Set the chisel up here where I can reach it,” she said. “I want you to watch.”

I went back down the length of the slab and looked at the god’s hand again. The sign of the pot rim came up to my shoulder. I could have cut our shoe with my arm straight out, without having to stoop like a polisher. When I went back to Sevra I set the chisel down on the block and kept the hammer. She put a thumb on the iron and rolled it back toward me. I put my thumb where hers had been. She laid her hand across my fingers. “Gather the heat from your fingertips now. Let the iron have it all the way to the point. I'll keep my hand here till you get the feel of it.” I thought I was only feeling the heat of her hand. The iron grew hotter than either of us. There was a swelling hard to locate in my hand, as if I had a second pulse farther down my thumb. She waited until I could do it without her.

I wanted to try the little shoe. Sevra asked me to start with the nail at the end of the god's smallest finger. “Take the corner off,” she said. “You’ll be able to tell what you did, all the way across the room.”

I walked the length of the slab and made the cut. At the blind threshold a thin line of daylight showed from top to bottom. I'd scraped the ends of enough new boards against that wall to know how solid it was. Now there was evening air coming out of it and it smelled like the first onions in the pan at home. On the other side a shopkeeper was pulling to a pair of shutters. I heard the first one slam and the second bang twice until somebody leaned hard on it.

I stood in one of the wheel ruts and put an eye to the crack. I'd thought the gods had closed the gate in the carving because nobody needed it anymore. The shutter I could hear was hinged to a real house. Sevra had wrapped her fingers round the top of the block. I could hear the nails catch as she tried to pull herself nearer the sound.

“I’m going to make it a little wider,” I said. Then I put the hammer down. “Who would I be opening the gate for?”

“I’ve given you the teaching. Come round here with the light, where you can see my shoulder. Something’s coming away under it as I push. I’d like to see the other side of the wall too, but I’d like to do it on my feet.”

While I'd been at the relief Sevra had worked her elbow out. She felt for my sleeve again while I worked down by her collar. I could see the fold ready to separate the instant she leaned. She gave a low grunt as she moved, and I put my hand in under her arm to carry some of the weight. A gray piece of the stone settled on the floor near my feet.

“You said you were going to finish this,” she said. “If you’d go round to the foot, there's one place I can almost get through if I push. And don’t let me fall on you.”

“You're getting out. I need to know who’s on the other side of that wall.”

I could hear her breathe through her nose. I had time to get a flake from under her elbow before she said, “My daughter will be at the shop where you just heard the shutters. I was to walk her home for supper. In the morning she was outside in her shirtsleeves when I told her I was to work for the gods that day. I made her go in for her green coat. I wanted to see what it looked like on her with the cuffs let out. I thought we’d get one more spring out of it and after that she'd have to pay for her own.”

She had her cheek out of the marble now, and one eye, fixed on the fingers she had been using to pick at my clothes. It wasn't a young face. I had been expecting somebody who had sat at a supper table with the gods to have a pretty way of managing even a bad expression, and she did, for half a breath. Then the corner of her mouth pulled down and she had to start over.

“I carved them the sign of every master they put in the guild. They used to seat them one after another on a stool to watch their signs go in. The gods would give them the news they were to be paid for what everyone else had been doing. Then they asked me to put a hand over the south gate because the ward was holding out. I've got a daughter over there, I told them. I remember I said it as if I'd discovered something wrong with the commission. One of them held the lamp so close that I felt the heat of it in the fingers I'd raised. I remember I could have put out his light with my palm. Well, I thought I could carve a hand holding the gate closed, and walk up there at supper and ask the god to open it for me. So I put the fingers in. I could still see the watchman on the other side, holding his own lamp. I was cutting the nail on the smallest finger when the light went out. There wasn't even a gate where he'd been standing. I tried to knock the hand off. They drove me back along the slab and pushed until the stone came up over my face. I could still hear the woman from the shop calling out that she was closing up. I knew my daughter would be outside getting tired of waiting for me.”

My knees had got damp where I knelt in the corner of the shop to free her feet. She could move one leg now, and she put a toe down over mine. In school they'd said a whole ward had gone down on its knees of its own accord. I wanted to ask Sevra how much they’d paid her for that, and for the pot rim I'd put under forty other people’s faces. She put enough weight into my hand for me to have to straighten my wrist behind her leg. I kept hold until I thought I could speak about something besides the money.

“If I take the rest of that hand off,” I told her, “I’ll open the front doors for the people who come through and tell them who cut the gate open. Will you be there to tell them who shut it?”

“Ketta, Asta is going to see a street on the other side of this shop that was never there before. I can think of a dozen ways out of here, and I don't know one place I could tell her to go for help if she gets lost. Would it do her any good at all to find out her own mother took her out of the world?”

I had not told her my name. The master would shout it all the way from the front of the shop when there were ashes to sweep. I wondered what other voices she'd heard, and how many people had stood as close to the chalk as I had with one foot about to go over. I looked back at the block where she’d scratched to get nearer the sound of the shutters. There were tiny white half-moons on the surface by her hand.

“If my mother missed supper I’d go to the shop after her,” I said. “I'd be furious by the time I got there. I’d still go.”

It was nearly dark at our end of the slab. One rim of stone still held her behind her left shoulder. I laid the chisel in close to it, almost flat against her back. She put her cheek down on my shoulder and stayed there while I struck. I felt the rim give against my wrist. She came forward with such weight that her feet pushed mine out of their places. For those first two steps I had to hold her up. There was a funny smell, about like a nail drawn from a board that’s been wet a long time. She was wearing a dark blue skirt cut differently from my mother's, with pleats I could see for the first time as she came forward into them.

Somebody tried the front doors. I knew it was the master before he spoke. He always drew the one door toward him first, because he was sure he'd find the bolt left across the other.

“Ketta, draw the bolt. I've got His Majesty’s men with me. Come and do it now or I’ll send one round.”

Sevra straightened up. She drew in a breath that filled her all the way down and looked at me properly, for the first time since I’d put my hand on the block. “I'd need paint to get those eyes right,” she said. “What a nuisance for your mother when she got round to doing yours.” I could often tell the moment someone noticed they were violet. I hadn’t expected it to matter to me what this woman would notice first.

“Come out by the stair with me,” Sevra said, taking my hand. A shoe struck the lowest tread hard enough to shake the stair. She let go of me. I snatched the hammer and ran down the length of the slab to the relief.

I set the point in the clean space beside the feather. The first part of our shoe was a curve that doubled back at the toe. I had copied it in chalk on a slate once, when I was small enough to think that her sign could go anywhere there was room for it. She'd kept that slate. I wanted to be there when someone from the palace brought her a block of marble and asked her where she would like it set up.

The man on the hoist stair was taking the treads two at a time. Out of the corner of my eye I could see the strip of light at the threshold. A shadow moved across it, and then the light came back. I could get the shoe in before he saw me. I wanted to see whether it had been a green sleeve in the light. I took my point off the white stone and the space beside the feather was still blank. I found the flat chisel in my black apron. I went to the hand.

There was a place underneath the third finger where Sevra’s point had hit too hard all those years before. I laid the edge of the flat chisel into the mark, where the whole weight of the hand hung out from the slab. The man from the palace had one boot over the sill of the hoist door. He opened his mouth to shout at me. I drew the heat into the iron as Sevra had taught me and struck. The chisel slid in and the god's hand came away from the carved gate in one piece. It was too big to catch. I let it fall and took my foot out of the way just before it hit the floor. The crack of it ran up through my legs.

I saw the man turn from me to the old threshold. The wind hit us hard enough to whip my braid across my face. I put a foot out to steady myself and felt the place where the old wheel ruts met the flagstones of the street. The wall above them stood wide open. It was getting dark in the street beyond and one house already had lamplight behind its shutters. A man with his supper on a plate in front of him got up from a bench and came to look at us with the napkin still in his hand.

I put my other foot over the threshold, where I had always stopped because of the wall. Away to either side, down a hillside I knew as a bare slope, I saw houses in rows running out almost as far as the palace. The lamps were coming up house by house, first in the street nearest me, then down where the roofs changed and all the chimneys were tall enough for a smith’s fire. On our side the palace lights were already up. The two cities were close enough together for the people at one end to see the people at the other holding lanterns to get a better look.

“Is this the south gate?” the man in the street asked. He had to ask me twice.

“It is now,” I told him. “The road out goes through the Carving House. Will you help me move the king?”

I gave the king's bed a shove from the end I had wedged up, and between us the man and I cleared a way wide enough to walk side by side. I could feel my own back again after we moved it. I had some idea how long I'd been leaning over the block only once it began to hurt.

While the man from the palace stood at the hoist door, I ran to unbolt the front. There was my master in the yard with his hand out for the door. From where we stood we could both see Sevra in the old gateway. She’d gone out far enough to look down the hill. When the master saw her he took his hand off the door and looked at the block. Even at that distance there was no missing the shape she had left in it.

“That's her, then,” he said. I wondered how many times he'd drawn that chalk line knowing whose wrist would come out if he put a hammer over it.

“I cut the gate open,” I said. I had to stand quite close to him so the people coming from the far end knew they could go around me. He looked down at my right hand. I still had the hammer. I laid it on the king’s bed.

The man from the palace came toward us. My master stepped between us and put his hand on the king's bed. “I gave her a rag and told her to clean him,” he said. The palace man went round the bed to the side I was on.

“I saw you. What's your name?”

“Ketta.” He asked for the hammer. I took it off the bed by the head and held it there between us until he put his hand out again. I gave it to him handle first. None of the men who took the work away had ever asked me my name before.

“You’ll come to the palace with me, Ketta,” he said. “They’ll need you to tell them how you did that.”

My master looked at me as if he'd found me with the king’s ear broken off in my hand. There was a folding of the skin around his eyes that I was sure he would take away with him and wear in front of my mother. I remember wanting him to ask me if I’d done the king’s neck too.

I went back to the threshold. A girl in a green coat was coming up to the wheel ruts. White hair had come free of the marble at Sevra’s temples. The girl looked right past it into the shop. She could have walked a long way past her mother without knowing her.

“Asta,” Sevra said. The girl started.

“I went back for you,” the girl said. “The watchman said he'd heard you working and then he couldn't see the light anymore. Is that our hill? Where did they get a house that size?”

“The great house is the palace, I think. They've had a long time to build it since I left you with the shopkeeper this morning.” Sevra stepped down into the old ruts, close enough to her daughter to talk quietly. “The gods wanted Low Hill shut in, sweetheart. I was the one who shut the gate. I thought you'd have to wait till I got off work for it to open. When the watchman’s lamp went away I couldn't get back to you. I'm sorry. I'd like to go home with you, if the house is still where I left it.”

The girl tried to look past her at the great white palace. She began to pick at the cuff of one of her sleeves. Somebody in the yard called for another lantern and people answered him from both sides of the shop. Sevra held out her hand, the one I had struck free first. There was still white dust in the folds of her palm. Her daughter set the green cuff free of its last loose thread and took hold of her.
