# Headlights Off

People say they didn’t know where to look when they heard the announcement. Up to that point they’d had excellent instructions. My sister Nelle had put HEADLIGHTS OFF in big black letters across the top of the program. Ross was to wait at the foot of the screen in a jacket I’d more or less picked out. I was to come down the middle with Nelle. Afterward, when Ross and I drove out, everybody could blow their horn once, and if certain people thought that gave them the right to lean their whole weight on it till we made the county road, I was willing to find out who they were.

For months I’d been telling anyone I invited to bring the whole carload. After a while I started to think I knew what every car would have in it. A lady I remembered from the ticket window had brought the little dog she used to say was at home. In a gold Lincoln under the booth a boy was getting his hair combed and taking a handful of popcorn every time the woman doing his hair had to reach around for his other ear. I’d spent summer evenings asking people like these to shut their doors or turn something down. I couldn’t get over being allowed to see them all getting ready for me.

I’d found Nelle a job that excluded crying until after I came down the aisle. She was to fetch me from the booth when Ross and the officiant were ready. After the vows she’d play the film I’d made out of bits of the last four years, which we’d already had one private showing of in the booth. I was counting on the part at the end to make her cry again.

I went out the back to put the flowers people had brought for me in Ross’s car. We’d tucked it behind the building to keep a clear path for the two of us to drive out. It was the end of September and the car was cold already. I got it open with his spare key and was leaning in to put the flowers on the passenger seat when the wrapping caught on his thermos. It stood by the handbrake on a folded piece of paper towel. Ross tore his towels in two. Half was plenty for his sandwich and the other half went under the thermos because the seal in the lid had gone. Every Sunday at my house he stood it up that way while I was still putting supper in.

We’d agreed he would stay the night and leave early. I was going to set the coffee to come on for him before dawn. Yesterday that had sounded like a thing I could do. I stood there with the door open long enough for the dome light to go off, and when I drew my hands out of the car I was surprised to find I’d let go of the flowers.

In the booth it smelled of the oil that got hot in the bottom of the popcorn kettle. I left Ross’s key on the desk beside the microphone and put both hands on the sill. I could see the screen, and under it two men testing whether the microphone on the platform could be made higher. Ross leaned in to save them the work. I could tell from where I stood that he was trying to see how low he could get without bending his knees.

Nelle came in with a slip from the gate. I held out a hand like it was time for her to take me down the aisle, and when she came close enough to take it I couldn’t get enough air to start what I wanted to say.

“I-I-I can't do this! I can't marry him!”

She looked down at the slip. “Um...There is over seven hundred guests outside.”

“Tell them to go home!”

There was a fair amount of silence for a woman who usually had her second idea ready before you’d heard the first. She undid a button of her good coat and got out the flask she carried to other people’s weddings. “Let me see. I could tell them to go home, or, I could give you some hard liquor and a pep talk?”

She turned the cap a little. “The pep talk first,” I said. “Then we’ll know which of us needs the drink.”

“Good. Come here. See the blue Buick in the third row? They’re listening to the ball game in that one. I was down there when I heard them cheer and I thought I’d missed the whole thing. There’s a man in a truck even farther back who can’t find the FM setting. He’s had three people over to have a look and all three of them leaned on the button that does the seat.” She had her finger to the glass now. I could just see the truck’s little yellow parking lights. “Put your weight on my arm on the gravel. When you get up on the boards you only have to find Ross. Start talking to Ross. If the man in the truck can hear you by then I’ll be surprised.”

She did get a laugh. I moved away from the window with her and gathered up enough of my dress to find my shoes. The afternoon before, when we’d walked it without the chairs and the flowers, Ross had been down at the front in his work shirt with the sleeves turned back. He’d practised waiting until I got there. For the last ten steps he’d offered me the stupidest smile he knew how to make, with his lower lip hanging so the ends of all his bottom teeth showed. I’d walked much faster than I meant to. I tried now to imagine him smiling like that while he waited for me to say the first thing I’d written on a card. I let the dress go.

“I could do it for the people in the truck,” I said. “I could stand there and say it straight through for them. When the florist wanted an address for the bill I gave her mine and said it would do for both of us. That’s what people hear when you say you’re going to marry somebody. Ross would be right there hearing me tell him it’s all right to leave in the morning. And next Sunday I’ll be home looking to see if he’s put his shoes by the back door so I know how long he can stay for supper.”

“Then ask him to stay till Monday night. There’s your first sentence. He can say no if he has to.”

“I want him there on a Monday nobody’s heard of.” The song we’d picked to run before the ceremony was coming to an end. I wanted badly to go down while it was still playing and let Nelle stand me where we’d practised. “Would you bring him up here? Before I walk down there and sound like I mean it.”

Nelle put the flask down and sat where she could start the music for the walk. “I want to walk out there with you,” she said. “I’ve been telling people all day when to get their cameras out. They’ll see me if I go out without you.” She had her finger on the button. A horn went off and a woman near the platform stood up with her phone held out in both hands.

She took her hand away. I heard the first note of the film I’d cut for after the vows. Up on the screen there was a white three the size of somebody’s house. People closed their car doors and got their faces forward. Nelle left the mic in its cradle and went down through the rows for Ross.

By the time they got back it was dark enough to see the colors on the screen. Ross and I were swimming off a dock with our two heads low in the brown water. Nelle held the door for him and went back out among the cars. He stood with his hands out from the sides of his coat in the way men do when somebody’s pinned a flower on them and they’re afraid of knocking it off.

“She told me you wanted me up here,” he said. “Do you want to wait a few minutes? I can tell the man.”

I hooked my heel under the spare chair and drew it out. I sat down because I wanted him to sit down with me. “Are you still leaving at five tomorrow?”

“I don’t have to leave that early. I can get in after lunch if I ask Davies to open the shop.”

I could see him seeing his way to it, when he’d call Davies and where he’d have to tell him to look for the shop keys. “I’d like to have you for breakfast. I’d like Tuesday when you get back from the shop. Where are we going to be then?”

He sat down. There was a heater under the desk that somebody had unplugged to make room for the coffee urn. His hands were close enough to mine that I could see the little pink crescent where he’d shaved the hard skin off his thumb. “We said we’d keep both our places when I took the job. We’d been out to see an apartment for the two of us that morning. The one with the stove. I asked you in the car if I should hold out for work nearer you. You folded up the floor plan and said take the job, for heaven’s sake. You’d drive in the summer and I could do it when the roads got bad.” He’d been looking down at his hands. He looked right at me when he said, “And I’ve been doing it. You’ve hardly seen a bad road.”

“I came up once when it was so cold I broke the handle off my scraper,” I said. “You’d left the porch light on and gone to bed at eight. I had to throw something at the window to get in.”

“I thought that was ice coming off the roof at first.” He smiled briefly, remembering what he’d gone out to the door in. “When they shut the county road last winter, I could have been home by five. I got over on Twelve and came down behind the dairy in somebody else’s wheel tracks. I parked out of the wind behind your house and you came out in your socks because you thought it was a snowplow coming up the alley. I was so glad to get my feet in your back door, I could hardly get one boot off to make room for the other. I wanted to be in your house then, Maris. I still do. I thought if we got married I could stop having to arrange with you to be there. Get a key and take it away with me.”

The screen filled up with Ross’s palm where he’d put his hand out to steady the camera. Nelle had asked me if I wanted that bit cut and I’d said leave it. In another minute or so the film would run to the end. I was trying to remember if, on the night he’d come through the snow, I had offered him a key of his own. I’d put his socks on the heater and brought the butter out so it would soften. What I remembered of the morning was waiting for him to put his arm back under the covers before I went to make the coffee. I would have lain there half the day if I could have gone out for milk and been sure he would still be there when I came back.

“You know at my house on Sunday, when you say should you help with supper, you’re already running that paper towel under the thermos and looking at the clock over the stove. I cut the potatoes smaller if you already have your bag by the door. I watch you take the bag out to the car before we eat so we can sit down as if you’re staying. Sometimes when you ask me something over supper I save half the answer to tell you next Friday. I wanted to be married to you so I could use up a whole answer.”

At the end of the film there was a shot Nelle had taken of us in the front seat of his car. She’d called to Ross to drive off slower and he’d done it so slow you could see his hand turn and come to rest on the top of mine. The screen went white again. Somebody yelled congratulations from the lot. Other voices came on top of it and one or two of the car doors opened. Ross looked at the door.

Nelle came in and shut the door behind her. “They’re getting out and coming up here. I’ve told one little girl where the toilets are and her grandmother where a bride goes when she needs five minutes. I can play the music again or tell them something.”

“Let me go stand where they can see me,” Ross said. He put one hand on the back of his chair. “You can have another few minutes in here.”

For a few seconds I thought I would let him do it. He even straightened the chair so I could get to the door behind him when I was ready. I thought about him on that platform, holding his hands away from his flower, trying to look like a man who’d wait exactly as long as I needed him to. If I went down after him now he’d give me his hand in front of everybody and I could get home without having to look at him once.

“Can we have a place with both our things in it before next September? Could we say where it is and how we’re going to get there?” Out in the lot a hand slapped the side of a car, twice. I kept expecting Ross to answer before the second slap. I’d let myself hurry all through that question and now I had to sit in the time it took to answer it.

Ross pulled his chair back out. “I can be at your place next Friday,” he said. “I can keep coming.” He sat back down. “If you decided you wanted to live where I do, I’d go see every place with you. There’s a house near the shop that’s got a door into the front room from the street. If you put your table in there, people could come to get their things fitted without having to go through the kitchen. I could take you to look on Tuesday. Do you want to do that?”

I wanted to see the room. At the place with the stove we’d stood in the kitchen waiting for the landlady to stop talking so we could put our hands the width of the table apart and see if there was room against the wall. Now he’d found me a door of my own. I wanted to choose a house and see if he really did fold his work shirts with the cuffs tucked under, even on a Wednesday when he’d have to wear one again in the morning.

“I’d like to see it,” I said. “Would you come look down here with me too?”

“Where would I get work here?” He put both hands on his knees. “I had three mornings at the garage after I’d asked them for a year. I’d have stayed if they could have given me the rest of the week.”

I knew what it had taken him to get those mornings. I’d packed him a lunch the first day just in case they let him stay longer. I was picking through those same employers in my head when I realized I had the same question for him about his town. I could buy a good length of blue cloth right down the street from my shop and know almost exactly who would come in and say she wanted a skirt made of it. I knew how to begin again in a different room here. Finding out if I could do it in his town was going to take longer than the time until Tuesday.

“I’d have to find out if I could earn a living there,” I said. “And if I couldn’t move, I can’t promise you I’ll be happy sitting down to one nice supper when your boots are already by the door.”

“I didn’t mean you had to decide about the house tonight.”

“I was going to stand out there and tell you Fridays would be enough.” When I said it I could feel how close I still was to doing exactly that. I had my hands flat on the desk. I moved the left one closer to his and then I put it back.

“We can’t get married tonight,” I said. I looked up at Nelle. “There isn’t going to be a wedding.”

Ross got up. He stood by me long enough that I thought he was going to reach down for my hand, and he did reach, to unhook the sleeve of my dress where I’d caught it on the edge of the desk. “Give me time to get to the car before you tell everybody.”

“I’ll wait,” I said. Nelle opened the door to let him by. I could hear each step as he went down to the back of the booth.

Nelle shut the door again. Her eyes were wet and she was lining up the papers on the desk with two fingers. “I could go down to the mic on the platform and say it. People know me here.”

I did wonder which hurt worse, to have Ross hear her say it or to have him hear me. I waited until I heard his car door shut, and then I looked out the back. He was behind the wheel with the dome light out again. I took our mic out of its clip. “Would you stand over there and look at me while I do it?”

Nelle stepped sideways. I wanted to ask her to go a little farther, so I could try the words on her with the microphone off. She planted both her feet and held out her arm the way we’d practised, ready to walk down the aisle between the desk and the wall. I could say it to her. I leaned toward the mic and pushed the switch up.

“This is Maris in the booth. There isn’t going to be a wedding tonight.” I’d heard Nelle turn people away from the Oriel when the picture wouldn’t start. She’d tell them she was sorry and say where to go with their ticket money. I hadn’t got anything of that kind to say. “I’m sorry I kept you waiting. You can go home.” Down in the gold Lincoln a woman put her hand all the way up to the radio and then left it there, as if she could get me to say it differently by making the words louder.

I switched the mic off. There was a single sharp, beautiful toot, the exact sort of horn we’d been asking for. It came from somewhere near the middle. Before you could guess what it meant, three or four more went. I pushed up the front window. Underneath it a man asked his wife whether I’d meant there wouldn’t be a wedding or the wedding was over. She told him to fetch two of the chairs either way, since they were out here now. The lot lit up piece by piece as people started their cars. One of the wagons pulled across the lane with the hatch up and a woman ran to stuff two chairs into the back. She had time to get one all the way in before anybody behind her blew a horn.

I saw the glow on Ross’s windshield as he nosed out from behind the booth. The line for the county road had already reached the corner by the Lincoln. I used to close up to that noise, tires breaking the crust of the gravel after the last song stopped. There were always a few cars you could hear after you turned the lights out downstairs.

“Could you come down with me?” I asked Nelle. “Just to where his car is.” I picked up Ross’s key and opened the door. It was colder than I’d thought it could get so quickly. The smell of popcorn was out in the lot, too, mixed in with the exhaust. Behind me Nelle put her folded arm out. This time I took it. The gravel slid under my shoes where a car had turned its front tires in it.

We got as far as the Lincoln and the woman with the comb got out. “Maris, we were just saying it was a lovely evening for you.” She looked over her shoulder at the boy and put the comb in her pocket. “I had a time getting him into that shirt. I wanted him to see you get married.” She stood by the open door with her boy leaning over to see between us. His hair had come up again on one side. I thanked her for coming, out of all the things I could have said, and she touched the part of my sleeve that had been caught on the desk. She had to take her hand away to close the door.

Ross rolled his window down when I came around the back of his car. I felt Nelle let go of my arm. The light on the dashboard was green, from the numbers of the station he still had on. I held his key out to him. He put his hand past it and around my wrist to warm it. I gave him a minute. I could see the flowers beside him. For some reason I had counted on showing him which flower to put his nose in to get a little smell of it, and I was sorry now he might never find it. “Goodbye, Ross,” I said, with his cuff against my fingers. “I wanted to come say it to you.”

“I’m glad you did.” He took the key. I could hear the engine running under us. “I’ll call you when I get home.”

The car in front of him moved. Nelle and I drew back to let him go and the car after his let its headlights right into our faces. We waited on the bank while the line inched forward, watching the white paper around my flowers catch the lights from one car after another.
