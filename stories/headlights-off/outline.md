# Headlights Off

## Promised experience

The four lines put someone who suddenly can't go through with a marriage within earshot of more than seven hundred people who expect it to happen. “Tell them to go home!” reaches for an instant solution at an absurd scale. The reply offers hard liquor and a pep talk with the air of someone finding a more practical alternative. I read room for warmth as well as bite in that exchange. The story owes the panicked speaker a real choice, the person making the offer a chance to show what they mean by help, and the crowd an effect on what happens. The relationship between the speakers, the reason for the refusal, and whether the wedding goes ahead are open.

## Takes considered

- **A wedding at the Oriel Drive-In.** In real time a bride with a flair for the public occasion discovers she can't make the private promise. She has to tell the groom in the projection booth and decide what to say over the radio that was going to carry their vows. Chosen because the crowd can press on the conversation while it unfolds and answer back when they hear her.
- **The couple on the poster.** At a civic mass wedding, the bride is one of dozens of people about to be married and the face of the publicity. The central scene is a fight with her fellow brides about whether she has the right to stop the proceedings or even leave. The ensemble would give the crowd individual demands and would leave less room for the bride and her would-be husband.
- **The groom comes around.** Start with a man hearing his intended refuse him through the door of a banquet hall's coatroom, then give him time alone with the seven hundred guests while she considers the offer. The central encounter would be his choice to go back inside to listen. His injured pride would hold attention that belongs to the woman making the choice in the first take.

## Provisional direction

Maris chose the drive-in where she and her sister worked the summer she met Ross. She and Ross have kept their homes and jobs two hours apart for four years. They looked for a place together in earnest until he got a permanent job where he lives. She urged him to take it and said they could keep driving for a while, without the nerve to ask how long either of them meant. The big wedding is partly Maris's pleasure in making their romance visible and partly an occasion she's been counting toward as if it were the end of that arrangement. While putting her flowers in Ross's car for their planned drive out together, she finds his thermos and work boots already laid out for the morning drive. She has seen him pack exactly this way at her house on Sundays, often while she was still fixing their supper. Today she'd put the date in a different category, though he hasn't changed the plan.

Her sister Nelle comes to the booth to fetch her. The four given lines lead to Nelle attempting a pep talk, still thinking she can get Maris out the door if she can get her laughing. Maris wants Nelle to stop the ceremony and find Ross. In private Ross tries to offer his faithful version of those weekends and what he thought marriage would give them. They each finally say what they would need from a marriage and hear what the other can offer. Maris calls it off for today. She takes the mic herself and tells the guests they can leave. The guests make far more noise going home than they'd been allowed to make at the wedding.

## Craft plan

**Teller.** Try close third person in the half hour of Maris's panic, with attentiveness to what she'd looked forward to seeing from the booth and what her body is doing against her will. Try first person addressed afterward to a willing listener, with Maris enjoying the preposterous occasion she made and less eager to admit what she was expecting Ross to change. In either voice her pleasure in a full house should have time on the page.

**Cast.** Maris is hospitable and routes difficult questions through offers. Under pressure she's emphatic, then tells the story behind an objection in a rush that can accumulate several Saturdays. She wants Nelle to believe her once without giving her advice, and Ross to admit he also saw the wedding as a deadline. Nelle runs the Oriel and wants one thing she can actually do for her sister. She has seen Maris go quiet in front of a crowd and expects embarrassment. Her sentences group options, often with one situation-specific joke she tries to carry through. Ross is careful with time and generous with mileage. He wants Maris to count the effort he's made and to keep their wedding. He's heard her encouragement about the job as an offer of continuing together this way. He usually answers with something he can do and has a blunt account of a particular weekend he's proud of. His wish to stay near his work survives the conversation. All three are new characters.

**The conversation owed.** Nelle's attempt to talk Maris through the door must lead somewhere in its own right. Maris then owes Ross the decision to his face, and both owe each other an answer about the life they think they'd be agreeing to. Both exchanges have room in the booth.

**Place.** The Oriel Drive-In in a small market town at the end of September, dry and turning cold right before dark. A room off the projection booth smells of popcorn oil, a monitor radio has a papery hiss, sound rolls in from open car doors, front tires sit on gravel humps, and a loose corner of the white screen knocks in the wind. On an ordinary weekend Nelle parks families up clear of one another's sightlines and tells them to turn their headlights off. Tonight the FM channel reaches every row for the vows, and guests have been promised one short horn blast apiece afterward. The incoming cars and the planned drive out share one long lane. Rosebank Hotel's ballroom could contain the number but would let the couple slip out unseen. The municipal grandstand at Ryder Field would keep the crowd visible, with far less chance of the bride hearing her own decision come back to her from different vehicles. The lot's shape and the separation of car radios let the Oriel play both ways. [Saco Drive-In venue FAQ](https://thesacodrivein.com/faqs/)

## Scene briefs

1. **About 20%.** Maris in and around the booth at dusk. She has just finished putting her flowers in Ross's car when she sees his things laid out for leaving. Nelle comes from the gate with a live headcount over seven hundred. Maris wants the people sent home before they see her, and Nelle wants to get her through the first ten steps. Give the four lines room for a laugh and an attempted pep talk about taking those steps. Maris hands back the unopened whiskey and asks Nelle for Ross. One guest tests the horn early. Maris used to be able to judge by ear what a full lot sounded like.
2. **About 55%, the long scene.** Stay in the booth. Ross has come for a delay and finds he may lose the wedding. He has been working at their life every Friday, something he can tell her about with pride as well as hurt. She tells him how a Sunday together begins ending while they're still making supper. Let them argue over what one ordinary weekend meant, then say what they'd wanted of the wedding. Maris can ask him to stay tonight and hear his willingness before she asks for weekdays he cannot offer. She tells him the wedding is off today. Outside, the sound of several car doors closing makes a lull that the pair wrongly takes for privacy. Ross wants her to let him get to his car before she tells everyone. She waits for him to get there. Summarize the job and rental search when they're forced into the talk. Leave the engagement and the earlier wedding plans largely offstage.
3. **About 25%.** In the booth and then out on the lot. Nelle thinks she can make the announcement and spare her sister, and Maris wants to use the radio herself. She gives the crowd the news with no story about Ross. A horn sounds in the expectant silence and sets a few others off. Then all the lights start coming on as cars bunch up in the exit lane. Let one car turn a familiar attempt to congratulate her into unmistakable disappointment, audible through an open window. Maris walks into the headlights the guests were told to keep off. In the lane she reaches Ross at his open window, in view of the backed-up cars. In the opening she was putting her flowers on his passenger seat for a joint exit. Here she pauses long enough to say goodbye to him and lets him drive it out empty. The line makes her wait too. She can't disappear from the wedding quicker than anybody else.
