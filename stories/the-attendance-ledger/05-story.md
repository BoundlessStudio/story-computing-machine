# The Attendance Ledger

By the third name on the paper attendance ledger, Gideon Tolland knew exactly
how much sleep three of his students had not had.

Northbridge Secondary’s network had chosen community-showcase day to display a
spinning circle instead of the online register, so Gideon was using the paper
backup. The ledger was a grey administrative object with coffee feathered
along one corner. It possessed no insight. Everything it told him about those
three names, Gideon had brought to it himself.

“Drita Ademi?”

“Present.”

Drita held her left wrist close to her body. The cuff of her uniform jumper
covered most of the bruise, but not the careful way she turned each page with
her right hand. Her fingertips were pale with cold.

“Bryn Ahn?”

“Here.”

Bryn had already drawn two routes out of the problem on the lab sheet, although
the problem concerned mineral samples and neither route was required. Her
pencil trembled once, stopped under the pressure of her thumb, then continued.

“Cassia Dominguez?”

“Physically.”

The class laughed. Cassia offered them a thin grin and lowered her face over
her folded arms.

Gideon marked all three present. At one seventeen that morning, in the
voice-scrambled privacy of an encrypted radio relay, Kiteglass had reported a
bad wrist and loss of body heat. Pulsewire had said that lights still had halos
after two difficult severances. Mothlight had joked twice as fast as usual,
which had been the clearest report of all.

Night Clerk had recommended a recovery day. The three girls had chosen school.

Mr. Tolland, who had no legitimate way to connect those reports to these
students, had prepared accordingly.

“Before anyone asks,” he told the class, “the revision token on the board is
for everyone. One deadline, moved once, no justification required. Lab roles
rotate every twelve minutes. The snack box is not charity; it is protection
against the paperwork generated by fainting.”

That earned a better laugh. He passed the box along the benches. Cassia took
two oat bars without looking up. Drita accepted one after seeing three other
students do it. Bryn chose the role nearest the door.

Gideon had not meant to learn their secret. Six months earlier, three
voice-scrambled strangers had asked for practical help over an emergency-radio
frequency: routes, weather, first-aid supplies, someone to notice when brave
became reckless. They had named him Night Clerk as a joke, and the name had
stuck.

Then he had seen three transformed figures disappear into an alley and Drita,
Bryn, and Cassia emerge moments later. After that came matching absences,
matching injuries, Drita’s angle-cutting hand signal, Bryn’s insistence on two
exits, Cassia’s habit of making a joke before admitting fear. No single clue
had proved anything. Together, they left almost no room for him to be wrong.

He had kept that knowledge to himself. Their anonymity was not a puzzle he had
earned the right to solve.

“Feet on the floor,” he said. “Before we begin, five things you can see, four
you can feel, three you can hear. Yes, this is educational. I have declared it
so.”

The room settled by degrees. Cassia’s shoulders lowered. Gideon reached the
next line of his ledger.

From the ventilation grate above Drita came a papery whisper.

“Kiteglass?”

Drita’s head began to turn.

Gideon let the ledger fall.

It struck the floor flat, loud as a slammed door. Pages slewed beneath his
desk. Twenty-eight students looked at him instead of at Drita.

“A compelling demonstration,” he said, crouching, “of gravity’s refusal to
respect staff authority.”

In the shadow behind the grate, something black and thin withdrew: a living
filament, wetly quick, vanishing after it had enjoyed a clear view of the page.

The Rollcaller had survived the night.

Gideon gathered the papers without hurrying. “Change of method. Mark yourselves
present on the silent check-in slips. Apparently I am no longer qualified to
hold a book.”

The class bent to the slips. Drita did not look at the grate again.

At the time, switching the register felt like protection. Gideon would later
understand that the Rollcaller had watched him protect exactly one reaction.

* * *

At lunch, Gideon locked himself in the equipment room behind the lab and
plugged an ordinary headset into an ordinary phone. The voice scrambler made
him sound like a courteous cupboard.

“Something in the school ventilation tried my alias during attendance,”
Kiteglass said. “Right after a civilian name. Same whisper as last night.”

“Assume the Rollcaller survived,” Night Clerk said. “Could it see the register?”

“From that grate? Yes.”

Silence held on the relay. Their health and location channels showed only what
each girl had chosen to share: three rough status lights, no names, no map
points.

“How many people reacted?” Night Clerk asked.

“I nearly did,” Kiteglass said. She stopped, then said it again, flatter.
“Nearly. Then a book hit the floor and everyone looked that way.”

Gideon let her account stand without adding anything she had not told him.

“It chose the moment you were least prepared for,” Night Clerk said. “Anyone
would have turned.”

Pulsewire’s scrambled voice cut in. “It needs correlation. Names give it
candidates, reactions narrow them, cameras prove them. And we’ve got the
showcase this afternoon. There’s a guardian livestream.”

“Agreed. My recommendation is that you leave before the showcase. Available
alternatives: separate departures, remaining in public view, or a contained
trap somewhere you choose. You decide whether you engage at all.”

“Three of us leaving together would be a confession with backpacks,”
Kiteglass said. “And we built that presentation.”

“For six weeks,” Pulsewire added.

Mothlight had said nothing.

Gideon resisted the urge to fill the quiet with instructions. “Mothlight, do
you want options, quiet, or a grounding count?”

“Count. Then options.”

He counted five slow breaths. He could hear her match him by the faint hiss of
the microphone. No one hurried her.

“I’m scared,” Mothlight said at last. “That’s not a vote. Nobody count that
as a vote.”

“Understood.”

“I vote we stay,” she said. “I also vote we don’t improvise in front of a
guardian livestream, because I do have standards.”

Kiteglass proposed drawing the Rollcaller toward the empty rehearsal corridor
during showcase setup, away from the auditorium audience. Pulsewire sent a
photo of the fire plan posted by the stairs, cropped so the school’s crest was
gone, with the cameras she knew marked in red. She traced two exits on it: the
public auditorium doors and a service corridor with no camera sightline. Three
taps on the relay would call the corridor plan off. No debate afterward.

Then Pulsewire ran the rest of her checklist, the way she did before every
job. “If the network is compromised?”

“Disconnect.”

“Transformation orders?”

“Night Clerk never orders a transformation.” It was the first rule they had
made together. “If you hear one, the channel is compromised.”

“Good,” Pulsewire said.

He looked at the photographed plan, at the three status lights, at every
variable he wanted to close his hand around.

“Send me your final route,” Night Clerk said. “I’ll work out evacuation timing
from the fire plan.”

* * *

By midafternoon, the auditorium smelled of dust, hot projector plastic, and
the citrus cleaner used whenever guardians were expected.

Gideon rotated presentation jobs across the whole class. Anyone could hand
their speaking section to a partner. Anyone could use the revision token.
Drita kept her speaking part but let someone else carry the display board.
Bryn checked both exits before connecting the class laptop. Cassia stood in
the wing, named five things she could see under her breath, and walked onstage
when she was ready.

Their rehearsal was good.

On the second run, Drita lost her place when the display board shifted. Gideon
did not rescue her sentence. He reminded the entire class that a pause counted
as speaking, then waited. Drita reset the board with her right hand and
finished the explanation herself. Bryn quietly moved the laptop charger so it
would not cross either exit. Cassia shortened one joke, drank half a bottle of
water, and asked to repeat her closing section.

Those choices cost the schedule eight minutes. Gideon spent them without
hesitation.

Gideon checked the boring safety features while they reset: the heavy acoustic
fire partition recessed beside the stage, the breaker panel near the service
door, the blind corridor beyond it. Above the auditorium laptop, the stream
camera’s green indicator glowed beside a guardian-view counter reading zero.

At that moment, Bryn came from the laptop, Cassia came from the wing, and all
three girls leaned over the same cue sheet. A hair-thin filament beneath the
camera housing pivoted to follow them. Gideon’s stomach tightened. If the
thing had chased a trio last night and marked Drita at the grate this morning,
then her two showcase partners were only a guess, but a narrow one, and easy
to test.

The public-address speakers clicked.

An administrator’s voice said, “Drita Ademi, Bryn Ahn, and Cassia Dominguez,
report to the main office immediately.”

All three girls went still.

Whatever had sent that summons had just watched three students react as one.

“Continue setup,” Gideon said. “The office knows the traditional method of
contacting staff.”

He called from the wall phone. The actual office had issued no summons.

“An audio test entered the wrong channel,” he told the class. “A thrilling
preview of the competence adults will display this evening.”

The room loosened. Students returned to cables and cue cards. The three girls
did not leave together.

Yet when Gideon returned to his desk, the relevant ledger page was gone.
Along the baseboard, an ink-dark filament slipped beneath the auditorium’s A/V
rack.

The camera counter changed from zero to one.

Then to six.

The livestream had begun forty minutes early.

* * *

The projection screen flashed white. Three civilian names appeared across it
in block letters: DRITA ADEMI. BRYN AHN. CASSIA DOMINGUEZ.

Beneath them jittered blurred images from the previous night—three bright,
human shapes caught at bad angles by an ordinary street camera the Rollcaller
had threaded with its own living filaments. The refraction veil had smeared
faces and voices in the recording, as it was meant to do. It could not hide a
visible transformation, and it could not stop someone patient from placing two
sets of evidence side by side.

Question marks blinked between Drita Ademi and Kiteglass, Bryn Ahn and
Pulsewire, Cassia Dominguez and Mothlight.

The class stared. The view counter climbed.

Black filaments spilled from the A/V rack, thin as drawn ink and moving with
the intent of fingers. They threaded the camera mount, the speaker cable, the
door hardware. The Rollcaller had pushed living pieces of itself inside the
machinery and was operating it from within.

From every speaker came Night Clerk’s copied voice.

“Kiteglass. Pulsewire. Mothlight. Transform now.”

Gideon’s heart lurched.

The three girls did not move.

Night Clerk never gave that order.

“Everyone away from the screen,” Mr. Tolland said. “Leave bags. Service
corridor, two lines.”

The class knew his evacuation voice. Chairs scraped. A filament snapped across
the main doors and cinched them shut. Another reached for the camera, carrying
the Rollcaller’s living attention toward the growing audience beyond it.

Three taps struck Gideon’s earpiece. The corridor plan was off.

Pulsewire’s voice came through the relay, pitched low under the noise of the
room. Pain roughened the scrambled words. “The wiring’s one route. Our
channel’s the second. It’s bridged both. I need full blackout.”

His screen still held three weak telemetry lights: cold, tremor, strain. Cut
the breaker, and the school network, cameras, and relay would all die
together. He would lose their reports and their positions, and with them his
illusion that enough data could make their choices safe.

The copied voice crooned from the speakers, “Transform now.”

Gideon could have warned each of them by civilian name on the relay, and each
warning would have been a betrayal.

He had reached the breaker panel by the service door ahead of the class. He
turned his face into the doorway, away from the room, and pressed the transmit
switch under the scrape of chairs and the crooning from the speakers. “Facts
once. The local breaker ends the stream, the cameras, and this relay. Result:
darkness, no telemetry, no contact from me. Your decision.”

A breath. The answers came back as murmurs he could barely hear over the room.

Kiteglass said, “Dark.”

Pulsewire said, “Dark.”

Mothlight said, “Very, very dark.”

Gideon pulled the local breaker. A hijacked A/V rack was still electrical
equipment. School procedure gave the adult nearest the panel one sensible
action.

The view counter vanished at twenty-three.

The speakers died. The relay died. Every telemetry light went out.

The main doors were visibly bound. Mr. Tolland opened the designated service
exit, as the evacuation plan required.

* * *

Emergency strips painted the corridor floor a weak amber. Gideon moved the
class through the blind turn by touch and voice.

“Hand on the wall. Keep moving. If you can complain, save it for the incident
form.”

They obeyed. One student held the service door. Another guided a classmate
whose glasses had fallen. Ordinary competence moved down the line without
waiting for Gideon to distribute it. His duty was here, with the whole class,
even while every frightened instinct tried to turn him toward the three status
lights that no longer existed.

Behind him came the sound of tearing cloth, though no cloth in the auditorium
was large enough to make it. A cold, angled gleam crossed the narrow window in
the acoustic partition.

He did not see who stayed behind. He did not see hands close on focus tokens,
or living power pass through those nonliving shapes and become transformed
light. By the time he dragged the partition from its recess, three refracted
figures moved beyond the glass, their features blurred even to his knowing
eyes.

The Rollcaller hurled filaments at the evacuation opening.

A translucent plane struck them aside. It appeared at a steep angle, flashed,
and settled across the service route like braced glass. Behind it, Kiteglass
held one arm tight against her ribs. The barrier shivered each time the
creature hit. She could have chased the sheet-thin body retreating toward the
A/V rack. Instead she planted her feet at the door.

“Route first,” Gideon heard her say.

With students still passing through, fire procedure required the acoustic
partition between them and the auditorium.

He pulled the heavy partition. Something caught its lower track. A filament
whipped through the gap and wrapped the handle. Gideon hauled back. Pain
streaked from his shoulder to his elbow, bright enough to turn the corridor
white at the edges.

On the auditorium side, a sharp blue flicker ran along the speaker cable. It
ended with a crack. Someone gasped. The lights stayed off, but Gideon heard a
body strike the stage steps and then get up again.

“One bridge cut,” Pulsewire said. Her voice sounded far too loud in the dead
room. “Two active.”

The breaker had killed the school’s current, not the creature. Its own
filaments still ran out through the walls to lines the panel did not control.

The Rollcaller answered in Cassia’s civilian voice.

“I can’t. I can’t. I can’t.”

It spoke from the stage, the ceiling, the dead speakers, each imitation
slightly out of time. One refracted figure stopped moving.

Gideon could see only fragments through the narrow glass: a pale hand lifted,
a shimmer gone ragged, filaments turning toward the stillness. He had no relay
and no right to break the darkness they had requested. He held the partition
and listened.

Mothlight’s own voice, blurred by the veil and coming from one place only, said,
“One breath.”

The angled barrier widened. The blue flicker paused.

No one told her to hurry.

Gideon took one breath too, though his shoulder shook. Beyond the glass, the
still figure inhaled. Exhaled.

Then Mothlight filled the dark auditorium with liars.

That was all Gideon could make of it at first. Shadows ran where no bodies
could fit. Light folded against the walls and came away as half a dozen figures
shaped like Kiteglass, a scatter shaped like Pulsewire, Mothlight after
Mothlight lifting bright hands. Displaced voices called the three aliases from
opposite ends of the room.

The Rollcaller lunged at recognition. Filaments ripped out of the camera,
speakers, and cable channels to seize every apparent identity at once.

For a moment the A/V rack stood exposed.

Something behind it peeled upward: a narrow living body, ink-dark and
sheet-thin, its many extended pieces pulled taut toward false targets. A
translucent plane appeared above it. Another below. Two more folded inward,
not striking but enclosing.

The pressure against Gideon’s partition doubled. The filament around the
handle tightened. His injured arm began to fail.

“External lines,” Pulsewire said somewhere beyond his sight. “Everything it’s
still holding outside. Consent to cut?”

“Yes,” came one voice.

“Yes,” came another, after one deliberate breath.

Blue-white light snapped through every black strand.

The filament on Gideon’s handle went slack.

He dragged the partition closed with his good arm. The latch caught. On the
other side came a sound like a ream of wet paper dropped from a height, then
silence.

Gideon stayed against the partition, shoulder burning, while the last student
cleared the corridor. Through the window he saw only a clear angular cage, a
dark shape folded small inside it, and three blurred figures supporting one
another.

He did not know how they had changed their plan in the dark. He did not know
whether Pulsewire could still hear properly, whether Kiteglass’s wrist had
worsened, whether Mothlight’s remaining glow meant strength or the last of it.
For the rest, he waited for whatever they decided to tell him.

* * *

At the outdoor muster point, the story available to ordinary witnesses was
strange but small.

The power had failed. A corrupted showcase file had displayed three students’
names beside fuzzy costume footage. The guardian stream had ended before
anything clearer appeared. Cameras had gone dark with the breaker. No one had
seen a transformation. Most of the class had been looking at Gideon or the
service corridor when the lights died.

By the time explanations passed from student to student, the likeliest theory
was an ugly promotional stunt interrupted by bad wiring. In a city where magic
belonged to old stories and cheap effects, that answer was easier to believe
than three exhausted classmates turning into the impossible.

Drita, Bryn, and Cassia emerged from separate gaps in the milling students in
civilian clothes. Gideon did not see where they came from. Drita’s hands were
still cold. Bryn flinched at a whistle from across the yard. Cassia ate the
second oat bar in four bites.

“We went out the back of the stage,” Bryn said before he could ask. “Wrong
door. Took ages to get round.”

Gideon told them to stand with their row and asked nothing else.

The stolen page meant he had to rebuild attendance. He passed silent check-in
slips down the rows at muster, and each student marked their own presence.
Drita, Bryn, and Cassia made three honest marks. Gideon entered them without
alteration.

That evening, after food, warmth, an ordinary check of his strained shoulder,
and several hours in which the world remained blessedly unremarkable, Night
Clerk reopened the reset relay.

Three status lights appeared by choice.

“Containment?” he asked.

“Stable,” Kiteglass said. “I held it while Pulsewire checked the cuts. Then we
closed the body in an unplugged metal equipment case. We’ll decide what to do
with it when we’re not dead on our feet.”

“That is a strong argument for rest. Available support: food delivery, medical
checks, deadline help, route coverage, or no contact.”

“What, no schedule?” Mothlight asked. “You always send a schedule.”

Gideon looked at the recovery plan he had already drafted and turned it face
down.

“Not unless requested.”

They chose food, injury checks, and two mission-free nights.

“And before anyone asks,” Kiteglass said, “I stopped chasing it because this
wrist does a shield or a chase. Not both. So I held. It’s fine. It’s a wrist.”

Pulsewire said the last cut had only worked once the decoys drew the living
core out of the rack. Mothlight said she had needed one honest breath before
she could make twenty dishonest versions of them.

“Also my ears are still ringing,” Pulsewire said. “In case anyone’s keeping a
list.” The channel hissed for a moment. “New protocol. We ask for dark, you
give us the facts once and hang up.”

“Agreed.”

The next morning, Mr. Tolland did not call the register aloud. Silent
self-check-in slips waited on every lab bench.

“Permanent?” Drita asked.

“Trial policy,” Gideon said. “It reduces the number of opportunities for me to
drop important documents.”

Cassia smiled. Bryn checked the door, then bent over her slip. None of them
looked at Mr. Tolland as though they heard Night Clerk’s scrambled voice, or
as though they suspected he knew who they were.

Gideon opened the replacement ledger. It was still only paper, incapable of
knowing what its marks meant. Beside Drita Ademi, Bryn Ahn, and Cassia
Dominguez, he recorded three simple presences.
