# The Friends I Built

Belphegor, my archdemon parent, signed the wrong corner of my exhibition form
with all four hands.

It was an impressive mistake. Four elegant strokes of green fire braided
themselves around the ornamental skulls in the margin while the line marked
GUARDIAN remained offensively blank.

I waved the paper between Belphegor's face and the cushion.

I was deaf and non-speaking; I argued fluently with my hands and, when
necessary, my pocket slate.

Their upper eyes opened. Their lower eyes considered it an unreasonable hour
and remained closed. Belphegor lifted two hands to sign, *Beautiful work. Very
proud.*

The other two tucked the blanket beneath their chin.

I pointed to the blank line.

*Of course,* they signed. Their fingers were graceful even when the rest of
them had surrendered to the couch. *Put it here. I will correct it after one
small rest.*

I held up the kitchen clock. The guardian deadline was that morning. The
promised conveyance was due in twenty minutes. Belphegor smiled at me with
genuine confidence, shut their upper eyes, and became a part of the couch.

Talvi lay on the rug beside the couch, half inside a quilt and half outside it.
She had once been damned for reasons she called both complicated and badly
documented. She was much better at remembering my first diagrams than the
present day. Every lopsided machine I had drawn as a child was pressed between
sheets in her cabinet.

When I touched her shoulder, she woke at once and signed, *Exhibition. Today. I
remember.*

I showed her the clock.

She sat up. Hope rose in me, embarrassing and automatic.

*I'll arrange the ride now,* she signed. *Ten minutes.*

Then she leaned against the couch while searching for the dispatch slate. Her
eyes closed with her hand still under a cushion.

They loved me. This was not a theory. Talvi had learned every variation of my
signing as my hands grew. Belphegor could watch a diagram unfold for hours,
provided they were horizontal. Love had never been the missing component.
Completed tasks were.

I laid the form on my workboard. With a brass rule, I scored two precise folds
between the decorated margin and the guardian box. I fixed a paper hinge along
the first crease with a needle of sealing heat, then swung the signed corner
over the blank line without cutting or changing a stroke. The green signature
now occupied the required box. The ornamental skulls stared upside down, which
improved them.

The dispatch slate remained dark.

I strapped my pressure strips, projection plates, copper rods, and case of
embermoss cultures to a handcart. At the door, I looked back. Talvi had found
Belphegor's hand in her sleep. Belphegor's thumb moved once against hers.

I left them a note large enough to cover the clock.

I hauled everything to school alone.

By the time the black chimneys of the Cinder Annex rose beyond the ash trees,
my palms were blistered and the first lesson was over. Dependence, I decided,
was only another word for delay.

* * *

The Aptitudes Exhibition occupied the furnace hall for three days each year.
Heat shivered above the floor grates. Student projects crowded the iron tables:
glass gardens that folded away from touch, bone-white bridges carrying beads
of molten metal, and one cage full of shadows arranging themselves into rude
gestures.

The students around me suddenly gathered their papers. A moment later, a
lesson lamp blinked above Instructor Bexel's table.

The bell again. It sent no light until it was finished.

Bexel caught my eye and signed, *Sorry.*

She signed competently, which made the apology worse. We had discussed the
sound-only bell twice.

When everyone faced her, Bexel raised the exhibition rules. *Advanced-workshop
eligibility requires a collaborative entry. Teams lock in three days from
now.*

I read the rule again. My design had been accepted as a solo project the week
before.

Around me, tables closed like jaws. Students pulled stools together and bent
over shared plans. I carried my slate to Ekri. His flame-figure engine stood on
the next table, a copper drum connected to the hall furnace. Pressure from his
demonstration traveled through the flagstones in crisp, rhythmic taps I could
feel through my shoes.

I wrote, `JOIN PROJECTS? MY RELAY CAN MAP YOUR LOAD.`

Ekri glanced at the slate, then at the students watching him. He knew enough
sign to answer, though he used only one hand.

*Too slow,* he signed.

I waited.

His face tightened. He took my chalk and added beneath my question,
`LOOKING UP. WRITING. EXTRA TIME.`

He had spent longer explaining the inconvenience than a signed answer would
have taken.

The remaining teams found urgent reasons to study their own tables.

Bexel approached. She kept her face toward me when she signed. *I can approve a
solo accommodation.*

I pointed to the workshop requirement.

Her mouth pulled sideways. *You could exhibit. It would not satisfy the
collaboration criterion.*

An accommodation that removed the thing I was competing for. Efficient.

I wiped Ekri's answer from my slate. Bexel watched me write two new words.

`BUILD TEAM.`

Her eyebrows rose.

I had three days.

* * *

Embermoss looked dead until it was hungry. Mine lay in two shallow dishes,
grey as old scabs, while I built brass frames around them.

I had planned assistants: four spindle legs each, a hinge for pointing,
receptive plates for my relay, and simple flags for answers. Reliable bodies
for reliable instructions. I fed the moss bitter coal-sugar, touched my palms
to the growth plates, and supplied the shaping current my craft lessons called
living magic.

Green warmth passed from me into the cultures. I stopped when my wrists began
to tremble. The magic was mine to give; the growth that followed was theirs.

The first moss climbed its frame in neat, measured bands. It tested one leg,
then the opposite leg, balancing before committing its weight. The second
burst over the copper ribs, ignored half the channels I had etched, and formed
a round body with three unnecessary loops.

I pointed to the coal-sugar.

The precise one sampled a crumb and spat it onto my ruler.

The round one ate six, flashed orange through the thin moss over its core, and
knocked over the dish looking for seven.

I offered sweet ash instead. The round one rolled in it. The precise one
selected a second bitter crumb and ate it while standing on my ruler,
apparently because contradicting me mattered more than disliking lunch.

Assistants were not supposed to have culinary spite.

I set out a box of labeled tools so they could learn word tiles. The precise
one walked over every label, paused at `KNURL`, and rolled the textured wheel
under two feet. The tiny ridges seemed to please them. They drew the tile close
and raised both flags.

Knurl.

The round one climbed into my thimble and curled around it. When I tried to
retrieve the tool, they wrapped all three loops over its rim and displayed a
red panel.

I placed the `THIMBLE` tile beside them.

The panel changed to green.

Thimble.

Names, I discovered, made introductions necessary. I signed mine. Knurl
repeated the shape with hinged flags, careful and exact. Thimble attempted it
with two loops, got tangled, and turned the failure into a dance.

By the second day, both had grown warm enough to seek food on their own and
strong enough to rearrange my bench. Knurl sorted my washers by diameter.
Thimble hung colored scraps from the relay arms, where they served no purpose
except delighting Thimble.

I removed one. Thimble put it back.

I removed it again. Thimble showed red.

It was my relay. It was also becoming our exhibition, though I had not yet
admitted the distinction.

Knurl was adjusting a sensor plate when I noticed the bracket beneath them was
loose. I reached across, caught their brass frame, and lifted.

Every spindle leg locked. Both flags snapped flat against their body.

I set them down.

Knurl retreated behind the coal-sugar bowl and pushed three tiles into line.

`DO NOT TAKE.`

Heat climbed my face. I had been about to save them from a fall. I had also
given no warning and allowed no refusal.

I signed, *May I lift you to tighten the bracket?*

Knurl considered the bracket, considered me, then raised the yes flag.

The frame relaxed when my hands closed around it.

After that I asked. Sometimes Knurl raised the no flag and pointed out another
solution. Thimble usually showed green, except when sticky with ash, when they
apparently considered touch a privilege nobody deserved.

The relay itself still failed. My pressure strips could translate vibration,
heat, and load into colored light, but the projected map muddied whenever two
signals overlapped. I tried separating the channels. Knurl placed each plate
in a perfect ring. The colors sharpened, but the delay doubled.

Thimble climbed the projector, pulled down all the decorative sails, and
stretched them at unequal angles.

I signed, *That is worse.*

Thimble flashed red, then tapped the pressure strip and pointed.

The staggered sails caught each pulse in sequence. Knurl studied them, then
rotated the neat ring by one plate, destroying its symmetry to preserve its
spacing.

The map opened across the wall: blue for clear, yellow for strain, red for
danger. Fast, legible, and nothing like my original design.

Knurl raised both flags. Thimble shone green through their moss until the whole
room looked seasick.

On my slate I wrote, `FRIENDS?`

The word felt more dangerous than living magic.

Knurl selected `YES` and aligned it precisely beneath the question.

Thimble slapped up a green sail, stole the `YES` tile, and nested with it inside
the thimble.

* * *

On exhibition morning, the dispatch slate was still dark.

Talvi sat beside it with sleep creases across her cheek. Her eyes widened when
she saw the time. *I meant to confirm it.*

Behind her, Belphegor lay buried under four separate blankets. One hand
emerged to sign, *I was going to wake.*

I did not answer. If I opened my hands, anger would come out.

I packed the projection plates and pressure strips. I folded the exhibit
chassis into its case, then reached for Knurl.

They stepped away and raised the no flag.

Thimble planted all three loops across the case and flashed red.

`SAFE`, I wrote. `I CARRY.`

Knurl rearranged the tiles.

`ASK.`

Thimble added another.

`WALK.`

They drew their plan directly over mine. The chassis already had jointed
supports to hold the sensors against uneven floors. Turn the supports down.
Move the load plate forward. Use Thimble's sails to balance the case and
Knurl's exact weight shifts to steer.

It was undignified. It was also clever.

I looked at them both and signed, *Will you help me change it?*

Two yeses, different as fingerprints.

We built the cart in eleven minutes. It walked with a stiff six-legged gait,
carrying the heavy case while Knurl rode the forward plate and directed each
turn. Thimble perched on top beneath a striped sail. I hauled only when the ash
road steepened.

Talvi followed us to the door. *Irix, I'm sorry.*

I looked at the three-day-old note still covering the clock.

*I know,* I signed. Then we left, because sorrow did not move equipment either.

The three of us reached the Cinder Annex while the entry lamp was still green.

* * *

Instructor Bexel inspected our walking chassis, Knurl's flags, and Thimble's
sails. She looked from them to me before signing, *Are they entering as
collaborators?*

I asked them.

Knurl raised yes. Thimble displayed green.

Bexel amended the entry card. Then she pressed a glass scoring seal over the
chassis hinge.

*If that breaks before judging ends, the score is void,* she signed.

I nodded. Knurl examined the seal's alignment and adjusted it by a hair.

The furnace hall filled. When the opening bell sounded, I knew because faces
turned and shoulders jumped. I waited until Bexel lifted the green lamp before
starting.

I laid pressure strips across the demonstration floor. Knurl placed calibrated
weights while my relay projected each load in blue geometry. Thimble scampered
between the strips, sending bursts of gold across the map. The audience could
see where every foot landed, where heat collected, and where the iron beneath
them strained.

The fixed hazards belonged on the map too. The painted evacuation chevrons
pointed toward the east doors, though the furnace release grate faced their
approach. Ekri's cutoff lever hung behind his copper drum. Above the feed pipe,
Knurl stopped beneath a cold-air shutter and tilted their flags to match its
crooked edge. I added all three to the projection.

A student wrote a challenge on the question board:
`SHOW TWO OVERLAPPING LOADS WITHOUT LOSING DIRECTION.`

I started drawing the answer. Knurl crossed both flags.

For one hot instant I wanted to lower the flags myself.

Instead, I signed, *Show me.*

Knurl shifted the sensor ring. Thimble climbed the projection arm and unfurled
the staggered sails. My first diagram collapsed. Their altered pattern rose
through it: two currents, one violet and one green, crossing while remaining
distinct.

Understanding changed the audience's faces. Students leaned over the strips.
Hands pointed. A few tried signs they had seen us use. When they brought their
palms together in applause, I saw the motion first and felt a soft confusion of
impact through the floor.

I had wanted to impress them. I had not expected the pleasure to belong to all
three of us.

At the next table, Ekri prepared his final flourish.

His engine accepted the living current he pressed into its copper drum, then
shaped it into flame figures above the furnace mouth. Each figure landed with
a pulse strong enough to tremble my slate. The hearing students grinned and
covered their ears. Ekri grinned wider.

I had seen the feed latch earlier. Its two teeth met crookedly, leaving a
yellow stress crescent on my relay whenever the furnace drew hard. I had
pointed it out. Ekri had written, `HOLDS FOR ONE SHOW.`

Now he saw the audience watching our table.

He pushed both palms against the drum.

A many-winged figure of fire spread to the rafters. The flagstones bucked.
Ekri's latch jumped one tooth, then the other.

The furnace feed door fell open.

Black ember-grain poured into the chamber. Flame pressed white against the
vents. Students flinched and looked up, but the alarm lamps stayed dark. The
sound-only alarm was giving them a warning I could not receive.

Bexel stood on a table and signed the standard instruction: *East doors. Move
now.*

My relay painted the floor red.

Pressure was building beneath the feed line. The east corridor crossed
directly in front of its release grate. If the grate opened, every student
following the usual route would meet the ember spray.

I caught Bexel's eye and slashed both hands across the order.

She looked at the red map.

The release grate began to shake.

* * *

The projector was too weak to throw my plan across the whole hall.

The walking chassis held the power plate I needed.

I put my thumb against Bexel's glass scoring seal. For three days I had measured
every choice against the advanced workshop. I broke the seal.

Knurl was already loosening the power housing. Thimble stripped the balancing
sails free. Together we fed the plate into the projector, and my diagram
climbed the furnace wall in fierce color.

Blue arrows turned students away from the east doors and toward the rear
service passage. Red marked the release grate. Yellow marked the cold-air
shutter above the feed pipe—a narrow iron panel I had noticed because Knurl had
stared unhappily at its crooked edge during setup.

Bexel read the map. She signed, broad enough for the room, *Follow blue. Watch
Irix.*

Her authorization changed panic into movement. She took the front of the line,
opened the service barrier, and pointed students through.

I drew Ekri's engine in yellow, then the cutoff lever in red. Ekri's face had
gone grey. I jabbed two fingers toward the lever.

He moved. Heat forced him back once. He wrapped his exhibition apron around
his arm, reached past the drum, and dragged the cutoff down. The great flame
figure folded into the furnace.

The pressure map remained red.

Knurl pointed to the cold-air shutter.

I signed, *No. Stay here. Both of you.*

Knurl's flags flattened.

Thimble flashed a red so dark it was almost black.

The old wish rose in me: friends who could not fail because they could not
refuse. It looked ugly under the red light.

I made myself ask. *You understand the danger? You still choose this?*

Knurl pointed from the shutter to their spindle legs, then raised yes.

Thimble spread the largest sail and raised green behind it.

I wanted to keep them safe. They wanted to help keep everyone safe. Neither
want erased the other.

I signed, *Together.*

Knurl ran along the sensor rail and up the angled pipe. They hooked two spindle
legs into the shutter wheel and aligned the bent shaft one precise fraction at
a time.

Thimble anchored one loop around the remaining chassis bar. They stretched the
sail between the release grate and the last students entering the passage.

I kept the projection steady and changed the route as blue sensors cleared.
Ekri held the cutoff. Bexel counted every visible face through the service
barrier and waved the last cluster onward.

Knurl forced the shaft straight.

The cold-air shutter dropped open. Pale vapor washed across the furnace, and
the red pressure lines on my map began to shrink.

The release grate snapped wide anyway.

Embers struck Thimble's sail. Light ran through the cloth in a ragged orange
line. Thimble recoiled but held the fabric until the spray thinned and fell.

Then their green core dimmed.

The map turned blue at the same moment the judging lamp began to flash.

I let the projector fall.

By the time I reached Thimble, Knurl was beside them, both flags trembling.
The striped sail had shriveled along one edge. The felt beneath was scorched,
not split, and Thimble pulled one loop away from my hand.

Even then, I remembered.

*May I carry you?* I signed.

Thimble's panel flickered green.

I lifted them out of the ash.

* * *

Bexel voided our exhibition score.

She did it after helping me cool Thimble's moss, after Knurl had inspected
every stitch in the new patch, and after Ekri had written a complete account
of the latch he had rushed. The original striped sail stayed attached. Its
blackened edge showed beneath the patch. Thimble refused every offer to make
the colors match.

Then Bexel stood where the whole team could see her.

*The score measures an intact exhibition,* she signed. *Your seal was broken.*

I held Thimble closer, with permission.

Bexel continued. *The advanced workshop measures aptitude and collaboration.
Today demonstrated both.*

She amended the record to list Irix, Knurl, and Thimble as three collaborators.
Not maker and equipment. Three.

Ekri waited until I looked at him. `I SAW THE LATCH`, he wrote on my slate.
Then, beneath it, `I WANTED THE FINISH MORE. I WILL HELP REBUILD.`

I did not offer instant friendship. I handed him the list of damaged parts.
He nodded and began sorting them.

Before we left, Bexel posted three changes on the hall board. Future event
alarms would carry light and floor signals. Team formation would include an
accessible process supervised by an adult instead of leaving excluded
students to bargain for entry. Living collaborators would be registered as
participants and asked for consent.

Local rules on one school board did not repair everything. They repaired three
things that had nearly hurt us.

Bexel came home with us carrying a support agreement she had drafted, not one
she expected me to invent.

Talvi and Belphegor were awake.

Talvi saw Thimble's patched sail and covered her mouth. Belphegor sat fully
upright, all four hands empty. Their attention was so complete that I almost
made the task easier for them.

Instead I wrote on my slate:

`YOU LOVE ME. I LOVE YOU. LOVE IS NOT THE WORK.`

They read it.

`I WILL NOT REMIND YOU TO SUPPORT ME. I WILL NOT BUILD YOUR REMINDERS.`

Talvi's eyes filled. She did not ask me to comfort her. She opened the dispatch
slate, arranged a standing school conveyance for every exhibition and workshop
morning, and assigned its confirmation to the adult dispatcher. Bexel received
the backup notice. Only after the confirmation glowed green did Talvi sign,
*This is my responsibility. If it fails, I repair it.*

Belphegor placed all four palms on the couch. Green fire sank into the cushions
and stitched a narrow line to the household deadline slate. At the next marked
time, the couch snapped upright and deposited Belphegor on their feet.

They blinked, offended by their own success.

*My consequence,* they signed. *My maintenance.*

Bexel laid the agreement on the table. It made her the school contact when a
guardian task was missed, gave her authority to activate transport, and
required the adults to report failures to one another. She asked my consent
before adding my name to the communication list. I gave it.

Talvi signed the guardian line. Belphegor signed the witness line. Bexel
checked both before taking the document with her.

The next morning, the conveyance arrived while the couch was still forcing
Belphegor upright.

Knurl waited beside the repaired chassis, aligning the new bolts. Thimble sat
inside the old thimble under their visibly patched sail. Neither had been
packed.

I signed, *Do you want to come to school with me?*

Knurl placed `YES` squarely in the center of my palm, then stepped onto the
steering plate.

Thimble flashed green, climbed my sleeve, and used the patched sail to point at
the door.

Talvi did not ask whether the dispatch slate had worked. She checked it
herself. Belphegor, vertical and rumpled, held the door.

I entered the Cinder Annex with the friends I had built, and who had chosen,
again, to come with me.
