# Provisional outline

The request promises the victim's view of a familiar child-superhero triumph: the supposed happy ending is someone being beaten almost to death while a quip makes the spectators laugh. “I'm terrified” and “I left town” make the fear and the decision to flee central; “no idea how to hold back and no empathy” asks the violence to have consequences beyond a cartoon knock-out. I infer dark satire turning into bodily dread, with the reader made to understand why apparently ridiculous fear is reasonable. No particular criminal identity, relationship, scale, revenge, redemption, or hopeful ending is specified. The later permission to use the highest rating permits consequence-focused graphic injury; it does not require an explicitness quota.

## Chosen direction, open to revision

A first-person story told by an adult costumed burglar, provisionally **Len Voss**, known professionally as **Latch**. He has committed real, frightening crimes; the story will not make innocence the price of being entitled to survive surrender. His power can release a metal fastening through touch. It is useful for theft and escape, not a means of matching a superpowered child blow for blow. His account is immediate, caustic, sometimes unflattering to himself.

The present frame is an all-night coach station outside his former city. An adult criminal recruiter offers him enough money for further surgery if he returns for a job. Len's damaged speech and leg brace initially look like ordinary occupational costs. He refuses, then tells the recruiter why. The invented relationship is transactional, not a substitute parent or a cure for trauma. Its purpose is to make leaving costly and give the confession a listener who understands danger but initially misunderstands this particular danger.

Two young heroes, provisionally **Skip** and **Wallop**, control the remembered assault. They are children competing, improvising, repeating a successful joke, and insisting on their moral roles. They know they have won and continue anyway; they cannot estimate what their strength is doing inside a body, and a defeated villain's distress carries no weight beside their victory. The audience supplies encouragement without supernatural compulsion. Adults may be culpable, but no handler secretly orders the cruelty or takes the children's agency away.

## Concrete movement and ending

- The recruiter finds Len counting out a bus fare with a hand that no longer closes. The proposed job is back home. Len gives the prompt's confession, and the recruiter's surprised laugh makes him put the money away. He was once prepared to pay for professional danger; he cannot price what happened to him.
- In the remembered crime, Len and an adult accomplice rob a payroll office. They threaten people and mean the theft. When the young heroes arrive, Len releases his weapon's fastening and offers surrender. A child interprets the falling weapon as the opening for a spectacular finishing move. The accomplice is already down. The blows continue after resistance has ended.
- Stay with Len on the pavement through a brief, exact account of destroyed teeth, breathing impeded by blood, and a limb being moved in a direction it cannot move. A hero makes a joke about his lock-opening name while lifting his injured arm; the crowd laughs. Len's attempt to ask for an ambulance sounds like further villainous bluster through the damaged mouth. A bystander's phone captures the joke much more clearly than the request.
- Survival becomes the active problem. Len cannot fight or run. He notices that the children stop watching the bodies when they turn to deliver the punchline and receive the laughter. He has one working hand and the fastening of the accomplice's heavy costume within reach. During a repeated quip staged for a second recording, he releases that costume, gets the other injured adult out of it, and drags them both out of the heroes' sight beneath the parked rescue vehicle. The bright discarded costume remains where the cameras expect the defeated villain. A paramedic finally reaches the two living bodies. No crowd-changing speech occurs; the heroic celebration continues above them.
- Afterward, the published clip ends at the laughter. Len's account continues through the hospital, his accomplice's permanent injury, and the painful practical act of leaving. The surgery money in the present remains tempting. The recruiter suggests that knowledge of the children's habits could be useful in a rematch. Len refuses that story too and spends his last fare going farther away.

The ending rewards escape rather than a better quip, a return fight, or the discovery that the children were kind underneath. Len does not acquire a lesson that makes the assault acceptable. His frighteningly undramatic victory is that this time he gets to leave before anyone begins applauding.
