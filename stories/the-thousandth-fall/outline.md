# Outline

## Story

- Central promise: In close third person, past tense, monk-healer Cavori discovers that sustaining self-healing through extreme exertion lets his body preserve each magical repair as adaptation instead of merely returning to its old baseline; he trains past human limits and must prove his absurd strength, unrivaled stamina, and impossible speed in a rescue rather than a fight.
- Character want and pressure: After he can mend people only once slow rescue has already delivered them to the monastery, Cavori wants to become fast and strong enough to reach danger before wounds become irreversible. His elders fear that using mercy to override every bodily warning will make him value himself only as a tool, while Cavori's mounting success convinces him that one more threshold will finally make him sufficient for every crisis.
- Turn: On the brink of recording his thousandth training fall, a rain-loosened section of the monastery's cliff road gives way beneath novices and pilgrims; Cavori abandons the private milestone and applies the “wrong” use openly, discovering that power matters only when he can choose whom it serves and accept help with what it cannot solve.
- Ending intent: Cavori survives the literal thousandth fall while saving the last novice, fully demonstrating the promised strength, stamina, and speed. He rejects both the elders' simple prohibition and his own self-erasure: the method remains a dangerous local discipline under safeguards, not a universal doctrine or an excuse to make one healer responsible for everyone.

## Beats

1. A storm casualty arrives too late for Cavori's self-healing gift to matter: he can close his own strain after helping carry the litter, but cannot give the injured traveler back the time lost on the cliff road. The gap between healing harm and preventing it fixes his want.
2. When a cracked infirmary beam drops during the same storm, Cavori holds it long enough for others to escape. Instinctively keeping his healing active through the effort repeatedly repairs failing muscle instead of waiting for rest; after everyone is clear, he realizes the repaired tissue has retained the load rather than returned fully to its former limit.
3. Under the infirmarian's observation, Cavori confirms the inversion without anatomical or training detail: ordinary healing restores a finished injury, but healing directed through ongoing exertion lets each repair become the body's new starting point. The abbot calls it the wrong way because it converts pain from a warning into something he can ignore, and permits only bounded trials with witnesses and rest.
4. A compressed training sequence makes each gain causal and visible. Cavori repeatedly lifts what stopped him, runs the cliff stairs before each repair has cooled, and holds his healing through longer circuits; his strength compounds, the same repaired motion becomes startling speed, and his healing focus develops enough endurance to sustain both without pause. Each genuine collapse or loss of footing is counted as a “fall,” keeping progress concrete while the narration remains non-graphic and non-instructional.
5. As the count climbs, feats that once required teams become casual: Cavori shoulders a stone lintel, overtakes a downhill cart, and works from dusk into the next morning without flagging. The monastery benefits, then begins unconsciously routing every heavy or urgent task to him; he accepts them all, hides his fear that stopping will make him ordinary again, and turns the elders' safeguards into obstacles to evade.
6. After fall nine hundred ninety-nine, the abbot confronts Cavori beside the tally marks. Cavori insists the thousandth will prove the method safe because he has survived the previous ones; the abbot answers that survival proves only what his magic can repair, not that his judgment, pain, or obligations are inexhaustible.
7. The argument is broken by the cliff road collapsing under returning pilgrims and novice escorts. Cavori outruns the descending rubble to warn the lower bend, races back uphill again and again to carry people clear without losing speed, and braces a broken stone span on his shoulders while the able-bodied survivors organize ropes and move one another across.
8. When the last novice slips beyond the span, Cavori releases the impossible weight only after the others take the ropes, dives faster than the falling child, and takes the impact on a lower ledge while healing continuously. This is the thousandth fall, but its meaning changes from proof of personal invulnerability to a freely chosen rescue made possible by communal support.
9. In the aftermath, Cavori allows others to treat, feed, and question him although his body can stand unaided. He and the abbot retain the method as a guarded practice whose unknowns must be respected; the final image pairs his effortless lift of a rescue stone with his deliberate wait for others to secure it, resolving the power fantasy without pretending power abolishes consequence.

## Continuity

- Canon used: Magic is real but culturally classified, multiple unrelated systems may exist, and only living agency uses it. Cost and desire are not universal fuels, so Cavori himself supplies this living, locally observed self-healing without an artifact or institution acting as a magic user. The treatment stays broadly accessible, non-graphic, and focused on choice and consequence.
- Boundaries and unknowns: This unplaced high-magic era, monastery, self-healing mechanism, adaptation effect, and “thousand falls” practice are new and local. They establish no universal healing rules, biological ceiling, training method, power cost, monastic institution, chronology, or connection to any existing healer, transformation, body-modification, or restoration system. Whether anyone else can reproduce Cavori's inversion, what its ultimate limits are, and whether it can fail despite active healing remain unresolved; healing does not erase pain, impaired judgment, other people's agency, or the need for ordinary rescue work.
