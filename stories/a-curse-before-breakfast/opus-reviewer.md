## Verdict

- Dialogue: PASS — every exchange holds up on grounding and uptake, the siblings are told apart by stance and pressure, and there are no thesis lines; two Minor polish items.
- Prose: PASS — concrete, varied and funny first-person narration with no systemic tic; one spelling slip.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is delivered in full and the ague's rules hold; one small staging slip with the toast plate.
- Edit scope: polish
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, Dialogue PASS; agree, though it missed the plate slip and the opaque "Awful."

This is a two-hander that works. Almost every exchange is tied to an object or errand (the pirate novel, the headphones, the bitten biscuit, the quilt, the tickets, the eyeliner pencil), and each bargain changes who fetches and who gets the sofa. The dialogue moves the plot rather than commenting on it. The collection's clipped house register does show up, in Coris's deadpan and in the coda's matched comebacks. Here it drives the sibling bargaining and the role reversal, and it is broken up by Philip's pleading, his fall into one-word wants under fever, and physical beats that carry the affection neither sibling will say aloud. The edit pass should stay at polish: settle one opaque deadpan, make clear who says the hinge line, fix one staging slip and one spelling, and add nothing else. That means no extra wit, no explanation of the reversal, and no new exchange.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · Opaque one-word verdict at the door

- Where: L35–L39, bedroom doorway, just after Coris checks Philip's place in the pirate novel
- Text:
> She took it from me, checked my place, and held it out again. "You were nearly finished."
> "Awful."
- Problem: Ground/referent. "Awful." could be mock sympathy (so close to the end, and now a test), a judgment on him for reading instead of studying, or a hint about how the book ends. Philip never picks it up, so nothing settles which. Coris's other doorstep deadpans ("Tomorrow.", "That's pirates.") all have clear targets. This one reads as a stock one-word verdict rather than a sleepy sister reacting to something.
- Fix: Either cut it, so Philip's foot in the door follows "I know." directly, or give the reaction a target the reader can hear. The best target also shows she is about to shut the door, since that is what the next paragraph's foot responds to. Illustrative only: "Poor you. Night." Keep it one short line; don't turn it into a quip about pirates or studying.

### D2 · Minor · The hinge line relies on alternation for its speaker

- Where: L195–L217, Philip's bedroom, when Philip remembers the transfer and pitches it
- Text:
> "It's too much," I said.
> "You could take it."
> "I could."
> "It's not that bad."
> Her eyes went to the six tissues on the duvet, then back to me.
- Problem: Attribution. The joke is that Philip, who has just called the ague "too much", plays it down as soon as it would be hers. That only works if the reader hears the line as his. Untagged straight after Coris's "I could.", it can read as Coris going on ("it's not that bad, put up with it"). Only her glance at the tissues settles it afterward, so the story's hinge lands a beat late.
- Fix: Add a plain tag so the line is clearly Philip's. Illustrative only: "It's not that bad," I said. Keep Coris's silent look at the tissues as the only comment on it; do not add a line that explains the reversal.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · American spelling in British-register narration

- Where: L19, the first line of dialogue at Coris's door
- Text:
> "I need a favor," I whispered.
- Problem: "favor" is the only American spelling in a story whose register is British throughout (Mum, rings, travelling, takeaway, chips).
- Fix: Change it to "favour". Nothing else needs changing; "recognized" (L157) is acceptable British -ize usage.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · The toast plate leaves the room, then is still there at sunset

- Where: L301, the sitting room after the transfer; L369–L371, sunset
- Text:
> She shook her head, then changed her mind, and I brought the plate back to the kitchen.
> "Your toast's still there."
> She looked at the cold plate and began hunting inside the pillowcase for the ticket.
- Problem: Staging. "Brought the plate back to the kitchen" reads as taking the toast away. Hours later Philip's jab and Coris's look both need the plate beside her. The intended reading is probably that he cut the toast smaller and brought it back, but the text never says so, and the sunset callback depends on it.
- Fix: Make L301 leave the plate within her reach, for example by having him cut the toast and return it. Illustrative only: She shook her head, then changed her mind, so I cut it into fingers and put the plate back by her elbow. Leave L369–L371 unchanged.

## Recurring patterns

- No dialogue or prose tic needs a sweep. D1, D2, P1 and C1 are single instances.
- Earned pattern, not a tic: after Philip fetches something, Coris makes it more specific in one line (L245, L255, L299, L315, L323). This is the role-reversal joke, mirroring Philip's own demands from bed (L123, L135, L163, L221). Do not vary these away or add more.
- One-line "She [verb]" action beats between spoken lines (for example L25, L29, L59, L145, L149, L223, L241, L275) are ordinary pacing in a two-hander, not the narrative pulse; the prose paragraphs around them are multi-sentence. Leave them.
