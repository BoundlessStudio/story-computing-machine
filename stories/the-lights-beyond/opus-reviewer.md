# Opus review: The Lights Beyond

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: PASS. Every exchange holds up on the scene's shared reality and changes the siblings' relationship. The fixes are small: one jab that echoes a word instead of describing what happened, one over-neat mirrored line, and narration that explains lines the reader already understood.
- Prose: PASS. The prose is concrete, varied and nearly free of em dashes. "Aircraft" breaks the Haskett-limited vocabulary, and there are a few staging snags and one stock beat.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS. The story delivers the lights, the metal birds, the first contact, the inherited enmity and a complete arc. The days and the money add up. There is one water-infrastructure policy nit.
- Edit scope: polish
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS". I agree, but it missed the polish items below.

The dialogue is sparse and mostly earns its place. The siblings share the same clipped field talk. Their stance tells them apart: Haskett keeps the plan (deadlines, orders, lists of risks). Dunley jokes until the argument starts, then concedes in single words and finally says what he wants. Pirvek communicates almost entirely by gesture: names, a drawing in the dust, a buckle, coins, a cupboard key. That is the story's best choice about how people talk. The dock argument turns on concrete things (the kitchen, the stove, a deposit, three hundred crowns) rather than principles. Haskett's decision comes through action and one sideways line. Where it slips, it slips toward the collection's house habits. One jab bends its wording to echo the line before it. One mirrored reply states the premise outright. Narration then explains lines the reader has already understood. The edit pass should mostly cut and pin lines to on-page events, not add talk.

## Edit guardrails

- Prompt: Haskett and Dunley are the first human scouts beyond the Great Wall in five hundred years. The city "covered in lights" (L49) and the metal birds crossing the night sky (L51–L53) must stay. Keep the length inside 2,500–4,000 words; the story is about 3,570 now. Keep the tone broadly accessible, with moderate tension and non-graphic content. The active craft profile is prospective-2026-08-23.
- POV is close third, past tense, through Haskett. Do not translate the Northern speech. Do not add an all-knowing explanation of Roskell's history or technology. The only Northern words are "Pirvek" and "Roskell".
- Internal facts to keep:
  - Timeline: six days, noon on the sixth. They leave at sunrise on day three and reach the Wall on the fifth morning.
  - Money: 600 crowns saved, 300 each, 40 short of the asking price. Haskett carries four travel crowns and pays two.
  - Seating: Haskett on the left, on the city side; Dunley on the right, on the sea side.
  - Haskett carries the cupboard key during the flight.
  - Pirvek's tusk has a silver cap.
  - The boots-and-twine thread runs through L21–L23 and L263.
- Universe: no LOCKED or CANON entry covers the Wall, the horde or Roskell. Personhood protection and the audience/content boundaries (both LOCKED) apply. Keep the tusked inhabitants ordinary people.
- What works and must not be flattened:
  - The concrete objects in the dock argument.
  - Haskett's decision made by action: weapons into the cupboard, the key, pointing at the seat.
  - No talk during the flight.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Minor · Map jab misstates the tear

- Where: L127–L129, at the dock, after Pirvek stops Dunley touching the wing tube. It calls back to L69.
- Text:
> “He thinks I'm going to break it,” Dunley said.
> “You nearly broke your map.”
> Dunley put the map away so quickly he tore it on a buckle.
- Problem: A Punchline Patch with a small Ground slip. The reply borrows "break" from Dunley's line, but you don't break paper. "Nearly" is also wrong: he did tear the map (L69). And it is "their map" (L63), not his. The tease is still easy to follow, which keeps this Minor. But it is the one line in the story worded for the echo rather than for what happened.
- Fix: Make the jab name what actually happened, or cut it and let Dunley's line stand alone. Illustrative only: "You tore the map just putting it away."

### D2 · Minor · Mirrored reply states Dunley's reason before he gives it

- Where: L183–L193, the dock argument about the inn.
- Text:
> “This morning you wanted it.”
> “This morning I hadn't seen any of this.”
> I want to find out how that thing works. I want to see where the big ones go.
- Problem:
  - Ground/referent: the only on-page moment of Dunley wanting the inn is the naming joke on day one, "as they started climbing" (L31–L35). This scene is the night of day two, so "this morning" points to something off the page while the reader remembers yesterday.
  - Echo: Dunley gets a neat mirrored last word that states the story's premise (seeing it changed what he wants). At L193 he says the same thing again, better and more concretely, as a three-part "I want" list. The argument already has two word-for-word reframes (L159–L161 and L191–L193). This third one feels the most written and least spoken.
- Fix:
  - Tie Haskett's jab to something on the page. Illustrative only: "Yesterday you were naming it."
  - Don't let Dunley match her phrasing. Give him a non-answer, a look at the harbor, or silence, and let L193 carry his reason.
  - At L193, consider cutting one "I want" clause so it doesn't read like a prepared list.
  - Keep "I can't spend my three hundred yet."

### D3 · Minor · Narration explains the argument's best lines

- Where: L161–L163, L173–L175 and L203, in the dock argument and Pirvek's refund.
- Text:
> “I'm waiting for you to decide if you want to come.”
> She heard the difference. So he would go by himself.
> There it was, in a form that she could do nothing with except hear it.
> Even he had understood that much.
- Problem: The two strongest indirect lines in the story are "I'm waiting for you to decide..." and "Don't give the innkeeper a deposit." Each is followed at once by narration that explains it, and Pirvek's plain coin gesture gets the same treatment. The reader has already worked these out; the explanation takes that away. "Even he" also reads as slightly condescending to Pirvek.
- Fix:
  - Delete "She heard the difference. So he would go by himself." The next sentence, "Pirvek might refuse a single passenger; she caught herself hoping he would.", already implies it.
  - Cut L175, or replace it with a small physical beat, such as her looking at the coins or at his hands.
  - Delete "Even he had understood that much."
  - Keep "She could have borne another joke more easily." (L201). It shows her feeling rather than explaining the line.

### D4 · Minor · "Keep your own key" relies on keys never shown

- Where: L257–L261, in the barracks, dividing the savings.
- Text:
> “I'm keeping mine here for now,” he said.
> She set his bag back inside. “Keep your own key.”
- Problem: A mild Ground/referent issue. The story never mentions the strongbox's keys, so the reader has to infer that each sibling has one and that she is telling him to hold his instead of leaving it with her. The line is plainly meant to echo the cupboard key she carried on the flight. That makes it read a little like a closing flourish rather than something said over a strongbox. At the last exchange between the siblings, it is harder than it needs to be to tell whether she is letting him go or taking a small dig.
- Fix: Tie the words to one physical action so they do a practical job. Illustrative only: she works the second key off her cord and puts it in his hand. Keep the spoken line short and don't explain it.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Minor · "Aircraft" breaks Haskett's vocabulary

- Where: L101, L121, L165, L203, L243, from the dock to the flight.
- Text:
> beside the moored aircraft
> Finally Dunley pointed at the aircraft.
> A larger aircraft rumbled above the harbor mouth.
- Problem: Elsewhere the close-third narration names the North in Haskett's own terms: "metal birds", "a wheeled box", "a little caged wheel", "six small suns". In her report she calls them "the flying boats" (L265). Her culture has no flight, so "aircraft" is not a word she would use. Here it breaks that strangeness at the dock and during the argument.
- Fix: Replace it with "bird", "flying boat", "craft" or "winged boat", whichever fits each sentence. "Pilot" and "engine" can stay.

### P2 · Minor · Dock staging: repeated ledge, two kinds of belt, unclear gesture

- Where: L135–L139, L189 and L207, at the dock.
- Text:
> set it on the board's narrow ledge
> He nodded and left both on the board's narrow ledge.
> The pilot pointed toward the coins, leaving them where they were, and stood back with folded arms.
> She picked up both bows, gathered the belts, and returned to the cupboard.
- Problem:
  - "The board's narrow ledge" repeats within four lines.
  - "Belts" means knife belts at L107 and L207 but seat belts at L131, L189 and L217. At L207 it comes soon enough after "held up the belts again" (L189) to trip the reader.
  - The L189 gesture (pointing at the coins, not taking them, arms folded) is hard to read. It could mean refusing, waiting, or "settle it between you".
- Fix:
  - Change the second ledge to "left both there".
  - At L207, write "knife belts".
  - At L189, make the gesture readable in one clause (for example, he leaves the coins and waits for them to decide), or cut the pointing.

### P3 · Minor · Stock "crying without noticing" beat

- Where: L241, the flight over the city.
- Text:
> and found she had been crying a little, without noticing when she started.
- Problem: This is a stock beat, a cousin of "the breath she didn't know she was holding". It is tacked onto one of the story's best images, her absurd search for the dog.
- Fix: End on the laugh, or give one concrete physical sign, such as wiping her face on her sleeve. Don't name the emotion.

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Minor · Policy nit: street gutter used as texture

- Where: L91, the fan and the dog.
- Text:
> It got up and lay in the rainwater gutter instead.
- Problem: The style guide's CANON rule "No default water infrastructure" says even passing references to drainage count. A street gutter is drainage texture the prompt didn't ask for. The harbor, docks and piers are fine: the seaplanes need open water, and seas are expressly allowed.
- Fix: Move the dog somewhere else out of the breeze, such as the far side of the step or the shade of a cart. Keep the joke and Dunley's laugh.
