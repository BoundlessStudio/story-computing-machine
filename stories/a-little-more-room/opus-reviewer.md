# Opus review: A Little More Room

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — the voices are distinct and the relationship moves, but three lines depend on things the listener never heard or saw (an unplanted "You said", a plea answering an exit that happens only in Robin's head, a record that is two objects at once), and the narration explains the key exchanges right after they land.
- Prose: REVISE — the narrator explains the blush and his own usefulness several times and ends on a thesis sentence; "not X, Y" definitions recur as a tic.
- Continuity and prompt: REVISE — every prompt element is delivered, but the green-sleeved record is both the rejected "tidy" record and its replacement at L203–L209; several small staging slips.
- Edit scope: targeted-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS, "Dialogue: PASS", Internal continuity PASS; disagree — it missed the record contradiction and the Ground slips in L57 and L265.

This story's dialogue is mostly sound, and the edit pass should not treat it as something to rebuild. Robin and Suzette pass the cover-the-tags test. He deflects with figurative quips and over-helping (felt pads, record pitching). She answers literally and formally, then turns that literalism into gentle, pointed insistence ("They are not moving." "It was during the handclaps."). The exchanges move from tolerance to stated preference, and the decisive request lands plainly. The failures are local. A few lines rest on something the listener never received, or on an object the story has scrambled, and the narrator keeps explaining what an exchange meant once it has already landed, including at the decisive beat. Repair those specific lines, cut the glosses, and leave the banter rhythm, Suzette's literal register and Robin's failing jokes alone.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Major · "You said you liked bass" cites a line Robin never said

- Where: L57, return-of-the-record scene; the first meeting (L13–L39) is played in full
- Text:
> "You said you liked bass. This one has a good bass part in the second track. I think.
> "A blue-label reissue. Lots of brass, no restraint."
- Problem: Ground/knowledge. Suzette cites a preference Robin never states. In the first meeting he says "brass" and "Very much" (which is a lie, L37), never bass. The scene is played line by line, so the reader goes looking for the remark and can't find it. It matters because the ending makes bass Robin's first honestly stated preference ("I like the bass after the second handclap.", L297), so the setup needs to be real.
- Fix: Either plant it in scene one (Robin, nervous, adds something about the bass line when he describes the record at L29), or ground her line in what she actually heard: bass is what comes through a shared wall. Do not add a quip. Illustrative only: "Your bass comes through my wall more than anything else. I thought you must like it."

### D2 · Major · "Please don't leave" answers an exit that exists only in Robin's head

- Where: L251–L265, decisive dance exchange
- Text:
> I could have said yes. I could have packed the records, left with everybody's dignity mostly unopened
> She paused, then tried again, more plainly. "Please don't leave. I want you to dance badly with me."
- Problem: Ground/uptake. Robin's leaving is only a conditional thought in narration. On the page he stays by the cabinet, argues about looking ridiculous and makes "an unhelpful circle" with his hand. Suzette has nothing observable to answer with "Please don't leave", so at the story's decisive beat she seems to reply to his private deliberation. Separately, "I could have said yes" right after "Do you mean no?" makes the reader stop and work out that yes means no.
- Fix: Give Robin one visible move toward leaving inside L253. The tidy line of sleeves he has been building at the cabinet (L213, L239) is the obvious hook: he starts sliding them back into the crate, or reaches for the crate handle. Then her plea answers something she saw. Rephrase "said yes" so it can't be misread. Illustrative only: "I could have let it stand as a no." Keep "I wasn't asking you to look good." and "I want you to dance badly with me." as they are.

### D3 · Major · Narration decodes exchanges immediately after they land

- Where: L81 (tidy-track disagreement), L131 (range-day exchange), L187 (filter explanation), L235 (decisive exchange)
- Text:
> Then she went home, leaving me with the useful discovery that sweetness did not oblige her to approve of my guesses.
> There was no invitation to turn her habit into a biography.
> It seemed more respectful than turning her explanation into a question period.
> She glanced down, and I heard the request hidden inside my line: tell me my records weren't a nuisance; tell me I wasn't. She left it unanswered.
- Problem: Subtext glossed. Each of these exchanges is already legible from its lines and beats: she dislikes the track he said was like her; she answers the gun question minimally and waits; he stops asking questions; she won't give the reassurance his self-mockery fishes for. The narration then tells the reader what it meant, which leaves nothing to infer. L235 is the worst case because it sits inside the decisive exchange and spells out the exact subtext that "She glanced down" and her next line ("I wanted another dancer.") already carry.
- Fix: At L81 stop at "Then she went home." Cut the L131 sentence and let "She looked at me, waiting." plus his change of subject carry it. If the range-day run (L123–L129) then feels too bare, make Robin's "It's Saturday." carry its implied question a little more audibly (why carry it when it isn't range day?), but add no explanation afterwards. At L187 keep the repeated "Okay," and cut the question-period sentence. At L235 keep "She glanced down." and cut the rest.

### D4 · Minor · "Better pressing than the copy I lent you" has no clear object

- Where: L143, Robin sorting his crate at the party
- Text:
> "This next one has a better pressing than the copy I lent you," I said.
- Problem: Ground/referent. "Pressing" compares copies of one album, and the copy he lent her is *Satellites for Beginners*. So the line suggests he has brought a second copy of that album, but the only copy in the crate later is "the blue one" he lent (L289–L295). The reader can't tell what "this next one" is.
- Fix: Make it plainly a different record, and say what's better about it in words Robin would use (sound, bass, mix), or drop the pressing comparison and keep the kitchen-pans line that follows.

### D5 · Minor · "The old description" has no recoverable source

- Where: L181, filter explanation
- Text:
> "The old description says I can't think evil thoughts or dirty thoughts. By dirty, it means sexual thoughts. Not mud."
> She managed the sentence in one careful run
- Problem: Ground/referent. "The old description" could be a manual, a sales listing, a medical file or something else, and nothing in the story says which. The line also reads as the prompt quoted back. The following narration calls three sentences "the sentence".
- Fix: Anchor the source in something she would plausibly cite and that fits her life (her paperwork, what she was told when the filters were fitted), in her own words, or drop the attribution and let her state it herself. Keep "Not mud." as her joke. Change "the sentence" to "the explanation" or similar.

## Prose findings

### P1 · Major · Themes explained repeatedly, ending on a thesis sentence

- Where: blush motif at L193, L229, L299; Robin's usefulness at L133, L151
- Text:
> It occurred to me how often I had treated it like a caption under her face, translating every word into an emotion she had not named.
> Her face was bright red, but the color could not answer for her.
> They might have meant nothing I could name. She had told me what she wanted, which was better information.
> allowed me my moment of unnecessary usefulness
> Selecting music gave my hands a job and placed me at the edge of the room, where I was most comfortable.
- Problem: The blush is introduced as unreliable at L47 and L55, and the dialogue at L195–L201 states it cleanly ("No. I always blush." / "So I've been reading unreliable instruments."). The narration then restates it three more times and closes the penultimate paragraph on the lesson itself ("which was better information"). The style guide asks that a point, once clear, not be explained again in narration and a closing emblem. The same goes for Robin's hiding in usefulness: sorting records at the edge while she dances already shows it.
- Fix: Keep L47 and L55 as the setup and L195–L201 as the one explicit statement. At L193 keep "Suzette's blush had not faded." and cut the caption sentence. At L229 end at "Her face was bright red." At L299 keep the heat/speaking/adults sentence and cut the last two. Trim the L133 clause to the action (she let go) and cut the L151 sentence about his hands having a job. The last two lines of the story (L301–L303) should stay as the ending, with no explanation after them.

### P2 · Minor · Definition-by-negation and button sentences as a recurring template

- Where: L21, L35, L47, L99, L159, L251, L271
- Text:
> Small did not make it decorative.
> Her voice was soft, but not frail.
> The hope in her face did not disappear. It became harder to read.
> It was not offended stillness.
> She waited, growing quieter rather than retreating.
> careful without being delicate
- Problem: The narrator often defines something by what it is not, or ends a paragraph on a neat button. Any one instance is fine; together they become the narrator's default sentence shape.
- Fix: Keep two or three of the strongest (L47's "weather, not necessarily news" and L99 both do real work). Recast the others as direct observation, or cut them where the next sentence already makes the point (L159's search-a-shelf image stands without "It was not offended stillness.").

## Continuity and prompt findings

### C1 · Blocking · The green-sleeved record is both the rejected tidy record and its replacement

- Where: L57–L83 (her record lent to Robin), L173 (filter talk), L203–L209 ("Not this one" beat)
- Text:
> She had tucked a second record behind mine, in a green paper sleeve softened at the corners.
> The first track was smooth electronic pop
> "I can think your tidy record is bad."
> From my crate she selected the tidy electronic record I had said seemed like her.
> She put on the green-sleeved record instead and skipped straight to the second track.
- Problem: At L57–L83 the tidy electronic track Robin said "seems like you" is the first track of Suzette's own green-sleeved record. At L203–L209 she takes "the tidy electronic record I had said seemed like her" from his crate, rejects it, and plays "the green-sleeved record instead", which treats them as two different records. L173's "your tidy record" assigns it to Robin as well. The "Not this one." / "Not this one." echo is a key relationship beat (she rejects his reading of her and he accepts it), and the reader can't tell which object she is holding.
- Fix: Pick one version and make L173, L203 and L209 agree with it. (a) One object: Robin has brought her green record back in his crate. She holds it up for "Not this one" about its first track, then drops the needle on track two. Rewrite "instead" at L209 and have L173 point to that track rather than to "your" record. (b) Two objects: Robin packed a separate neat, cheerful record because he still thought it suited her. Say so at L203 (replacing "I had said seemed like her"), and at L209 identify the green record as hers, which he brought back. Then L173's "your" can stand. Option (b) keeps the whole-record echo cleanest and shows he is still casting her, but either works if it's applied consistently.

### C2 · Minor · Small staging slips

- Where: L71 (hallway), L151 (her dancing), L253 (exit sentence), L279/L303 (final dance)
- Text:
> Suzette had made it two doors away.
> The silver articulations in her ankles made tiny adjustments above the stiff shafts of her boots.
> White go-go boots came almost to her knees.
> The exit was close enough that I could see the wall box holding her railgun and the neat white toes of the boots she had planted on the rug.
> We stood side by side instead.
> Suzette's white boots landed on either side of my socks
- Problem: She lives next door, so "two doors away" puts her past her own door. Her boots reach almost to the knee, so her ankle joints can't show above the shafts. The L253 sentence ties his view of the exit (the wall box beside the door) to her boots in the middle of the rug, which blurs where everyone is standing. In the final image her boots land astride his feet, although the step she taught him has them side by side, so the reader has to guess that she turned and hopped.
- Fix: "Two doors" → her own door, or give her a reason to be walking past it. Ankles → knees, or whatever joint shows above a knee-high boot. Split L253 so the exit and her position are separate observations (and fold in D2's visible move). At L303 add the few words that show she turned to face him before she landed.
