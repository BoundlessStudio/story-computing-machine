# A Friendly Little Game

The fourth cup had begun to sweat a ring into the bar.

You shifted it back onto its coaster and restored the row: your cup at the end, Gwen's on the right where she could watch the door, Alba's beside it, Felix's nearest the open stretch of counter. Four places, four tasks, everyone where they worked best. If they came through the door in the next twelve minutes, you could still reach the spillway before the river uncovered the lower stones. The old iron gate would open. The recovery crew would get inside. The rest of the fee would be yours by dawn.

If they came in twelve minutes.

River damp had silvered the windows. Wet grit rasped under boots behind you, and every time the door lifted on its leather hinges you looked up. Each time it was a stevedore, a pair of net-menders, a kitchen hand hauling in onions. Never Gwen's narrow shoulders, Alba's canvas medical bag, or Felix ducking under the lintel.

You rubbed a scraped knuckle against your cup. At the bridge, an overloaded cart had slewed sideways and tipped, its shafts blocking the narrow crossing. The driver had been dazed. One wheel had kept turning in the air. Gwen had called for rope; Felix had put a shoulder under the axle; Alba had crouched beside the driver. They had said they would handle it and follow.

You had squeezed past with the gate key knocking against your hip. Someone had to protect the low-water window.

The key was still there, heavy in the inner pocket of your coat. You checked it without seeming to check it.

A stool scraped beside you.

The hooded stranger had come from a rear booth, not through the door. They sat close enough that the wet hem of their cloak brushed your boot. Beneath the hood there was only an ordinary chin, a thin mouth, and skin made sallow by the bar lamps.

They studied the four cups. Then the door. Then the hand resting over your inner pocket.

“While we wait for the rest of your party to arrive, would you like to partake in a friendly little game?”

“No.”

“A decisive answer.” Their hands emerged from the cloak, bare and clean. “You must be the one who makes the plans.”

The door opened. A gust stirred the lamps. A man with a basket of eels came in.

“I'm waiting for people,” you said.

“So I observed.”

The stranger set a small leather purse on the bar. It landed with the dense sound of enough coin to replace the rope and oil you had bought with the client's advance, perhaps enough to cover meals besides. They did not untie it.

“Three predictions,” they said. “Surely a planner knows what their own party will do.”

That was when you should have picked up the key and gone back to the bridge.

Instead you said, “What predictions?”

The stranger laid down a shallow wooden rack and three thumb-sized pieces of dark, salt-glazed ceramic. Each piece was blank. They placed one in front of you.

“Before each companion arrives, you will tell me that person's first meaningful choice upon entering. You may define it narrowly. For ten breaths, you will give no instruction. If the choice matches your forecast, the point is yours. If it does not, the point is mine.”

“Who decides what counts?”

“Everyone present. Any uncertainty counts against my claim.”

“And the stakes?”

The stranger nudged the purse with one pale nail. “Two matches, and this pays for your party's drinks as well as your trouble. Two misses, and you surrender one useful thing brought to this table for tonight's work, chosen by you. The third turn is played regardless. It completes the account.”

You thought of the spare wedges in Felix's pack, Gwen's rolls of survey charcoal, Alba's excess bandage. Things used in common and replaced from the common purse. None worth half the coins before you.

“I can stop?”

“Before the first piece, freely. After a forecast is set, stopping concedes the game and the same forfeit. Nothing here compels you to pay.” The stranger tapped the ceramic. “Nor do the pieces compel your friends. They hold only what I put into them.”

“How far?”

The thin mouth moved, almost a smile. “Twelve paces, if I keep a hand upon the rack or a piece. Farther, or without my touch, the marks cool. The witnesses may remember the score if the pottery does not.”

No prophecy, then. No trap hidden in a speaking board. A little heat-work, a purse, and a boast dressed as a wager. Gwen would laugh at you for hesitating.

You put your thumb on the first piece. It was cold and slightly rough.

“Do you accept?” the stranger asked.

“Yes.”

They placed a forefinger on the opposite edge. Warmth entered the ceramic between you. Fine orange lines followed the stranger's fingertip, branching over the glaze in letters too small to read from a distance. Their nail bed blanched.

“Gwen,” you said. “She'll come in, report the cause of the delay before anything else, then take the right-hand stool. She always takes the position with the clearest exits.”

The lines fixed into a compressed record: GWEN—REPORT FIRST—RIGHT STOOL.

“Always?”

“When we're working.”

“Does Gwen ever choose another seat?”

“Not if she has looked at the room properly.”

The stranger stopped asking. They slid the warm piece into the first slot.

Three minutes later the door struck the wall.

Gwen came in sideways against the gust, rain darkening one shoulder and bridge mud striped to her knees. You began to stand.

She passed you.

“Hot water and whatever food travels,” she told the bartender. She put down two small coins. “Send it to the driver at the bridge. Has anyone from here gone back that way?”

“My kitchen hand can go when the bread comes out.” The bartender glanced toward the oven door. “Five minutes.”

“Make it four.” Gwen stayed on her feet, watching until the coins disappeared into the till.

The stranger touched the first piece. The heat-writing brightened. You did not need the repeated words to know the score.

“She reported the delay,” you said. “The request tells us the bridge is still blocked.”

“I told the bartender what the driver needed,” Gwen said. She looked from the stranger's fingers to her name glowing on the ceramic. “Why is that there?”

“A prediction. First meaningful choice.”

“Mine?”

“A small game while we waited.”

Gwen picked up the cup you had set at the right-hand stool. She moved it to the middle of the bar and remained standing. “You left before we had the first rope fixed.”

“You said you could handle it.”

“We did handle it.”

The stranger turned the piece so the words faced Gwen. “The forecast was a report offered first to the player, followed by that seat. I claim a miss.”

“I was about to sit,” Gwen said, though she plainly had not been.

For one bright second you thought she meant to help you.

Then she said, “Now I'm not.” She looked at the other two blank pieces. “What else have you promised?”

“One tool, if I lose. I choose it.”

“Whose tool?”

“A useful thing brought for the work. The wording was clear.” You heard the mistake as soon as you repeated it, but the purse sat within reach and two rounds remained. Alba would come through that door and see your hand. That was as reliable as low water.

You pressed your thumb to the second piece.

Gwen caught your wrist without touching the ceramic. “Don't.”

“If I stop, I lose now.”

“You've already lost one point.”

“Which is why I need the next two.”

The stranger waited. They did not urge you. That courtesy was worse than urging.

“Alba will see my knuckles,” you said. “They'll take my hand and start cleaning it before they ask what happened.”

“Take it?” Gwen asked.

“Alba always treats what's in front of them.”

“That's not what I asked.”

You adjusted the forecast because precision, at least, was still yours. “Alba will reach for my hand, and begin treatment before asking how it happened.”

The stranger's fingertip whitened as the second piece warmed. ALBA—REACH—TREAT BEFORE QUESTION. When they fitted it into the rack, a tremor ran through their index finger and stopped.

Gwen saw it. So did you.

“They're doing that,” Gwen said quietly. “Not the stones.”

“Pieces,” the stranger corrected. “Salt-glazed.”

The door opened before Gwen could answer.

Alba stepped in with their medical bag hugged high against the rain. The knees of their trousers were soaked. They paused just inside, taking in Gwen standing, the stranger's hood, your hand pinned beneath Gwen's, and the two pieces glowing between you.

Alba came closer. Their gaze settled on the red scrape across your knuckles. They unfastened the top flap of the bag, then stopped with both hands on the buckle.

“May I see?”

The hand was already halfway toward them before you understood that they were waiting. They had seen. They had opened the bag. They had not touched you.

Alba's eyes lifted from the scrape to your face.

Say yes, and the stranger could call it close. Say nothing, and ten breaths would pass. The stupidest part was how badly you wanted Alba to stop asking and simply become right.

“Not now,” you said.

“All right.” Alba buckled the bag again. “The driver is awake. Shoulder bruised, no break I could find. Someone local took over.” Then they turned to the rack. “What powers those marks?”

The stranger laid two fingers on the second piece. Its writing flared bright enough to shine on the damp leather of Alba's bag.

“A forecast freely recorded,” they said. “A clear miss.”

“Freely?” Gwen said.

“The recording was.”

It was an exact answer, and not an honest one.

Alba did not touch the ceramic. “Lift your other hand from the rack.”

“That is not a move in the game.”

“Then it costs you nothing.”

The stranger regarded Alba for a moment. They lifted the hand.

At once the first piece dimmed. On the second, the word ALBA blurred at the edges. When the stranger put one trembling finger back against the wood, heat gathered again, slow and uneven.

“The pieces hold the pattern,” Alba said. “You're keeping it warm.”

“The marks are mine.”

“Do they make anyone do what was said?”

“No.”

Alba nodded once. It was not approval. “Then don't put my hand near them.”

“The game is decided,” Gwen said. “Pack it up.”

“The score is decided,” the stranger replied. “The third turn completes the agreed proof and clarifies the available forfeit. The player may stop, of course.”

Stop, and the stranger would point to the two warm failures and choose the story of what they meant. Finish, and Felix would prove that there was a difference between a plan and a trick. More important, the exact third result might narrow useful thing to something cheap.

You put your thumb on the last blank piece.

Alba let out a tired breath. Gwen released your wrist as if it had become someone else's problem.

“Felix will see a hooded stranger beside me,” you said. “He'll put a hand on his knife and wait for my order.”

The stranger's pale fingertip rested opposite yours.

“Don't use that,” Gwen said.

“Use what?”

“What happened in the last bar. He has spent two years making sure it doesn't happen again.”

“Which means he knows when to wait.”

“Does Felix know that's the part you gave him?” Alba asked.

“He carries the knife.”

No one answered. The stranger drew the heat-lines without looking down. FELIX—HAND TO KNIFE—WAIT FOR ORDER. Their hand shook badly now, three pieces drawing color from the fingers that fed them.

The door lifted and stayed open on Felix's shoulder.

He carried a compact lifting jack under one arm. Grease blackened both sleeves, and rain ran from his hair down the sides of his face. He lowered the jack beside the door rather than bring its iron feet across the clean floorboards.

Then he read the room.

Gwen stood with her folded survey tucked under one elbow. Alba kept a full arm's length from the rack. You were rigid on the stool. The stranger sat close enough to touch your coat. Felix's right hand moved toward his belt.

There. You knew him.

His fingers closed on the hook above the sheath. His gaze found yours and stayed there. Whatever he saw made his mouth flatten.

Slowly, he unhooked the sheathed knife from his belt. He walked past you and laid it at the far end of the bar, beyond the cups, beyond anyone's easy reach.

“What do you want me to do?” he asked.

You had an answer ready. Get the knife. Stand by the door. Make this person leave.

Felix waited for what you wanted, not for an order he had already accepted.

“Nothing,” you said.

The stranger touched the third piece. Its letters burned steadily, preserving every word you had supplied.

“He put his hand to the knife,” you said.

“To remove it,” Alba said.

“He waited.”

“He asked,” Gwen said. “You answered.”

The stranger turned the piece beneath one drained-white finger. “Your forecast described readiness to use the weapon at your direction. His first choice was to put it beyond you. Any uncertainty counts against my claim, but there is none. A third miss.”

Felix looked at his name in the heat-writing. “Start at the beginning.”

You tried. The bridge, the tide, the offered purse, the three forecasts. The explanation became less sensible with each clipped fact. You left out nothing except how easy it had been to accept the phrase useful thing.

The stranger listened without correcting you. When you finished, they looked at Gwen.

“A survey is useful for tonight's work.”

Their hand moved toward the folded paper under Gwen's arm. Gwen pulled it back and tucked it inside her coat.

“My survey,” she said. “No.”

The stranger inclined their hood toward Alba's bag. “Care is useful.”

Alba put the bag behind their legs. “You may not have the bag. You may not have my hands.”

“A small service would satisfy me. A short walk, a carried parcel, a door opened. The player may choose.” The stranger looked to Felix. “Strength was brought to the table as surely as any tool.”

Felix's hands hung open at his sides. He did not glance toward the knife. “I did not enter your game.” His speech had gone careful and formal, the way it did when anger had to pass through each word before release. “You have no answer from me.”

The bartender set down a cup hard enough to spill foam. “Nor from me. I heard one person make a wager. I heard none of these three offer you a thing.”

The ceramic pieces did not move. They did not brighten toward Gwen's survey or Alba's bag. No hidden hook pulled Felix across the floor. They held only your three predictions, kept hot by the stranger's failing hand.

“You said I chose the forfeit,” you said.

“You do.”

“You let me think you meant my property.”

“I let you decide what your party brought for your work.” The stranger's voice stayed courteous. “You were very specific about their uses.”

Route. Care. Force.

Not the words you had used, exactly. Worse: they were the shapes underneath.

The key pressed against your ribs. You had carried it for six years, bought from the old gate keeper with money from your first independent route. It opened one iron gate, nothing more. Without it, the spillway stayed closed. The mud shelf flooded on the returning water. The client's lost equipment stayed where it was, and the remaining payment vanished with the chance to retrieve it.

You took the key from your coat.

Its shaft was as long as your palm, its teeth brown with old rust. Gwen shifted between the stranger and the door. Felix looked once toward his knife and did not move. Alba watched the scraped hand close around the iron.

You placed the key beside the three pieces.

“Mine.”

The stranger's fingers left the rack and closed on the key. The heat-writing began to fade at once.

“It qualifies,” they said.

“You knew I had it.”

“At the bridge, it swung from your coat when you climbed past the axle. You hid it, then guarded that pocket while the others took hold of the cart.”

There had been no glimpse of the future. Only the four cups, the turning wheel, and your own hand giving away what mattered.

The stranger swept the cooling pieces into the rack and put it inside the cloak. The purse followed. When they rose, Gwen had already moved into the path to the door. Felix could have reached it in two strides. Alba's voice, quiet behind you, said, “That bargain was built crooked.”

It had been. The stranger had shown you the stake in pieces, each harmless enough to accept, and saved the ugliest reading for after refusal had acquired a price. Nothing in the cooling pottery made that fair.

Nothing in it made the key jump from your hand, either.

“Let them go,” you said. “The key was mine.”

Gwen did not move for you. She looked at Felix. Felix looked at Alba. Then Gwen stepped aside because she chose to, and the others left the doorway open.

The stranger went out into the river damp. The hood passed the silvered window and was gone among the quay lamps.

For a while, nobody sat.

Beyond the glass, the river drew down the embankment stones toward low water. There was no spare key. Even if you found the gate keeper tonight, no replacement could be cut and hauled to the spillway before the turn. The recovery was over. The unpaid portion of the fee was gone, and the advance already spent on oil and rope would have to come out of your account.

You reached for the cups, meaning to put them back in order. Your hand stopped above Gwen's.

“I should have stayed at the bridge,” you said. “And I shouldn't have made three people into a route, a bandage, and a knife.”

Gwen's mouth tightened. “We said we'd handle the cart. We didn't say it was nothing.”

“I know.”

“You know now.” She drew the right-hand stool away from the bar and sat on it backward, facing the room instead of the door. “Tomorrow's route gets discussed tomorrow.”

Not your route. Not your decision. You nodded.

Alba set the medical bag on a stool of their choosing. “May I see your hand now?”

This time you opened it and offered it across the gap. “Yes.”

Alba cleaned the scrape with warm water from the bar. It stung. They wrapped it without forgiving you, which was not the same as refusing care.

Felix retrieved his knife only after the bandage was tied. He fixed it to his belt, pulled a stool from the far end, and sat where no cup had been waiting for him.

“We should—” you began.

Three faces turned toward you.

The rest of the plan went unsaid. “What do you want to do?”

Felix considered the question. Gwen looked toward the rain. Alba tested the bandage with one finger.

Then, without agreeing on anything larger, they drew the four cups into an uneven ring. Your newly bandaged hand rested open among them, on the bar above the empty place where the key had been.
