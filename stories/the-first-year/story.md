Before there were months, the Cat could walk from winter into summer in an evening, provided she did not let the Bear see her.

The Bear hated the Cat's footsteps in her strawberries. The Cat hated the Bear's noon. Between their countries the Serpent kept a slope of warm stone, and anyone crossing it paid her in secrets. The Fox usually paid with somebody else's.

Elsewhere the Boar broke the earth and the Frog filled the hollows. The Wren made flowers grow wherever she sang. The Salmon's rivers ran toward the sea and, beneath the sea, found their way home. The Spider caught dew, the Wolf hunted beneath a frost that never melted, the Hawk owned the height from which everything could be seen, and the Stag stood among bare trees with the cold caught in his antlers.

They were living spirits. Their shadows went deeper than the shadows of other creatures. The black shape beneath the Wren's feet reached through every flowering branch in her country; the Salmon's shadow lay along the beds of his rivers. A spirit could withdraw into that darkness and come out a hundred hills away. It was how each knew its own land, and how the land knew its sovereign.

Their borders were bitter places. Rain trespassed on ripe fruit. Frost stopped a river. The Hawk stripped the Wren's blossoms from their stems, then said she should have grown them lower. Each wanted the others' countries. None would yield its own.

The Wheel of Ages came to the place where all twelve borders met.

It lay flat on the ground, a black circle no wider than the Boar was long. Grass bent beneath it. An eye opened in its hub.

“Who owns this place?” it asked.

All twelve answered.

The Wheel listened gravely. Its eye had a gold edge, and each sovereign saw a different country reflected in it. The Bear saw her berries growing beneath every mountain. The Wolf saw frost on the sea. The Wren saw a forest flowering from horizon to horizon, with no Hawk above it.

The Fox saw himself running through all eleven kingdoms without once being caught.

“I can settle this,” the Wheel said.

“And afterward?” asked the Wren.

“Each shall have what it sees.”

“With what?” asked the Boar.

“I have no country. I have no quarrel. Stand about me, and let each tell the others how far its kingdom ought to reach.”

The Stag planted his forefeet at the northern edge. The Salmon came up through a spring. The Spider lowered herself from a thorn. The others took their places around the circle, each facing the eye in which it had seen everything it desired.

The Fox watched the grass.

It was bending inward.

He sprang, but the black ring rose faster. A ridge of warm hide shut around his hind legs. Beneath the twelve sovereigns, the ground opened into a throat.

The Stag thrust his antlers across it. For a moment the circle could not close. Then the Wheel bit down. The sound carried into every country, and the Stag fell without his crown.

The others struck, sang, spat, leaped. The Wheel swallowed their shadows.

It pulled the Wren out of her blossoms and the Salmon out of his rivers. It drew the Wolf's frost through a hole no wider than a tooth. The twelve black roots stretched into spokes, and the Wheel rose upon its edge, taller than the hills. Its hide closed over them.

Inside, each sovereign lay in a hollow of its own shadow. Their roots ran inward, buried in the Wheel's living flesh. Through a single opening in the rim they could see the country they had lost.

The Wheel's eye turned from one to another.

“Everything,” it said. “As promised.”

Then it pulled on the Cat.

***

The Cat had never been carried anywhere.

She struck at the walls until the dark about her was full of falling claws. The Wheel dragged her to the opening and pressed her against it. Her root shone black through the flesh beneath her feet.

Night went over the country.

The Bear's strawberries disappeared beneath stars. In the Frog's country, people carrying baskets home stopped on paths they had walked in daylight a thousand times. The Wren's flowers closed. The Cat could feel every roof and branch beneath her darkness, but she could not set one paw upon them.

“There,” said the Wheel. “Your kingdom.”

She called her darkness back. The hollow tightened. It held her until she put the night out again.

At dawn the Wheel eased its grip and let her lift it. At dusk it squeezed. Night after night the Cat was brought to the opening, and every morning she was hauled a little way back into her cell. The moon grew round above the hills and dwindled to a horn. People burned the wood they had saved for crossing the Wolf's country. Children who had never seen frost began to dream of it.

The Cat made her nights as short as she could. The Wheel made them longer.

When the moon vanished, it shut her hollow and pulled the Serpent forward.

Warmth entered the ground. Ice slid from roofs in great sheets. People came out to find the whole country smelling of stone in sunlight, and they said that the season had changed.

The Serpent pressed her head against the opening. Her hills were there, among all the others. She could have reached them in a breath if the shadow rooted in her belly had stretched one inch farther.

She went still. She loosened her scales. Slowly, with the patience that had made the stones warm before anything else awakened, she slipped out of her skin.

The empty skin lay in the cell.

The Serpent rose new and bright. Her root rose with her. The Wheel laughed until pebbles fell from the hills outside.

It let the empty skin blow through the opening. The people found it stretched across three fields and built their first shrine beneath its glittering scales.

For another waxing and waning moon the Serpent warmed the country. Seeds woke in the earth. Her skin whitened where people touched it for good fortune.

“They think you gave it to them,” the Fox said from the next hollow.

The Serpent turned her new head toward his voice.

“I know.”

***

The Fox went to the opening walking backward.

“You could have asked,” he told the Wheel.

“You could have refused.”

“I still can.”

The walls touched his ribs.

Outside, dusk spread through the country. The Fox drew one last brightness along the western hills, made the path beside a village seem to fork where it did not, sent warm weather over a frozen field and a sharp shower across the Bear's former country. The people learned to hesitate before believing an evening sky.

While the Wheel watched his work, the Fox left an afterimage at the opening and slipped behind it.

He tried the rim first. He got his head and forelegs into the country. Grass brushed his nose. A woman on the path saw a red creature hanging in the air and dropped her firewood.

The Fox stretched toward the earth. His shadow-root drew tight. The Wheel pulled him backward, through his own afterimage, and struck him against the far wall of the hollow.

For three dawns he could not stand. On the fourth he crawled behind the eye.

There, where the Wheel had folded its own hide to shut the trap, a narrow crease made a darkness deeper than his cell. He pushed his nose into it. The eye rolled. It could not see him. The flesh on either side thickened and pressed, but the folded lips bore against one another and left the little space between them untouched.

The Fox tucked his tail over his injured legs.

The Wheel called him.

He said nothing.

It squeezed his empty cell.

He watched the walls meet.

For the first time since the borders had closed, the Fox slept.

He was dragged out by his root when the Wheel wanted dusk again, but between those hours he had somewhere to go.

When the next moon began, the Boar was brought to the opening. The ground of the country split beneath his power. Hard fields turned over. Shoots thrust up beside roads, through floors, between the white scales of the Serpent's shrine.

The Boar saw them and charged.

He did not charge the opening. He drove his tusks into the living wall beside it.

The Wheel screamed. A seam opened beneath the Boar's feet. In it lay his root, a black curve knotted into wet red flesh.

“At last,” he said, and bit it.

His right foreleg thinned. Through it the Fox could see the far wall. Outside, the shoots in a field blackened from their tips downward. A woman knelt among them with both hands full of dead leaves.

The Boar let go.

His leg grew solid again. The dead shoots did not rise.

The Wheel closed the seam halfway, then stopped. It had felt the thinning too. It had almost lost something it meant to keep.

The Boar lay with his snout against the wound.

“That was me,” he said.

No one answered him.

The rain began when the Frog's turn came. It struck dust from the leaves and filled the Boar's furrows. For days people stood in doorways, listening to water that no longer stopped at a border. Wells rose. The dry spring at the meeting of the countries ran again, though the Salmon did not come out of it.

The Frog listened too.

She had filled her mouth with rain and was dropping it, bead by bead, into the seam the Boar had opened. The drops ran along the inner rim. Far away, beyond the Cat's hollow, something tapped.

The Frog leaped toward the sound. The Wheel pulled her root. She landed hard at her own opening. She leaped again, and again she came down where she had begun.

But the drops kept going. One touched the Fox's nose inside his fold.

“Do that again,” he called.

“I have done it twice.”

“The rain.”

She sent a little more through the wound.

The Fox followed it out of his refuge, along a narrow furrow under the cells. He went past the Serpent and heard her breathing. He went past the Boar and smelled the opened earth. When he came around to his own hollow, the Frog was still waiting.

There was a road between them. It was round, and it went nowhere, but it was a road the Wheel had not given them.

The Fox said nothing about the fold behind the eye.

***

The Wren's moon began with flowers.

They came out on bare wood, over the Boar's dead field, in the wet straw of roofs. People woke to petals on their blankets. Orchards blossomed before anyone had known the trees were orchards. The Wren had never sung to so much country.

The Wheel held her at the opening.

“Again,” it said, whenever her song faltered.

The Boar heard her between the walls. He had kept his snout against the wound all through the rain. He was trying to work his root free without biting it, and sometimes he forgot and put his teeth down.

“Stop that,” the Wren told him.

“Sing something else.”

She put her feet against the exposed root and sang the hard little song she had once used to split the shells of seeds.

The Boar lifted his head.

The sound ran down his darkness. It entered the Wheel beneath the eye, and the eye shut.

For one whole beat the grip on the Wren loosened. Her song stopped. Outside, half-open flowers waited motionless, bees suspended above them.

Then the eye opened again.

The Wheel flung the Wren against the wall. It kept her there until she sang, and the flowers opened too far, tearing themselves from their stems. Petals covered the paths so deeply that people could not find their way home.

Later the Fox came along the rain-road.

“Do it again,” he whispered.

The Wren's breast moved fast.

“Not while it is looking.”

At the end of her moon the Stag's first new tine touched the wall of his hollow.

The Bear followed the flowers with fruit.

Heat thickened over the country. Berries bent branches to the ground. Plums split their skins. Children came home with purple hands and no hunger. The Bear smelled all of it through the opening, and the Wheel let her smell it.

It would not let her eat.

“You have fat enough,” it said.

She watched a boy climb into a tree that had once grown in her country. He filled his shirt with little yellow pears.

That night, instead of sending another wave of heat across the hills, the Bear put her head upon her paws. She remembered her deepest cave. She remembered the weight of snow above it and the sound of her own breathing beneath the earth.

Sleep went down her root. The Wheel slowed; its eye sagged. Its great body settled a little into the land. In their hollows the twelve felt the pull slacken, and the Bear slept more heavily.

“Wake,” said the Wheel.

She let a long breath out through her nose.

The Wheel seized the Wolf's root and dragged him beside her at the opening. His frost tore across the Bear's hot country. Pears froze in the boy's shirt. Branches broke under fruit suddenly hard as stones. The boy got down from the tree and could not feel his hands.

The Bear woke.

The Wheel's eye was bright above her.

“Lie down again.”

She stood until her moon was done.

From her hollow the Cat said, “One at a time.”

The Wheel turned its eye toward her.

“You will take one of us at a time,” she said. “And when the moon is gone, you will turn.”

“Will I?” said the Wheel.

It looked back at the Bear, who could still smell the frozen pears.

The Salmon came to rivers filmed with the frost that should never have been there. Beneath it, the fish of the country were gathering to return. Their bodies shone red and silver in the dark water. They struck the ice from below.

The Wheel forced the Salmon's root toward the opening. Water rose about his cell. He faced it, as he had always faced the current, and drove upstream.

Outside, fish broke through the ice. They climbed falls that had never before known their return. Along the banks, people ran beside them shouting.

Inside, the black flow in the Salmon's root began to run backward.

The Boar's wound opened again. Dark junctions rose through the flesh, twelve roots crossing near the hub, each pulled toward the same narrow throat.

The Wheel snapped its eye toward the Salmon. He drove harder.

For a moment the Wren could see the Cat through the opened flesh. The Cat was lying on her side. One ear moved.

Then the Wheel pulled the Salmon out of the water and held him above his own river until he could no longer struggle.

It put him back before he died.

The junctions sank. A little black water remained in the Boar's wound.

That night the Spider came down into it.

***

The Spider's moon left dew on everything.

At dawn every field carried a thousand small reflections of the sky. Mist lay between houses. Travelers stopped at familiar turnings and saw white roads where there had been no roads before. Webs stretched between the tines of the Stag's growing antlers.

Inside the Wheel, beads of water settled along the buried roots. The Spider followed them through the wound.

“There are twelve,” she told the Fox.

“I had counted.”

“You counted prisoners.”

She laid a foot against his root.

The Fox felt the foot inside his own chest.

He drew back.

“Be still.”

She fastened a thread. Then she went to the next root, letting the mist conceal the silver line behind her.

The Fox waited until she had gone and crept back to his fold. Through the thread he felt the Spider pause at the Serpent. He felt the Wren's quick heart. When the Boar shifted in his sleep, the Fox's foreleg ached.

He lay very still in the only place the Wheel could not reach him.

When the Wolf's moon came, the frost returned properly. It silvered the fields each morning and hardened the paths beneath people's feet. The fruit the Wheel had frozen in the Bear's reign still hung on the boughs. Deer came down from the high country to find what they could beneath it.

The Wolf smelled them.

The Wheel held him at the opening and made him call.

Packs answered from every part of the country. Their howls went through doors and into the sleep of children. The Wheel drew the Wolf back and let him listen to the hunt.

The Spider was halfway around the roots when the wound began to close.

The Wolf came to it along the rain-road. He put his jaws between the closing lips of flesh.

The Wheel stopped calling for him. It pressed.

The Spider's thread trembled in the Fox's chest. He felt the pressure on the Wolf's teeth, felt the animal hold, and then the thread went on.

For the rest of that moon the Wolf could not howl. Frost came in silence. Outside, the packs hunted without him.

By the time the Hawk was pulled to the opening, the Spider had passed every root but the Cat's. She could go no farther. The way ahead folded inward behind the hub, and whenever she followed it she came back to the same place.

The Hawk spread her wings.

Wind stripped the trees. It tore the last fruit down and drove it against walls. People bound their doors shut. Up on the hills, the shrines built under the Serpent's skin came apart and went flapping across the country in pale pieces.

The Hawk rose inside the ring.

Her shadow-root stretched beneath her, and the Wheel pulled. She rose higher, straining against it, while the whole country bent beneath her wind. From above she could see the twelve hollows, the wound held open in the Wolf's jaws, and the silver thread trembling between them.

She could see behind the eye.

The Fox looked up from his fold. The Hawk looked down at him.

“There,” she said.

The Spider lifted her forelegs.

The Fox shook his head once.

The Hawk's root jerked. She fell, caught herself on the rim, and rose again. Roofs lifted outside. The Spider waited, with the loose end held between her feet. Somewhere behind the walls the Wolf's teeth scraped against one another.

The Fox came out.

He took the thread in his mouth. It tasted of dew and the other prisoners. He carried it into the fold, across the little hollow where his body had fitted, and out through the other side toward the Cat.

As he drew it taut, the silver line propped the folded lips apart. The eye moved, and a thin gold light entered his refuge.

The Wheel looked into the place where it had not been able to see him. Flesh reached after the thread, found the hidden hollow, and pressed it flat.

The Fox kept going. He laid the thread against the Cat's root.

For the first time all twelve felt the same beat of the Wheel's heart.

***

The Stag's moon came with bare boughs and deep cold.

Across the country, people cut branches to burn. They had learned that a season would end, but they did not know what would follow this one. They stacked wood against the houses and took the old and the small into the warmest rooms.

The Wheel drew the Stag to the opening.

His new antlers had grown through eleven moons of confinement. He had turned his head a little each day to keep them from the walls. Now their branches lay back along his shoulders, pale and crowded, twelve long tines ending in points the Wheel had never seen.

The moon grew round and thinned. Each dawn the Stag stood at the opening and gave the country his cold. Each night he turned his crown another little way toward the wound. Beyond the rim, people counted the wood left in their houses. Inside, the Wren counted the days upon the thread.

On the last day, when only a sliver of moon remained, the Wheel felt a tine touch its buried flesh.

The next dawn came without a moon. The Cat waited to be pulled forward. Instead the Wheel tightened its hold on the Stag, driving cold deeper into the country. Frost stopped the smoke in chimneys. People brought their last wood in from the stacks.

“My moon is done,” the Stag said.

“This one pleases me. Lower your head,” it told him.

He lowered it.

Through the thread, the Wren gave one small note. The Cat put her darkness into the hub, and the Wheel's eye vanished beneath it. Outside, noon remained noon. Inside, the creature struck blind at its own walls.

The Bear sent down sleep. The turning body staggered. The pull on the roots slackened, and the Salmon drove against it. Blackness flowed backward through the flesh. As the junctions rose into the wound held between the Wolf's jaws, the Stag thrust his antlers among them.

The Wheel woke fully.

It pulled on the Cat. The Stag turned his head, and a tine caught her root. It pulled on the Wolf; another tine lifted beneath his. Root by root the crown opened, holding the twelve apart where the Wheel had drawn them together into its throat.

The Boar put his shoulder beneath the Stag's neck, and the Frog braced herself against his hind feet. The Serpent wound her body around the roots so they could not slip between the tines. The Spider kept the thread tight, and the Hawk watched above them through the darkness. The Fox stood against the Cat and felt the Wheel trying to find her.

“Let go,” it said.

No one did.

The Stag lifted his head. The Wheel turned backward.

Its lower rim dragged across the hills. People fell in their houses. Snow slid from the mountains. At the old meeting of the twelve borders, a black furrow opened in the ground.

“Forward,” the Cat said.

The Salmon ceased his struggle. The Bear let a little sleep leave her. The Stag shifted his crown. Together the prisoners made their captor turn.

It moved one hollow's breadth and stopped.

The Stag's root fell slack. For the first time in a moon he could lift his head without sending cold into the country. He was still inside the walls.

The Cat's opening faced the country.

Behind her, the Stag stood trembling. His crown was wedged deep among the roots. He lowered his head, pulled once, and left the antlers there.

The Wheel seized his root and tried to drag it beside the Cat's.

Bone caught against bone. The crown held the two roots apart. The creature strained until every prisoner felt its fury; then it eased before it tore the living darkness it had taken such care to own.

The Wolf let the wound close upon the shed antlers.

For a while there was no sound inside the Wheel but his breathing.

***

The Cat's nights returned.

People recognized them. They brought out lamps and put fresh wood on their fires. In the country that had once been twelve countries, they counted back through the changing moons. They named each for the creature whose work they had seen, and when they came to the Cat again they understood that the changes would return.

They called each reign a month, and the completed round a year.

The Wheel heard them through the opening.

It held the Cat against the rim and squeezed her root. Night deepened. Frost took the edges of the lamps. At the hour when dawn ought to have come, a child went to a door and found the stars still there.

“A little longer,” the Wheel told the Cat.

She felt its heart through the thread. She felt the Wren begin to count the beat, and the Bear stir in her hollow. The Salmon turned toward the current. The Fox, with nowhere left to hide, stood against the wall of her cell.

The Cat took the darkness from the sky and put it into the Wheel's eye.

Dawn reached the hills.

The Wheel crushed her against the rim. The Fox felt her bones bend. It held her there while, outside, people opened their doors and saw the light. Then it eased its grip just enough to keep her alive.

The lamps went out, one by one. Children were lifted to windows. In the orchards, the fruit killed in the Bear's moon lay frozen beneath the trees, and beyond them smoke rose straight from a thousand roofs.

The Cat did not cry out. She could feel all eleven others waiting for her through the Wheel's flesh.

At dusk her root tightened.

The Wheel turned its blind eye toward her.

“Again,” it said.
