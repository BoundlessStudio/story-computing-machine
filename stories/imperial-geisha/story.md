# The Last Courtesy

Iori had been given two pairs of shoes, black for mourning and gold for the throne, and when I went in to show her the bow she had one of each on. She pushed the black shoe out from under the hem of her coat. “This one pinches. They said to practice in the others, so I put one on to see if my feet got any different.”

At the Open Lotus, a girl could be fitted for her father’s funeral and her own coronation by the same woman on the same stool. Fusa, our shoemaker, had cut my first pair. At my first performance a man from one of the steel houses sat with his mouth open through the whole third dance because Tsune had killed him in the second and his wife was too polite to wake him. I approved almost everything about the photograph of that evening until I saw that I was turning my right foot out.

I took Iori to the hinge of the great outer petal and set the toe of her gold shoe against the little depression in the floor. She would have to wait there when the flower opened until she saw me make room. Then I showed her how the samurai would fold at the knee. I did it slowly so she could see my hand go down for balance and my white shin plates slip past each other by a hair. The mechanical samurai had already set their feet at the stage apron. Their low hum came through the boards into my wrist. Resin was sweating out of a knot beside my hand. Pink light came in at the edges of the closed lotus petals and turned violet where it met the blue under my ribs. Iori watched that point and tried to hold her breath, which was her idea of how a person could stay still that long.

“Will they kneel like that for me?”

“As soon as I give them your name. Look how far down I go. Then they’ll get up and stay with you.”

Iori leaned over her gold toe to try to see through the petals. “Would they do what I told them, or would they ask Mother’s people first?” I caught her by the cuff where the three gold bars of her mother’s house were sewn.

“Kase will give them the orders until you’re old enough. Bring your toe back to the mark, please. It should sit there in the dark, too.”

“Kase can tell the samurai I’m going to stay up. Father has seven bells tonight and I only got to hear one yesterday. The woman who brought me my supper went back for a spoon and by the time I got my spoon there was only one left.” Iori kicked off the black shoe and undid the buckle of the gold one, holding her hair out of her mouth. “If it isn’t allowed you can tell me here. I can keep it to myself.”

It was the first time she had spoken to me as though I were someone who had a door of her own and could shut it. I touched the board beside me. “You can stay with me until the last bell.” I was still on the floor when Tsune came in. She had a way of walking onto boards already crowded with people so there was space for her a step before she needed it. In her ceremony whites she could reach the narrow pink gap between the petals without picking up any of their color. I had never managed that. My casing took up every scrap of light.

She bent to point out to Iori where she had dropped the other gold shoe. Then she took me down the back stair until we stood below the level of the floor.

“You’ll take her to the far dressing room in a minute, Naho. I thought you could bring her one of the sugared plums. She always finishes those for me. She knows you and you can get her to sit.” She settled the edge of my collar where it had caught against a wire. “When I come to fetch you the girl must be dead. Quietly, so that people can go home believing the emperor’s illness took her too.”

We stood close enough that I could have turned her head between my palms and found the two little scratches I had made when she was teaching me how to hold a partner’s face. I had been the youngest of the Imperial Geisha long after I was tired of anybody calling me young. Tsune still waited for a nod from me when she gave an order, the way she waited for every pupil to find the first beat.

“Let me give her name as planned. I’ve shown her where to stand. She’ll come out if I ask her.”

“Dear heart, the other houses have put their armies on the roofs. When you give the guard to Kase they’ll start shooting before the child has taken her first step.” Tsune took a folded paper from her sleeve. “Here are the seals of every house. They’ve agreed to leave the guard in its posts if the emperor’s line ends.” She had folded the paper through Kase’s seal. One of the three gold bars had cracked where the crease ran through it.

“Does Kase know what you’re asking me to do?”

“Kase wants that regency. I asked its lawyers what they’d do if Iori died before taking the throne. The order about Iori is mine.”

Whenever they renewed the peace, the great corporate houses sent our order dishes in their colors. We had a thousand years of them, shelves of good bowls from companies in cities I had never been allowed to visit. Our makers gave us hands that could carry a bowl of broth through a whole dance without stirring the surface. A man once took mine in both of his and thanked me for the peace, while I counted the seconds left to him. Tsune had put me beside him specially because his daughter would be dining too. I was supposed to have learned something from how little of that evening the girl had had cause to remember.

Upstairs a small shoe scraped against the boards. I had let people talk as if Iori wasn’t in the room all afternoon. The next thing I said, I wanted to be able to say in front of her.

“I’m taking her out of the city,” I said. “I won’t kill her.”

“Then bring her to me, love,” Tsune said. “I can do it for you. When it’s over you can come and say anything you like to my face.”

The first bell struck. Tsune stepped onto the strip of stage the petals left bare and spread both hands to welcome the city. Beyond the samurai at the edge of the apron, the terraces dropped all the way down Fold Street to the tram lights. I took Iori’s warm wrist and told her to bring the black shoes. She managed to get both into one hand. From the back stair I heard Tsune dispatch our sisters as they bowed beside her. Her voice had scarcely more force in it than it took to send for a clean glass.

“Bring me Naho before the last bell. Let her come back walking if she’ll do it.”

***

I took Iori down from the stage into the passage that led out to Fold Street. It was deep enough in the hill for winter to come up through the floor. The smell of cedar held in the folds of the spare curtains, which hung from a rail above the bench where we changed our shoes. At the far end I held up a curtain for Iori to slip behind. I had never seen such small bare feet on those stones. I helped her get both black shoes on and buckled, though she tried to do the second one herself while I was still doing the first.

“Where are we going?”

“The last tram on Fold Street has seats that face the way you’re going. I’m going to see if there’s still room for us. You’ll be out of the wind here.”

“I can go where there’s wind. I want to come while you see.”

I could make it down the terraces and back in the time it would take her to cross the first one. I wanted to tell her just that much. “There’ll be people out there looking for me, Iori. Keep behind the curtain until you hear me say your name. Then we’ll go together.”

She drew a blue length of it across her lap. “Will you be back by the third bell? After three there’s four more.”

“By the third bell,” I said, with no idea how much time they meant to leave between them.

Down the middle of Fold Street ran a row of brass studs to keep room for the trams. When the street got full, people balanced on the studs to see how close they could get to the passing cars. The parents who called for their children to get down were the first to go up on the brass when a performance began. I could hear them calling now over the drum that marked the procession’s time. I slipped outside in the smell of singed plum sugar from the offering stalls and went down the hill along the tram lane.

The temple signs of Karusen could shine clear across a street on the nights we mourned a sovereign. The Temple of the Twenty Vows lit one vow after another in letters high enough to cover the tenements opposite. My shoulder wires went blue in the first vow and pink in the next. Farther downhill Kase’s three gold bars curled under the statue of the Birdkeeper. They’d put up the bars under every Birdkeeper in Karusen when they bought the trams. You could find the steps by the spread wings on her hands in weather too thick to see the lettering.

From the public stair above the platform I could see the white lights of the tram coming to take the night workers out past the gates. There were still people on the platform who cared about that tram more than the imperial succession. One woman tried to read the board through the top half of a cake she was eating. I was deciding how much I could give her to bring a little girl down from the temple without making her wonder why I’d picked her. I had got that far before I thought what it might be like for her to have my sisters knock on her door later. I wanted a hand from the crowd that could be explained afterward.

The white lights went out. A change travelled down the board, replacing the fares and stops with a suspension of service by order of the court. The tram was still coming. Women stepped forward with their bags. It went straight by, with the temple signs lighting row after row of empty seats, and stopped just short of the lattice the samurai were drawing across the tracks at the wall gate. When I looked along the lit streets running down to the city wall, I could see lacquer shoulder plates at the other gates. The women on the platform began talking about how long it would take to walk home. They were still talking as if a samurai had ever opened a closed gate for somebody who wanted to go home.

I heard the second bell while I stood there. I had promised the girl three.

Two of my sisters came down across from me toward the side gate of the platform, with a length of mourning silk between them. One held it high to keep it off the ground while the other bent to address the samurai at the foot of their stair. I could see his waiting face reflected in the white of her cheek. She showed him the folded end of the cloth, and he drew the side gate open as a court sentry does for the women officiating. He shut it again behind them and resumed his watch. The sisters lowered the silk and looked among the people it had screened from them.

My knee ticked as I turned back toward the street. Tsune had once heard it from the far side of an orchestra and made me start a whole dance over. All the women who had worked in our hall that year knew it. One of my sisters lifted her chin and passed her end of the silk to the other. She came across the platform for the public stair where I stood, so fast that the woman with the cake turned right around in the breeze she left without seeing whom to blame.

I ran up to Fold Street. The rival companies’ troops were on the roof of the Twenty Vows, just visible when the lettering went white. By the time I reached the first terrace my sister was at my shoulder. She touched my arm so lightly she might have been testing a sleeve for fit, and by the time I felt her fingers close on my elbow we were passing a dark stall window in the posture of two women greeting each other. My face in the window was very pale, with all the red on it gathered at the mouth. The pink flower was coming loose from the crown of my black hair.

The first row of mourners raised their paper lamps and bowed to us. My sister had to return the bow in full view of the street. Her fingers were still on my arm as she bent. I pulled until a crack ran all the way around my elbow and I felt the cold at the joint. She kept hold of the white plate. I was halfway down in my own bow when the next vow lit up pink and put the same color on my face as it did on the paper and all the faces above it. I came up between two swaying lamps and went in the door off Fold Street. The flowers in my hair dragged against the lintel. I was still close enough to the street to hear someone ask my sister if she had dropped something.

The third bell began as I came down to the passage. I had kept Iori’s time. The last bell would open the stage, and if I got her up to the apron I could put the guard between her and my sisters. I’d spent half the afternoon learning how long to pause after I gave her name, so every Kase officer on the terraces would have time to stand.

***

“Iori,” I called down the passage. My bare elbow was still cold from the street. I cradled it in my other hand while the curtain moved. She came out with her fist closed over the cloth and took as long to smile as if she were making sure it was me from the shoes upward.

Somebody was coming down from the stage. I could tell it was Tsune from the way the sound of her feet stopped at the edge of a step before the rest of her came into view. That pause had taken me weeks. “I heard your knee on the way in, Naho,” she said. She came down another step and caught sight of Iori at the end of the bench.

The street door opened behind me. The sister from the platform stood on the top step with the white plate from my elbow cupped in one hand. “Keep the door,” Tsune told her. “I’ll see to Naho myself.” My sister set the plate on the step and went back outside. Iori was still holding the curtain when the door shut.

“It’s very cold down here, dear,” Tsune said to Iori. “Come as far as the top and feel how warm the boards are tonight.”

Iori looked at me. “Are we still seeing if we can get on?”

“The trams have stopped.” I put my hand down between hers and the curtain. She let go and came against my side. I turned the bare iron at my elbow out of the way of her coat. “Walk us through the wall at the tram gate, Tsune. The samurai shut it on the court’s word, and you speak for the court. Let the last bell go by without a name. The guard can keep its posts and we can ask the houses for one more night.”

“Kase will want the girl wherever you take her. They’ll look for her to give her a crown and the other houses will look for her to put her in the ground. If you’re very good at hiding, the other houses may only have to send you word of what they have done to somebody who once gave you a room.” Tsune lowered herself onto the step without using a hand. Her knees made no sound. “All that can begin in the morning, if we leave somebody to inherit. I want you to be able to go across a room in any of their cities without hearing the people in it get up and run.”

“There’s somebody left,” said Iori. “I can sit in Father’s chair. He showed me the gold letters on the back.” She worked the buckle loose on one of her shoes with a fingernail.

Tsune drew her folded coat in to make room beside her. “Come and sit here, Iori. It’s only a few steps. I’ve sat on these stairs with Naho after nearly every performance she’s given.”

“Would you tell me what for before I go up? The man in Father’s room sent me for a blanket. When I came back with it he took it in, and then a woman came out and handed it back to me. I had to go right across the palace twice for nothing.”

I knew how to send Iori up to sit beside Tsune in the same voice I’d used to tell her to bring her toe back to the mark. My sister was waiting with both palms flat on her knees, the way she did when there was to be no audience. I could have brought Iori straight into those hands.

“Tsune came down to kill you,” I said. Iori’s fingers came open against mine, and I went on because I had heard people try to make things clear for her all day by stopping in the middle of what they had to say. “I came back for you because I want to take you upstairs and say who you are, so the samurai will come and stand around you.”

The fourth bell shook the quiet out of the staircase. Iori drew in a short, badly made breath. I held her while she got a better one. When I looked up again Tsune was sitting square on the step with her head up, ready for someone to take her picture.

“I can wait here with her,” Tsune said to me. “She won’t need to go through another room. I can do it here and take her upstairs myself. You can go and talk to the people on the terraces, and I would like you to, because they think you’re lovely and you make them feel that they’ve come to see a very good thing.”

Tsune reached for her coat where it lay beside her. “Naho, Fusa has brought her whole family up to the fourth terrace. I saw them taking blankets to sit on, the good red ones she puts out when her sons come in from the country. Do you remember the night one of those boys got caught in the foundry riots? I was with her outside the shop. She had got his coat off its hook and for three hours she kept it folded in her arms, the way you carry somebody who’s fallen asleep. I said I would go and get him. I found him in the foundry infirmary after the surgeon took off his right foot. When I carried him up to her door his mother wrapped his coat around the left one. That came of shooting in one street. The men on the roofs will fire into their own city tonight to stop Kase taking it. The people who make their shoes are sitting on the steps between them.”

Tsune’s own coat was white that winter, and by the time she got home it was wet through with the first snow of the year. I could remember how long it took our house to get the creases out. I’d wanted it to look as it had done before she went out for him, in case she gave it to me one day. I had been studying what it would be like to be given the things she didn’t need anymore.

“When I started dancing in front of people, I always looked for you at the end,” I said. “I used to think one day you’d see me get it exactly right and we could stop practicing that part. You’re asking me to go up there and get it right again. When you came past with Iori in your arms, I’d be looking at your face to see if you’d noticed me turn my foot out.”

“I’d see you, Naho,” said Tsune. “You wouldn’t have to look for me.”

I was still waiting for Tsune to make the choice easier. She could still do what she’d offered. If I handed her the girl, tomorrow Fusa would be at her stall. I could take her the black shoes to have the backs let out. There would be no one to put them on. There was a relief in the thought of how much of the morning I could get through before I came to that fact again. I looked up at the steps and at Tsune’s two hands. She would have the body carried quietly after the vigil. I could tell people Iori had slept right through her father’s bells. People would come up to me in the street and put both their hands over one of mine.

“If I stood out where you showed me, could they see my feet?” Iori had let go of my hand. She put one shoe down flat and the other at an angle so wide we would have stopped a rehearsal. The shoe with the loose buckle started to slip off and she held it there with her toes. “I can stay there if I have to. Can I keep my feet like they are right now?”

I went down on my knees so close to her that the blue light came up under her chin. She leaned against me, trying to work the heel back where it belonged. The drum on Fold Street stopped. When it began again Iori was still trying. I wanted her to get the shoe on. I could see Tsune looking down on us, keeping the place on the step where she had said the child could sit.

“Keep your feet any way you please,” I said to Iori. Then to my sister, “I’m going to say her name. Please get those people off the terrace, Tsune. There’s still time to get them down the steps.”

Tsune rose. I got Iori past Tsune’s step, all the way up the stair to the space beneath the lotus. The red wiring behind my shoulder had caught on Iori’s coat and she stood quite close while I got her free. You could see the tip of a black shoe through the opening on the apron if you knew where to look. I asked her to stay where the hinge of the petal touched the floor. She touched it once with the toe so she’d be able to find it again.

Tsune came up the stairs after us. When she drew level with me she lifted her hand to my head. I held still to let her set my loose flower in its place. She had put a flower there before my first performance and afterward I had gone to look for it in my hair when the audience was finished, as if I thought the people would have watched it so hard they could have taken it from me. I could feel my sister’s fingers working through the hair at my scalp. The pin had always hurt a little when she set it.

She let her hand fall and glanced at the dark at the head of the stairs. “You can still give me Iori when we bow. Everyone will be watching your face.”

“Iori, wait for me here.” I took my sister’s hand so she couldn’t pass behind me. The tips of her fingers rested cool and light on the back of my hand. She matched my grip exactly.

The last bell rang. Behind me, Iori said, “Seven.” The petals turned and the pink light reached the roofs at the far side of Fold Street. At our feet were the tops of twenty samurai helmets, smooth as the bottoms of good bowls. Beyond them silk offerings hung in rows down among the people. Tsune led into the bow, and with her free hand she reached for Iori.

I stepped between Tsune’s hand and the hinge. She struck my forearm hard enough to take my feet off the floor. I had spent half my life learning the moment at which she would put her weight into a movement, but she had never expected me to stay in its way. I felt the pin she had just set drag at my scalp. My heel came down. The plank gave under it, and I turned Tsune toward the stair as you turn a partner through the open part of a figure. It brought her cheek as close to mine as when she had taught me where to put my hand on a person’s face. Through the bow I saw the two scratches I had left on her temple, which always took longer than the rest of her face to go pink in this light. I held Tsune on the side next to the stair. On my other side Iori had both black shoes down solidly with nothing between her and the apron.

“I want to come now,” she said.

I turned my face straight to Iori. The corner of my mouth kept trembling after I had got the rest of me still. She looked quite hard at me before she went ahead. I let go of Tsune and took the two steps to the apron with Iori, in the time it took the people who were sitting to see there was a girl going out in front of the dancers. I could hear Tsune come after us. Even then I could have lifted Iori up and given her back to my sister.

I gave the name aloud. “I present Iori, daughter of the emperor and your sovereign.”

I held the pause I had practiced. On the first count the two samurai nearest us left their places. They set their lacquered bodies into the space we had just come through and folded at the knee. I felt in the floor the hum I had felt through my wrist when I knelt for Iori. Tsune stopped on the other side of the samurai. She had one hand up in the air where she could have reached the girl. As the shoulder plates came together I saw her turn her head to look down Fold Street. By the third count the Kase officers on the terraces were all on their feet. The far ranks turned out to face the roofs, and three gold bars lit up on their collars all the way down the hill, even on the sentries below the tram platform.

The first shot crossed from the Temple of the Twenty Vows. The silk offerings jumped clear of their stalls and came down out of the light. One of the ranks of samurai turned its weapons on the roof, and in the first noise after that I could hear the tram windows giving way. People were lifting children off the brass studs by their clothes. Where every other face was moving away I saw Tsune go down toward the fourth terrace, her hands at chest height as though she were about to ask somebody to stand.

Iori put her hand in the one that had held my sister. Her fingers found the flesh between the plates. We could see over the shoulders of the guard to the place where the first people had fallen. A corner of one of Fusa’s red blankets lay under an overturned bench. Tsune dug both hands under the bench and lifted it, and Iori and I watched to see who would come out from under it.
