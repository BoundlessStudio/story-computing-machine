# The Last Courtesy

Iori had been given two pairs of shoes, black for mourning and gold for the throne, and when I went in to show her the bow she had one of each on. She pushed the black shoe out from under the hem of her coat. “This one pinches. They said to practice in the others, so I put one on to see if my feet got any different.”

Fusa took the black shoe off. She rested Iori’s bare foot on her own thigh and rubbed her thumb over the place where the leather had left a welt. “There she is. Give her a minute out of that thing, Naho. It’s a long time to be wearing something that hurts and have everybody tell you how fine you look.”

Fusa had cut my first dancing shoes, back when we wore cloth on the boards. I still had the photograph from that evening in the mirror above my dressing table. A man from one of the steel houses sat with his mouth open through the whole third dance because Tsune had killed him in the second and his wife was too polite to wake him. In the picture his wife was looking right at the hollow of his throat. I always looked at my feet. I had turned the right one out.

Fusa pressed the back of the shoe with the heel of her hand before she put it on again. She had brought a red blanket to spread on the fourth terrace when her sons came. One of them still went cold as soon as he sat still, she said, and would sooner walk home all that way than ask the flower sellers for a blanket. I told her which way to sit if she wanted to see Iori come through the petals. Fusa tapped Iori’s foot twice and left us the shoes.

I took Iori to the iron hinge of the great outer petal and set the toe of her gold shoe against the depression in the floor. She would have to wait there when the flower opened until she saw me make room. Then I showed her how the samurai would fold at the knee. I did it slowly so she could see my hand go down for balance and my white shin plates slip past each other by a hair. The samurai were already standing at the edge of the apron. Their lacquer collars were blank. Their low hum came through the boards into my wrist. Resin was sweating out of a knot beside my hand. The petals gave off just enough light at their edges to turn the blue under my ribs to violet. Iori watched that point and tried to hold her breath, which was her idea of how a person could stay still that long.

“Will they kneel like that for me?”

“As soon as I give them your name. Look how far down I go. Then they’ll get up and stay with you.”

Iori leaned over her gold toe to try to see through the petals. “Would they do what I told them, or would they ask Mother’s people first?” I caught her by the cuff where the three gold bars of her mother’s house were sewn.

“Kase will give them the orders until you’re old enough. Bring your toe back to the mark, please. It should sit there in the dark, too.”

“Kase can tell the samurai I’m going to stay up. Father has seven bells tonight and I only got to hear one yesterday. The woman who brought me my supper went back for a spoon and by the time I got my spoon there was only one left.” Iori kicked off the black shoe and undid the buckle of the gold one, holding her hair out of her mouth. “If it isn’t allowed you can tell me here. I can keep it to myself.”

It was the first time she had spoken to me as though I were someone who had a door of her own and could shut it. I touched the board beside me. “You can stay with me until the last bell.” I was still on the floor when Tsune came in. She had a way of walking onto boards already crowded with people so there was space for her a step before she needed it. In her ceremony whites she could reach the narrow pink gap between the petals without picking up any of their color. I had never managed that. My casing took up every scrap of light.

She bent to point out to Iori where she had dropped the other gold shoe. Then she took me down the back stair until we stood below the level of the floor.

“You’ll take her to the far dressing room in a minute, Naho. She always finishes those sugared plums for me. You can give her one and see if she’ll sit.” She settled the edge of my collar where it had caught against a wire. “When I come to fetch you the girl must be dead. Quietly, so that people can go home believing the emperor’s illness took her too.”

We stood close enough that I could have turned her head between my palms and found the two little scratches I had made when she was teaching me how to hold a partner’s face. I had been the youngest of the Imperial Geisha long after I was tired of anybody calling me young. Tsune still waited for a nod from me when she gave an order, the way she waited for every pupil to find the first beat.

“Let me give her name as planned. I’ve shown her where to stand. She’ll come out if I ask her.”

Tsune showed me the roofs from the window on the stair. At the Twenty Vows the first word of the prayer lit a row of men standing higher than the god on her ledge. By the light I could see the steel house’s crest on their banner. The next word picked men out on a second temple, past the cloth stalls. Down on the terraces the red blankets had just begun to go out over the benches. “Dear heart, when you give the guard to Kase, the houses on those roofs will open fire before the child has taken her first step.” Tsune put a folded paper in my hand. “Here are the seals of every house. They’ve agreed to leave the guard in its posts if the emperor’s line ends.” She had folded the paper through Kase’s seal. One of the three gold bars had cracked where the crease ran through it.

“Does Kase know what you’re asking me to do?”

“Kase wants that regency. I asked its lawyers what they’d do if Iori died before taking the throne. The order about Iori is mine.”

Whenever they renewed the peace, the great corporate houses sent our order dishes in their colors. We had a thousand years of them, shelves of good bowls from companies in cities I had never been allowed to visit. Our makers gave us hands that could carry a bowl of broth through a whole dance without stirring the surface. A man once took mine in both of his and thanked me for the peace, while I counted the seconds left to him. Tsune had put me beside him specially because his daughter would be dining too. I had been careful to go on talking to the daughter. I finished saying something about the lamps she could see over the wall while I felt his fingers come loose from mine.

Upstairs a small shoe scraped against the boards. I had let people talk as if Iori wasn’t in the room all afternoon. The next thing I said, I wanted to be able to say in front of her.

“I’m taking her out of the city,” I said. “I won’t kill her.”

“Then bring her to me, love,” Tsune said. “I can do it for you. When it’s over you can come and say anything you like to my face.”

The first bell struck. Tsune stepped onto the strip of stage the petals left bare and spread both hands to welcome the city. Beyond the samurai at the edge of the apron, the terraces dropped all the way down Fold Street to the tram lights. I took Iori’s warm wrist and told her to bring the black shoes. She managed to get both into one hand. From the back stair I heard Tsune dispatch our sisters as they bowed beside her. Her voice had scarcely more force in it than it took to send for a clean glass.

“Close the gates in the court’s name,” she said. “Bring me Naho before the last bell. Let her come back walking if she’ll do it.”

***

I took Iori down from the stage into the passage that led out to Fold Street. It was deep enough in the hill for winter to come up through the floor. The smell of cedar held in the folds of the spare curtains, which hung from a rail above the bench where we changed our shoes. At the far end I held up a curtain for Iori to slip behind. From either stair you could see the front of the bench run right into the cloth. I had never seen such small bare feet on those stones. I helped her get both black shoes on and buckled, though she tried to do the second one herself while I was still doing the first.

“Where are we going?”

“The last tram on Fold Street has seats that face the way you’re going. I’m going to see if there’s still room for us. You’ll be out of the wind here.”

“I can go where there’s wind. I want to come while you see.”

I could make it down the terraces and back in the time it would take her to cross the first one. I wanted to tell her just that much. “There’ll be people out there looking for me, Iori. Keep behind the curtain until you hear me say your name. Then we’ll go together.”

She drew a blue length of it across her lap. “Will you be back by the third bell? After three there’s four more.”

“By the third bell,” I said, with no idea how much time they meant to leave between them.

Down the middle of Fold Street ran a row of brass studs to keep room for the trams. When the street got full, people balanced on the studs to see how close they could get to the passing cars. The parents who called for their children to get down were the first to go up on the brass when a performance began. I could hear them calling now over the drum that marked the procession’s time. I slipped outside in the smell of singed plum sugar from the offering stalls and went down the hill along the tram lane.

The temple signs of Karusen could shine clear across a street on the nights we mourned a sovereign. The Temple of the Twenty Vows lit one vow after another in letters high enough to cover the tenements opposite. There was a breath of dark between the lines. In it I could see which rooms still had their lamps on and the people pressed against the glass they had forgotten to shutter. Farther downhill Kase’s three gold bars curled under the statue of the Birdkeeper. You could find the station steps by the spread wings on her hands in weather too thick to see the lettering.

From the public stair above the platform I could see the white lights of the tram coming to take the night workers out past the gates. One woman tried to read the board through the top half of a cake she was eating. I thought of paying her to fetch Iori from the temple. I was still trying to imagine what she could tell my sisters if they came for her when the lights went out.

The court’s order closing the gates came down the board where the fares and stops had been. The tram was still coming. Women stepped forward with their bags. It went straight by, with the temple signs lighting row after row of empty seats, and stopped short of the lattice at the wall gate. A pair of samurai in blank lacquer took the ends of the lattice at their shoulders and walked it shut across the tracks. The women began talking about how long it would take to walk home.

I heard the second bell while I stood there. I had promised the girl three.

Two of my sisters came down the stair opposite mine, holding a length of mourning silk between them. One took it high enough for the other to bow to the samurai at the side gate. I could see his waiting face reflected in the white of her cheek. He let them onto the platform as women of the court. The sisters lowered the silk and looked among the people it had screened from them.

My knee ticked as I turned back toward the street. The first time it gave way in rehearsal, Sae caught my whole weight without letting a single person in the room see me fall. She stayed after we had put out the stage lights because I would only try it again in the dark. I saw her lift her chin at the sound and pass her end of the mourning silk to our other sister, who drew it back to make a space for her. Sae came across the platform for the public stair, so fast that the woman with the cake turned right around in the breeze she left without seeing whom to blame.

I ran up to Fold Street. The rival companies’ troops were on the roof of the Twenty Vows, just visible when the lettering went white. By the time I reached the first terrace Sae was at my shoulder. She touched my arm so lightly she might have been testing a sleeve for fit, and by the time I felt her fingers close on my elbow we were passing a dark stall window in the posture of two women greeting each other. My face in the window was very pale, with all the red on it gathered at the mouth. The pink flower was coming loose from the crown of my black hair.

“Stay on my arm and we’ll go back up,” Sae said. “Everybody who saw you will think you missed your cue. Tell me where the girl’s waiting and I’ll bring you both in.”

I had to look at her in the glass to see whether she would come the rest of the way with me. She had the patient look she wore when I kept missing a step and she had worked out why. “I’m the one you came for, Sae. Leave Iori where she is.”

“I can bring you back in one piece, Naho. I want to. Tell me where she is.”

I watched our two pale faces go blue and then dark. From farther down Fold Street I could hear the drum calling the mourners up to the bier. I kept quiet between one beat and the next. When the next word of the vow came across the glass she had crushed the casing at my right elbow. I felt the plate give all at once and something warmer than the sleeve run down to my wrist. In her face I could see the small attention she had once given my bad knee when she kept me standing on it. A man ducked around us with his lamp held high. He thanked us for letting him pass.

The first row of mourners raised their paper lamps and bowed. Sae had to return the bow in full view of the street. Her fingers were still on my arm as she bent. I pulled clear and left the white plate in her hand with a wet strip of me hanging from it. I was halfway down in my own bow when the next vow lit up pink and put the same color on my face as it did on the paper and all the faces above it. I came up between two swaying lamps and went in the door off Fold Street. The flowers in my hair dragged against the lintel. Inside I tried to shut my hand. Two fingers came as far as my palm. I made a fist with the other hand to show the rest of them what they had to do.

The third bell began as I came down to the passage. I had kept Iori’s time. The last bell would open the stage, and if I got her up to the apron I could put the guard between her and my sisters. I’d spent half the afternoon learning how long to pause after I gave her name, so every Kase officer on the terraces would have time to stand.

***

“Iori,” I called down the passage. I held my bad arm out from my side so I wouldn’t get blood on her when she came. The curtain moved. She showed me one black shoe before she brought herself out, with her fist closed over the cloth, and took as long to smile as if she were making sure it was me from the shoes upward.

Somebody was coming down from the stage. I could tell it was Tsune from the way the sound of her feet stopped at the edge of a step before the rest of her came into view. That pause had taken me weeks. “I heard your knee on the way in, Naho,” she said. She came down another step and caught sight of Iori at the end of the bench.

The street door opened behind me. Sae stood on the top step with the white plate from my elbow cupped in one hand. The torn skin had folded in on itself. I’d worn it in a straight line for years. “Close that door and stay by it, Sae,” Tsune said. “I’ll see to Naho myself.” Sae set the plate down on the step and went outside. Iori was still holding the curtain when the door shut.

“It’s very cold down here, dear,” Tsune said to Iori. “Come as far as the top and feel how warm the boards are tonight.”

Iori looked at me. “Are we still seeing if we can get on?”

“The trams have stopped.” I put my hand down between hers and the curtain. She let go and came against my side. I felt her draw back when she touched the wet sleeve. “Walk us through the wall at the tram gate, Tsune. The samurai shut it on the court’s word, and you speak for the court. Let the last bell go by without a name. The guard can keep its posts and we can ask the houses for one more night.”

“Kase will want the girl wherever you take her. They’ll look for her to give her a crown and the other houses will look for her to put her in the ground. If you’re very good at hiding, the other houses may only have to send you word of what they have done to somebody who once gave you a room.” Tsune lowered herself onto the step without using a hand. Her knees made no sound. “All that can begin in the morning, if we leave somebody to inherit. I want you to be able to go across a room in any of their cities without hearing the people in it get up and run.”

“There’s somebody left,” said Iori. “I can sit in Father’s chair. He showed me the gold letters on the back.” She worked the buckle loose on one of her shoes with a fingernail.

Tsune drew her folded coat in to make room beside her. “Come and sit here, Iori. It’s only a few steps. I’ve sat on these stairs with Naho after nearly every performance she’s given.”

“Would you tell me what for before I go up? The man in Father’s room sent me for a blanket. When I came back with it he took it in, and then a woman came out and handed it back to me. I had to go right across the palace twice for nothing.”

I knew how to send Iori up to sit beside Tsune in the same voice I’d used to tell her to bring her toe back to the mark. My sister was waiting with both palms flat on her knees, the way she did when there was to be no audience. I could have brought Iori straight into those hands.

“Tsune came down to kill you,” I said. Iori’s fingers came open against mine, and I went on because I had heard people try to make things clear for her all day by stopping in the middle of what they had to say. “I came back for you because I want to take you upstairs and say who you are, so the samurai will come and stand around you.”

The fourth bell shook the quiet out of the staircase. Iori drew in a short, badly made breath. I held her while she got a better one. When I looked up again Tsune was sitting square on the step with her head up, ready for someone to take her picture.

“I can wait here with her,” Tsune said to me. “She won’t need to go through another room. I can do it here and take her upstairs myself. You can go and talk to the people on the terraces, and I would like you to, because they think you’re lovely and you make them feel that they’ve come to see a very good thing.”

Tsune reached for her coat where it lay beside her. “Naho, Fusa has brought her whole family up to the fourth terrace. I saw them taking blankets to sit on, the good red ones she puts out when her sons come in from the country. Do you remember the night one of those boys got caught in the foundry riots? I was with her outside the shop. She had got his coat off its hook and for three hours she kept it folded in her arms, the way you carry somebody who’s fallen asleep. I said I would go and get him. I found him in the foundry infirmary after the surgeon took off his right foot. When I carried him up to her door his mother wrapped his coat around the left one. That came of shooting in one street. The men on the roofs will fire into their own city tonight to stop Kase taking it. The people who make their shoes are sitting on the steps between them.”

Tsune’s own coat was white that winter, and by the time she got home it was wet through with the first snow of the year. I could remember how long it took our house to get the creases out. I’d wanted it to look as it had done before she went out for him, in case she gave it to me one day. I had been studying what it would be like to be given the things she didn’t need anymore.

“When I started dancing in front of people, I always looked for you at the end,” I said. “I used to think one day you’d see me get it exactly right and we could stop practicing that part. You’re asking me to go up there and get it right again. When you came past with Iori in your arms, I’d be looking at your face to see if you’d noticed me turn my foot out.”

“I’d see you, Naho,” said Tsune. “You wouldn’t have to look for me.”

I was still waiting for Tsune to make the choice easier. She could still do what she’d offered. If I handed her the girl, tomorrow Fusa would be at her stall. I could take her the black shoes to have the backs let out. There would be no one to put them on. There was a relief in the thought of how much of the morning I could get through before I came to that fact again. I looked up at the steps and at Tsune’s two hands. She would have the body carried quietly after the vigil. I could tell people Iori had slept right through her father’s bells. People would come up to me in the street and put both their hands over one of mine.

“If I stood out where you showed me, could they see my feet?” Iori had let go of my hand. She put one shoe down flat and the other at an angle so wide we would have stopped a rehearsal. The shoe with the loose buckle started to slip off and she held it there with her toes. “I can stay there if I have to. Can I keep my feet like they are right now?”

I went down on my knees so close to her that the blue light came up under her chin. She leaned against me, trying to work the heel back where it belonged. The drum on Fold Street stopped. When it began again Iori was still trying. I wanted her to get the shoe on. I could see Tsune looking down on us, keeping the place on the step where she had said the child could sit.

“Keep your feet any way you please,” I said to Iori. Then to my sister, “I’m going to say her name. Please get those people off the terrace, Tsune. There’s still time to get them down the steps.”

Tsune rose and put on her white coat, giving it the tug she always gave it before she went out to bow. I got Iori past her and up to the space beneath the lotus. The red wiring behind my shoulder had caught on Iori’s coat. She stood quite close while I got her free and asked me if it hurt. I said it did. I asked her to stay where the hinge of the petal touched the floor. She touched it once with the toe of a black shoe so she’d be able to find it again.

Tsune came up the stairs after us. When she drew level with me she lifted her hand to my head. I held still to let her set my loose flower in its place. She had put a flower there before my first performance and afterward I had gone to look for it in my hair when the audience was finished, as if I thought the people would have watched it so hard they could have taken it from me. I could feel my sister’s fingers working through the hair at my scalp. The pin had always hurt a little when she set it.

She let her hand fall and glanced at the dark at the head of the stairs. “You can still give me Iori when we bow. Everyone will be watching your face.”

“Iori, wait for me here.” I took my sister’s hand so she couldn’t pass behind me. The tips of her fingers rested cool and light on the back of my good hand, where I still had all my feeling. She matched my grip exactly.

The last bell rang. Behind me, Iori said, “Seven.” The petals turned and the pink light reached the roofs at the far side of Fold Street. At our feet were the tops of twenty samurai helmets, smooth as the bottoms of good bowls. Beyond them silk offerings hung in rows down among the people. Tsune led into the bow, and with her free hand she reached for Iori.

I stepped between Tsune’s hand and the hinge. She struck my bad arm hard enough to take my feet off the floor. The pin she had just set dragged at my scalp. When my heel came down the plank gave, and I started to turn her toward the stair as you turn a partner through the open part of a figure. Her cheek came as close to mine as when she had taught me where to put my hand on a person’s face. Through the bow I saw the two scratches I’d left on her temple, which always took longer than the rest of her face to go pink in this light.

My hurt arm had begun to fold. I could watch it happen before I could feel it yield. Tsune came back through the space I’d opened and reached again for Iori. I could have let my arm drop. Tsune was close enough to take Iori while I went on with the bow alone. I knew which way to turn my head so the terraces would follow my face down to the guard, and how long to wait before I looked back. The iron rib of the open petal stood just behind Tsune. I could see the girl’s black shoes at the mark. I put all my weight into finishing the turn against the iron, knowing it would kill my sister.

Tsune struck the rib sideways. It held under the weight of both of us. I felt the plates across her breast come open. A white edge beside her ribs stuck out through the skin. She caught at the place with her hand and the torn flesh pulled after it. I had never seen what her breath moved inside her until it tried to move there in the open. The red folds under the plate drew tight and fell back slick against the boards while I lowered her. Blood went on coming through her fingers after all the strength had gone out of them. I thought she was saying my name. When I bent to hear her, it was the sound of her trying to get a breath past the blood in her mouth.

Iori stood on the far side of us, near enough to have watched where the blood went. She had got her feet set in the place she wanted. I turned my face to her. I felt my mouth keep trembling after the rest of me had gone still. “I can come now,” she said. She lifted one foot and set it back down. The second time she got the shoe over Tsune’s hand. I took the two steps to the apron with her while my sister was still trying to breathe.

I gave the name aloud. “I present Iori, daughter of the emperor and your sovereign.”

I held the pause I had practiced. On the first count the two samurai nearest us left their places. They set their lacquered bodies into the space we had just come through and folded at the knee. The boards hummed. Behind me my sister took a breath that lasted two counts. The third count went by without the next one. Beyond the guard the Kase officers on the terraces were all on their feet. The far ranks turned out to face the roofs, and three gold bars lit up on their collars all the way down the hill, even on the sentries below the tram platform.

The first shot crossed from the Temple of the Twenty Vows. The silk offerings jumped clear of their stalls and came down out of the light. One of the ranks of samurai turned its weapons on the roof, and in the first noise after that I could hear the tram windows giving way. People were lifting children off the brass studs by their clothes. I watched the bench where I’d told Fusa to sit go end over end across the fourth terrace. One of the blanket’s red corners spread in the air, high enough to be a flag, and then I could see the ground through the place where her family had been.

Iori put her hand in mine. I hadn’t wiped it yet. By the time I drew her back from the edge of the boards, Sae had come up from the street and knelt beside Tsune. She looked straight across the stage at me. Then she spread the white of her sleeve across Tsune’s open body. With the hand underneath, where hardly anybody could see it, she reached for our sister’s face.

***

In the morning they brought the dead up to the Open Lotus, where there was still a roof over the far end of the stage. The first boards they carried in smelled of wet wool and burnt paper. I knew Fusa by the flat place on her thumb where she had pressed the heels of all our shoes. They had laid her out in the blanket she’d brought to keep her son warm. Several of her teeth showed through the place where her cheek had torn. I watched one of the men set the board down and smooth the wool clear of them with the back of his hand. I could remember exactly which way I had told her to turn the bench for the best sight of the child.

The farthest petal had come down across the steps. Some of the pink light still worked in it. It was pale against the ash in the street, like daylight showing through the back of a strip of cloth. The other petals had stopped with the flower open. There was nowhere to stand at the apron without seeing what had become of the terraces. From the side of the stage Iori watched the men take the boards in, with a shawl someone had found for her gathered up under her chin. She had stayed in her black shoes. There was one buckle she kept looking down to make sure of.

A Kase officer brought a man with a camera to the stage and asked for a picture of the sovereign and the woman who had brought her out. They could put it up across their cities, he said. There was fighting now in the foundry towns and at two of the great ports. The company was taking the tracks for the guard. There were people who would have to give up their trains and their cargo for the fighting, and he wanted them to see whose business it had become. He showed me where he wanted us, between the pink flower and the men of the guard. Somebody had scrubbed the space he pointed to. Along the groove in the boards where Tsune’s casing went down, the water had dried nearly black.

The samurai came and stood in the place where I had shown Iori how they would bow to her. Each lacquer collar still had the three gold bars. Iori put the shawl down and took one step out on the apron. “I want you to stop the fighting,” she said. “The people who have to be carried up are still down there. You could carry them.” The nearest pair folded at the knee. She waited for them to look up the way I had when she asked me something. “Please stop now.”

“You have the sovereign’s order,” the officer told them. “Go and put down the rebels.” He said it for the crowd below as much as for the guard. The samurai rose and began to turn for him. I could see along Fold Street how far that turn travelled. There were men coming through the tram gate already, with the gold marks lit on every collar. The two at the apron moved as soon as the ranks opened to let them through.

“That isn’t what I said,” Iori said to me. She pulled my hand. “Naho, tell him what I said. He’ll listen to you.”

I opened my mouth to speak. A samurai at the officer’s shoulder came far enough between us to part her hand from mine. The officer looked at me over his lacquer arm. “If you have a different account to give, he will take you downstairs. If you have come to attend the sovereign, you can sit with her for the picture.”

Iori was waiting. The man behind the camera moved the dark cloth away from his face to see what I would do. I could see how it would look if I knelt near enough for the child to touch me, and what it would look like to her if I let the man put me out of the room. The samurai took half a step back as I lowered myself beside her. She drew her legs up close to mine, and had no more to say to me.

I put my good hand down flat behind me, where I could feel the split in the boards. Tsune’s flower was still there in my hair. I felt the pin go deeper when the man asked me to raise my face. I did it carefully, so Iori could have as much of me beside her as they would let her keep. The guard went on moving in the ground under my hand. I held still for the picture.
