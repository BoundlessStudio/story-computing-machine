# The Last Courtesy

Iori had been given two pairs of shoes, black for mourning and gold for the throne, and when I went in to show her the bow she had one of each on. She pushed the black shoe out from under the hem of her coat. “This one pinches. They said to practice in the others, so I put one on to see if my feet got any different.”

At the Open Lotus, a girl could be fitted for her father’s funeral and her own coronation by the same woman on the same stool. The shoemaker had cut my first pair. At my first performance a man from one of the steel houses sat with his mouth open through the whole third dance because Tsune had killed him in the second and his wife was too polite to wake him. I approved almost everything about the photograph of that evening until I saw that I was turning my right foot out.

I showed Iori how the samurai would fold at the knee. I did it slowly so she could see my hand go down for balance and my white shin plates slip past each other by a hair. The mechanical samurai had already set their feet at the stage apron. Their low hum came through the boards into my wrist. Resin was sweating out of a knot beside my hand. Pink light came in at the edges of the closed lotus petals and turned violet where it met the blue under my ribs. Iori watched that point and tried to hold her breath, which was her idea of how a person could stay still that long.

“Will they kneel like that for me?”

“As soon as I give them your name. Look how far down I go. Then they’ll get up and stay with you.”

“Can I tell them things to do?”

“Your mother’s house will tell them, until you’re old enough.”

“They can tell them I’m going to stay up. Father has seven bells tonight and I only got to hear one yesterday. The woman who brought me my supper went back for a spoon and by the time I got my spoon there was only one left.” Iori undid the buckle of her gold shoe, holding her hair out of her mouth. “If it isn’t allowed you can tell me here. I can keep it to myself.”

It was the first time she had spoken to me as though I were someone who had a door of her own and could shut it. I was still on the floor considering what I could promise when Tsune came in. She had a way of walking onto boards already crowded with people so there was space for her a step before she needed it. In her ceremony whites she could reach the narrow pink gap between the petals without picking up any of their color. I had never managed that. My casing took up every scrap of light.

She bent to point out to Iori where she had dropped the other gold shoe. Then she took me a little way down the back stair, until we stood below the level of the floor.

“You’ll take her to the far dressing room in a minute, Naho. I thought you might bring a bit of cake. She knows you and you can get her to sit.” She settled the edge of my collar where it had caught against a wire. “When I come to fetch you the girl must be dead. Quietly, so that people can go home believing the emperor’s illness took her too.”

We stood close enough that I could have turned her head between my palms and found the two little scratches I had made when she was teaching me how to hold a partner’s face. I had been the youngest of the Imperial Geisha long after I was tired of anybody calling me young. Tsune still waited for a nod from me when she gave an order, the way she waited for every pupil to find the first beat.

“Someone else can give her name tonight,” I said, “if you’re taking the privilege from me.”

“Dear heart, the other houses have put their armies on the roofs. When you give the guard to Kase they’ll start shooting before the child has taken her first step. We have every house’s seal on a common command, if the throne stays empty for good. Kase gave us its seal this morning.”

The first Imperial Geisha had been engineered a thousand years ago, when the corporate houses had at last grown too rich to afford an open war. For most of the years we had kept the peace, the people we entertained had credited our faces and the beautiful manners of the court. They liked the sight of a white hand empty on a table. A man once took mine in both of his and thanked me for the peace, while I counted the seconds left to him. Tsune had put me beside him specially because his daughter would be dining too. I was supposed to have learned something from how little of that evening the girl had had cause to remember.

Upstairs a small shoe scraped against the boards. I had let Iori overhear me talking about her in the third person once already that day. When I went back to her I meant to have something I could say to her face.

“I’m taking her out of the city,” I said. “I won’t kill her.”

The bell for the opening bow struck. I had the width of the passage while Tsune went to the apron. At the first bell the audience could see only the little piece of wood between the petals and the drop to the street, where the eldest of us had to stand with both hands spread for the welcome. I took Iori’s warm wrist and told her to bring the black shoes. She managed to get both into one hand. From the back stair I heard Tsune dispatch our sisters as they bowed beside her. Her voice had scarcely more force in it than it took to send for a clean glass.

“Bring me Naho before the last bell. Let her come back walking if she’ll do it.”

***

I took Iori down to the passage that ran under the Open Lotus. It was deep enough in the hill for winter to come up through the floor. The spare folds of curtain lay on a cedar bench there, alongside the wool shoes we put on when we came off the boards. I had never seen such small bare feet on those stones. I helped her get both black shoes on and buckled, though she tried to do the second one herself while I was still doing the first.

“Where are we going?”

“To the last tram on Fold Street. Let me see if I can get us on it. You’ll be out of the wind here.”

“I can go where there’s wind. I want to come while you see.”

I could make it down the terraces and back in the time it would take her to cross the first one. I wanted to tell her just that much. “There’ll be people out there looking for me, Iori. Please sit here in the curtain. We’ll try the tram together when I get back.”

She drew a blue length of it across her lap. “Will you be back by the third bell? After three there’s four more.”

“By the third bell,” I said, with no idea how much time they meant to leave between them.

Down the middle of Fold Street ran a row of brass studs to keep room for the trams. When the street got full, people balanced on the studs to see how close they could get to the passing cars. The parents who called for their children to get down were the first to go up on the brass when a performance began. I could hear them calling now. I slipped outside in the smell of singed plum sugar from the offering stalls and went down the hill along the tram lane.

The temple signs of Karusen were allowed to shine across the full width of the street on the nights we mourned a sovereign. I passed through blue letters high enough to take in the houses on both sides. The Temple of the Twenty Vows had lit its vows one below the other, and the tenements across the street wore them against their windows. Farther downhill a pink advertisement for Kase trams curled under the statue of a god with birds in her hands. There were red wires at my shoulder that were my own red again by the time I reached the platform. I was glad of how little there was left of me that could feel the cold.

From the public stair above the platform I could see the white lights of the tram coming to take the night workers out past the gates. There were still people on the platform who cared about that tram more than the imperial succession. One woman tried to read the board through the top half of a cake she was eating. I thought I might send her up to fetch Iori, and recognized the thought as one I had learned to have at work. I wanted a hand to come out of the crowd and help me. A hand which could be explained afterward.

The white lights went out. A change travelled down the board, replacing the fares and stops with a suspension of service by order of the court. The tram was still coming. Women stepped forward with their bags. It went straight by, with the temple signs lighting row after row of empty seats, and stopped just short of the lattice the samurai were drawing across the tracks. When I looked along the lit streets running down to the city wall, I could see lacquer shoulder plates at the other gates. The women on the platform began talking about how long it would take to walk home. They were still talking as if a samurai had ever opened a closed gate for somebody who wanted to go home.

I heard the second bell while I stood there. I had promised the girl three.

Two of my sisters came down the upper stair with a length of mourning silk between them. One held it high to keep it off the platform while the other bent to address a samurai. I could see his waiting face reflected in the white of her cheek. She showed him the folded end of the cloth, and he drew the side gate open as a court sentry does for the women officiating. He shut it again behind them and resumed his watch. The sisters lowered the silk and looked among the people it had screened from them. I could have put out my hand and shown the sentry I wore the same white. It would have bought me admission to the platform they were on.

There was a tick in my knee when I turned from the stairs. Tsune had once heard it from the far side of an orchestra and made me start a whole dance over. All the women who had worked in our hall that year knew it. One of my sisters lifted her chin. The other gathered the silk and came so fast that the woman with the cake turned right around in the breeze she left without seeing whom to blame.

I ran back up Fold Street. Above the roof of the Twenty Vows I caught sight of the low, quite steady movement of the rival companies’ troops. Down among the stalls the mourning procession was coming around the foot of our terraces, bearing hundreds of paper lamps they would carry uphill and lay at the emperor’s bier. A white vow burned across the backs of the people ahead of me. My own reflection in a dark stall window was so sharp I could see the pink flower coming loose from the crown of my black hair. I took the next long step up into the company of the mourners. Above us the lit vow gave way to the next one. Every white lamp took on the same pink, and for three steps I carried the same color on my plates as the mourners did on their sleeves. I dipped with the lamps. From the stair under the stage I saw my sister halt and make way as they bowed between us.

The third bell began as I came down to the passage. I had never been so glad to have mistimed a ceremony. I could put the guard between Iori and my sisters now, if I brought her up from under the stage and gave her name where it was meant to be given. I had meant to say it that night, before Tsune came for me. There had been a whole order of things I was to say afterward. I could hear even the pause I had practiced for the honor of her mother’s house.

***

Iori had got up off the bench, taking a part of the curtain with her. Her little fist was closed over the cloth. She saw me then, came as far as she could without letting go of it, and pointed to the stairs up to the stage. Somebody was coming down them. I could tell it was Tsune from the way the sound of her feet stopped at the edge of a step before the rest of her came into view. That pause had taken me weeks.

“It’s very cold down here, dear,” she said to Iori. “Come as far as the top and feel how warm the boards are tonight.”

Iori looked at me. “Are we still seeing if we can get on?”

“The trams have stopped.” I put my hand down between hers and the curtain. She let go and came against my side. I could feel where her breath raised the fine hairs on my arm. “Let me take Iori out through the gate, Tsune. The guard stays where it is if she isn’t presented tonight. We can come back to the houses with new terms in the morning.”

“Kase will want the girl wherever you take her. They’ll look for her to give her a crown and the other houses will look for her to put her in the ground. If you’re very good at hiding they may only have to send you word of what they have done to somebody who once gave you a room.” Tsune lowered herself onto the step without using a hand. Her knees made no sound. “All that can begin in the morning, if we leave it tonight. I spent a little of today getting their promise on what they’ll do if there’s nobody left to inherit. I want you to be able to go across a room in any of their cities without hearing the people in it get up and run.”

“There’s somebody left,” said Iori. “Is that the throne that has Father’s name on the back?”

Tsune drew her folded coat in to make room beside her. “Come and sit here, Iori. It’s only a few steps. I’ve sat on these stairs with Naho after nearly every performance she’s given.”

“Would you tell me what for before I go up? The man in Father’s room sent me for a blanket and when I brought it back somebody had put it in my arms to take away again. I had to go right across the palace twice for nothing.”

I could still make a word like *nobody* sound soft in my mouth and persuade the child to go up to Tsune as if there were only the business of her shoes left to settle. For an instant I thought that was what my sister wanted me to do. Then I saw how she was waiting with both palms on her knees, the way she did when there was to be no audience.

“Tsune came down to kill you,” I said. Iori’s fingers came open against mine, and I went on because I had heard people try to make things clear for her all day by stopping in the middle of what they had to say. “I came back for you because I want to take you upstairs and say who you are, so the samurai will come and stand around you.”

The fourth bell shook the quiet out of the staircase. Iori drew in a short, badly made breath. I held her while she got a better one. When I looked up again Tsune was holding herself very straight. It was the first time that evening I had seen her sit as squarely as if she were waiting for someone to take her picture.

“I can wait here with her a little while,” Tsune said to me. “She won’t need to go through another room. I can do it here and take her upstairs myself. You can go and talk to the people on the terraces, and I would like you to, because they think you’re lovely and you make them feel that they’ve come to see a very good thing.”

I looked at the child’s shoes. “While I give them something to look at.”

“Naho, the shoemaker has brought her whole family up to the fourth terrace. I saw them taking blankets to sit on, the good ones she puts out when her sons come in from the country. Do you remember the night one of those boys got caught in the foundry riots? I was with her outside the shop. She had got his coat off its hook and for three hours she kept it folded in her arms, the way you carry somebody who’s fallen asleep. I said I would go and get him. By the time I carried him up to her door a surgeon had taken off his right foot. His mother wrapped his coat around the left one. That came of shooting in one street. The men on the roofs will fire into their own city tonight to stop Kase taking it. The people who make their shoes are sitting on the steps between them.”

Tsune’s own coat was white that winter, and by the time she got home it was wet through with the first snow of the year. I could remember how long it took our house to get the creases out. I’d wanted it to look as it had done before she went out for him, in case she gave it to me one day. I had been studying what it would be like to be given the things she didn’t need anymore.

I had expected her to give me a way out of this as well. She could still do what she’d offered. If I handed her the girl, tomorrow the shoemaker would be at her stall. I could take her the black shoes to have the backs let out. There would be no one to put them on. There was a terrible relief in the thought of how much of the morning I could get through before I came to that fact again. I looked up at the steps and at Tsune’s two hands. She would have the body carried quietly after the vigil, and I could say that I had given Iori the darkness she’d had to do without all day. People would come up to me in the street and put both their hands over one of mine.

“If I stood out where you showed me, could they see my feet?” Iori had let go of my hand. One shoe was partway off. She pulled it on again with a little gasp. “I can stay there if I have to. Can I keep my feet like they are right now?”

I went down on my knees so close to her that the blue light came up under her chin. She leaned against me while she got the heel back where it belonged. Time passed enough for the sound of a drum from out on the street to cease and come again. I wanted her to get the shoe on. I could see Tsune looking down on us, keeping the place on the step where she had said the child could sit.

“Keep your feet any way you please,” I said to Iori. Then to my sister, “I’m going to say her name. Please get those people off the terrace, Tsune. There’s still time to get them down the steps.”

Tsune rose. I got Iori past the step where she had been sitting, all the way up the stair to the space beneath the lotus. The red wiring behind my shoulder had caught on Iori’s coat and she stood quite close while I got her free. You could see the tip of a black shoe through the opening on the apron if you knew where to look. I asked her to stay where the hinge of the petal touched the floor. She touched it once with the toe so she’d be able to find it again.

Tsune came up the stairs after us. When she drew level with me she lifted her hand to my head. I held still to let her set my loose flower in its place. She had put a flower there before my first performance and afterward I had gone to look for it in my hair when the audience was finished, as if I thought the people would have watched it so hard they could have taken it from me. I could feel my sister’s fingers working through the hair at my scalp. The pin had always hurt a little when she set it.

She let her hand fall. “We have the whole time it takes to bow. The girl could go down the stair while they look at us.”

“Iori, wait for me here.” I took my sister’s hand so she couldn’t pass behind me. In its accustomed place, the tips of her fingers rested cool and light on the back of my palm. She matched my grip exactly.

The last bell rang. The petals turned and the pink light reached the roofs at the far side of Fold Street. At our feet were the tops of twenty samurai helmets, smooth as the bottoms of good bowls. Beyond them silk offerings hung in little rows down among the people. Tsune led into the bow. She had one arm free and with it she reached past my shoulder for the space where the child was waiting.

I had spent half my life learning when Tsune would put her weight into a movement. The first step of the dance makes room, and there is a step after it for passing a partner through. I took her weight and turned with it. The boards buckled against my heel, enough to lift the grain out of the plank. Our faces were at the height people like to see in the picture of a bow, hers as close to mine as when I was small enough to run under her hands out of a bath. I had her arm across me. It lifted both my feet off the floor before I set them down again. Looking past her I saw that one side of the space beneath the petals was clear. Iori stood with her two black shoes facing in different directions. She had her own balance. She was looking straight ahead.

“I want to come now,” she said.

I let her see my face. She went past me. I let go of Tsune and took the two steps to the apron with Iori, in the time it took the people who were sitting to see there was a girl going out in front of the dancers. I could hear Tsune come after us. It was possible, even then, to lift Iori up and give her back to her.

I gave the name aloud. “I present Iori, daughter of the emperor and your sovereign.”

The two samurai nearest us left their places. They set their lacquered bodies into the space we had just come through and folded at the knee. I felt their weight in the floor as I had felt it in the wood when I knelt for Iori. Tsune stopped on the other side of them. She had one hand up in the air where she could have reached the girl. As the shoulder plates of the samurai came together I saw her turn her head to look down Fold Street. The far ranks turned out to face the roofs. There was a moment in which all of Karusen could see which house had the guard.

The first shot crossed from the Temple of the Twenty Vows. The silk offerings jumped clear of their stalls and came down out of the light. One of the ranks of samurai turned its weapons on the roof, and in the first noise after that I could hear the tram windows giving way. People were coming up the steps as fast as they had been able to come down and taking children off the brass studs by their clothes. Where every other face was moving away I saw Tsune go down toward the fourth terrace, her hands at chest height as though she were about to ask somebody to stand.

Iori put her hand in the one that had held my sister. Her fingers found the flesh between the plates. We could see over the shoulders of the guard to the place where the first people had fallen. Tsune was there now. She dug both hands under an overturned bench and lifted it, and Iori and I watched to see who would come out from under it.
