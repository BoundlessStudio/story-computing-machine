At the first bend in the wood, Tessa stopped to let the person behind her catch up.

Nobody came round the bend.

She raised the lantern. The path showed empty as far as the hazels, with a little wet shine on each root. Beyond them she could see nothing. She waited, holding the light out until her arm began to ache, then lowered it and went on.

Three steps. A pause. The soft weight of a fourth.

An animal, then. She knew the noise of a badger in leaves, brisk and busy, and the sudden scrambling retreat of deer. This was neither, but there were plenty of things in the wood she had never seen. She had walked here for twenty years without seeing an owl eat anything.

The path climbed gently toward the fallen beech. Beyond that was the stile at the top corner of her field, which ran downhill beside the wood. The cottage stood above the stile. Ten minutes, if she kept walking. She had stayed too long at supper, accepting another slice of cake while the kitchen filled with the smell of rain. Nobody had quite wanted to be the first to get up.

The lantern was borrowed. It had a dent in its brass side and a door that rattled when she swung it. She tried holding it still. Behind her, something pressed down through the leaves.

Tessa looked ahead. Her kitchen window was shining through the trees.

She felt such relief that she laughed. There was the yellow curtain, with its triangular tear low on the left, and her blue cup on the table beneath it. Someone was stirring the cup. She could hear the small, pleasant taps of the spoon against its rim.

“Who's there?” she called.

She took another step before she remembered the beech.

She had not reached it. The path had not yet steepened. And the window was down among the trees on her right, whereas her cottage stood above the stile.

The spoon tapped again.

She lived alone. There was no fire laid, no tea in the pot. She had left the blue cup upside down on its draining board.

She lifted the lantern higher. Near branches sprang into view, some reaching between her and the yellow light. She could not see a wall. Beyond either edge of the window, the trees went on.

“Who's there?”

Her own voice came back. It had her little breathless laugh before it, the laugh she had made when she thought she was nearly home.

Tessa backed away until her heel struck a root. She caught herself with one hand against a trunk. The window went dark.

For a moment she thought whoever was there had blown out a lamp. Then the yellow square appeared again farther to the left. It passed behind a tree, vanished, and came out on the other side.

Something pale unfolded beneath the sill.

Her lantern caught a smooth length as thick as her thigh, with two bends in it. The end was clasped around the fork of an ash. It tightened; the fork creaked. The window swung toward her, and the pale length twisted to follow, caught, and pulled again. A branch broke with the sharp report of a snapped chair leg.

Tessa ran back down the path. The borrowed lantern banged against her knee. Its light jumped over stones and leaves. She heard the spoon. It was beside her now.

The yellow curtain moved between trunks. Pale joints appeared and disappeared above it, gripping one branch, then another. The whole bright room tilted. The blue cup leaned with the table, but the spoon kept going round.

At the bend, her kitchen turned to face her.

It hung over the path she had come by. Beneath it, a little light fell on the hazel leaves. There was still no wall, no step, nothing to put a house upon.

Tessa stopped so abruptly that the lantern struck her shin. She heard herself draw a breath through her teeth.

From behind the yellow curtain came the same sound.

Below her lay the field hedge. She knew its shape from the other side: the long dark slope, the ash growing through its middle, the shallow hollow where badgers had worked a passage underneath. Once, fetching a pillowcase the wind had taken, she had knelt there and reached through up to her shoulder. Her fingers had found wet cloth on the leaves beyond.

She had never put her head into the hole.

She went downhill between the trees. There was no path here. She held the lantern close to the ground and pushed through dead bracken, finding places for her feet a moment before she had to use them. A twig caught her braid and pulled it loose. She left the ribbon hanging.

Above her, wood creaked under weight. She lifted the lantern, saw the yellow window sliding between two trunks, and lost her footing on a root. She caught a handful of bracken. It broke off in her hand. She kept the light down after that.

“Who's there?” said the kitchen.

The voice was very close. It came from somewhere beyond her right shoulder, with a bright clink of spoon against cup. Her own warm room was following her through the wood.

Tessa pushed harder. The hedge found her before she found it: a thorn across her cheek, a stiff branch against her chest. She thrust the lantern underneath and saw woven stems, roots, a clot of old leaves. No opening.

She went left, bent almost double. There should have been an ash here. She could see a trunk above her, but not its base. Her skirt caught. She dragged it free and heard the cloth tear.

The tapping stopped.

She could hear the small rattle in her lantern door. Nothing else.

She dropped to her knees. At ground level there was a dark slit beneath the lowest stems. She pulled leaves out of it. Beyond them she saw a blade of grass, bent toward her through the gap.

The light behind her grew yellow.

Tessa pushed the lantern through. It came to rest upright on the field grass, just beyond her reach. Its little flame burned steadily. She lay flat and put her head into the hole.

Damp earth pressed against her jaw. Her shoulders stopped her. She tried again, pushing with both boots, and a root dug into the top of her arm. Beyond the glass of the lantern was open grass. She could see it. She could smell it.

The spoon tapped once.

She turned onto her side and drove one shoulder forward. A thorn scraped past her ear. She got her elbow onto the grass and pulled until her other shoulder came free.

Something descended behind her heels.

Her coat tightened down her back. Then she was sliding backward, her elbow ploughing the grass, the horn toggle at her throat pressing hard against her windpipe.

Tessa caught a root with her left hand. With her right she fumbled for the toggle. It was pulled tight across its loop. She could not turn it. The root slipped through her wet fingers an inch, and the grass beyond the lantern moved farther away.

She let herself go backward just enough to slacken the coat.

The toggle turned.

She folded an arm against her chest and pulled it out of its sleeve. Cold struck her neck and back. The other sleeve held her wrist, turned inside out, and released it.

Tessa dragged herself onto the grass. Her hips scraped through, then her knees. As she pulled her boots clear, the hedge bowed above her. The yellow square struck the upper thorns and tipped almost on its side. Something heavy crashed against the stems. A shower of small twigs fell over her hair.

She snatched up the lantern and ran. Halfway across the field, she heard a long crack from the hedge. She ran harder. The cottage roof was a darker shape against the sky; then the step was under her foot, and the latch was under her thumb, and it lifted.

She fell through the doorway and shoved it shut with her shoulder. The bolt would not go into its socket. She lifted the door by the handle, the way she always had to, and it slid home.

Tessa stood with her palm against the bolt. Her breathing filled the passage. She tried to hear past it. There was a little popping sound from the lantern wick, and the ordinary ticking of her kitchen clock.

She took one step toward the kitchen, then another. The room was where she had left it. The blue cup stood upside down beside the sink. On the chair lay the apron she had pulled off before going out.

She picked up the matchbox from beside the kitchen lamp.

Beyond the curtain was darkness. If she lit the lamp, a yellow square would open in it. The triangle she had torn with the end of a broom would show low on the left.

Tessa put the matchbox down. She set the lantern on the floor, opened its little door, and blew it out. For a while she could see only the shape of its flame, red and green whenever she blinked. Then the room slowly returned, grey at the edges.

She stood beside the window and drew the curtain back with one finger.

Across the field, at the hedge, her kitchen was still alight.

Her coat hung beside that window. One sleeve was inside out.
