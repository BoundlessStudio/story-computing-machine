# After the Porch Lights Go Out

## Promised experience

“The woods are scary at night...” now explicitly asks for “that Halloween/fall-night horror”; the correction also names “dont go into the woods” and “spooky/horror story.” The pleasure I infer is the gathering dread of a familiar autumn place becoming somewhere the reader wants to stay out of, followed by a chill that survives the ending. Halloween should supply the invitation and the danger, not merely the date. The request prescribes no protagonist, relationship, threat, scale, length, or ending. A cautionary campfire voice, a vanished friend, and an unresolved supernatural threat are my choices, not requirements. Fear can be sustained and consequential while its treatment remains non-graphic.

## Provisional direction

An unnamed adult recalls the Halloween when she and her friend Marnie, both fourteen, took their familiar daytime shortcut between two streets. They were getting too old for trick-or-treating and wanted one more rich street before its porch lights went out. The narrator proposed the shortcut. Marnie wore a cheap green witch mask and pointed hat; the narrator wore a skeleton costume. The woods offered them another Halloween, and only the narrator came back. Close first person keeps the reader with what she could hear and glimpse; she has no account of what lives there or why it let her out. These are limits on her knowledge, not a system of monster rules.

## Possible movement

- Begin directly inside the remembered Halloween, without an opening explanation of the narrator's loss: candle smoke escaping a jack-o'-lantern, hard candy knocking in a plastic pail, the cold coming through a costume after the fun has started to wear thin. Her mother said to come home by the road, all the way around. At the last lit house, they can see the other street's porch lights through the bare trees. The short path is familiar. They step into it before either quite admits how dark it is.
- Leaves cover the turns they ordinarily recognize. Behind them the neighbourhood's voices dwindle much faster than their distance should allow. Ahead are orange lights and children calling to one another. They take this for another group heading home. Then Marnie notices that the lights are inside the woods, where there are no houses. The two turn back promptly; the story does not depend on their continuing to ignore obvious danger.
- The path back is occupied. Costumed children stand among the trunks, so still they first look like discarded decorations. Their paper crowns and cloth masks are wet and sagging. There are more the longer the narrator looks. The girls try to squeeze past one and ask it to move; it does not answer or make room, but its face follows them. Their smallest movements crackle in the leaves; the others make no sound when they turn. Give this attempted passage time as an encounter before the girls run. Warm door-light opens far beyond the gathering, between trees, and a woman calls the children in. The narrator cannot see a house around that light. The whole wood now seems to be waiting for the girls to join it.
- The narrator takes Marnie's gloved hand and pulls her away. Their retreat breaks into a frightened scramble through branches and dry leaves, with only fragments visible: a mask close beside a trunk, another light ahead, the same waiting figures where open ground should be. No revealed creature starts a chase, and no discovery makes the route safe. The narrator ducks under a low limb, leading Marnie by the hand. Marnie jerks to a stop behind her. The narrator braces and pulls; she feels Marnie's fingers grip back, then the glove flattens in her hand. Her next steps carry her onto the road. The physical contact and its loss are legible, but she does not witness or understand what took Marnie.
- Marnie calls from just beyond the trees. The narrator starts back. The same call comes from farther along the verge, then from behind the first voice. She stops with one foot in the leaves and withdraws it, refusing to follow before she hears any approaching car. Only afterward do headlights expose ordinary tree trunks and bring an adult who takes her off the road; they do not reveal Marnie. The narrator cannot establish whether the first call was really her friend. Searches in daylight find the familiar short path and no place that could have held the gathering.
- A brief return to the adult narrator closes the warning. On Halloween, after the last porch lights go out, she sometimes hears Marnie's impatient invitation from the woods: they can still get to one more house. The voice has not grown older. The narrator takes the road, all the way around. End on the invitation remaining behind her, without identifying its speaker, offering a cure, or making the loss a lesson she has mastered.

## Possibility and risk

The strongest possibility is Halloween's ordinary invitation becoming unbearable: light, costumes, children's voices, and the promise of one more house all lead farther into the autumn dark. This addresses the user's correction in the causal beats: the holiday motivates entry, its apparent celebration delays recognition, and its invitation survives the disappearance. The most consequential risk is allowing the short escape or the narrator's guilt to displace the woods' accumulating presence. Preserve time for the approach and the waiting gathering; keep the escape fragmentary and the aftermath brief. The ending should leave a place to fear, not a creature to understand or a grieving narrator to reassure.
