# Provisional outline — The Window in the Wood

## Promised experience

The complete request is: "[SP] The woods are scary at night..." Its explicit elements are woods, night, and fear. I infer a pleasure in entering that fear rather than explaining it away: darkness should make the woodland consequential, with a disturbing discovery or encounter that earns the trailing ellipsis. The wording leaves scale, viewpoint, length, characters, relationships, the source of danger, and the ending open. A supernatural threat and an unnerving survival ending are choices below, not user requirements.

## Provisional direction

A compact, close-third story following Tessa, an adult who knows a footpath through the wood well enough to take it after sunset. She wants to reach her cottage on the far side. No central human relationship is proposed; her attachment to her familiar home gives the threat something recognizable to imitate. The wood contains a living, concealed hunter that offers a counterfeit glimpse of home. Its nature remains unknown. Its observed movement and limited reach matter; there is no stated universal rule about forests, darkness, or magic.

1. Tessa enters on an ordinary errand's return with a small hand lantern. Establish the path's turn, a fallen beech, and the thorn hedge separating the wood from the open field below her cottage. In daylight the shortcut is unremarkable. As daylight leaves, the lantern keeps showing one little piece of it at a time; something walks when she walks, just beyond that piece.
2. Her kitchen window shines ahead of her. Its yellow curtain has the familiar triangular tear, and a spoon taps her blue cup. She almost calls out before noticing that she has not reached the fallen beech and that her cottage should be uphill, beyond the field. She lives alone and left the house dark. A branch crosses the apparent window without interrupting its light.
3. She retreats. The window goes dark, then appears between different trunks, closer. Her lantern catches a pale supporting limb beneath it, bending where a sill should be. Her own tentative "Who's there?" comes back from the kitchen in her own voice. The apparent refuge has heard her and is following her; the path now leads toward it whichever way she turns.
4. Tessa abandons the path and its offered lights. She goes downhill through the unlit undergrowth toward the hedge she knows borders the field. This is a frightened gamble on remembered ground, not mastery of the creature's rules. The counterfeit window slides alongside her through the branches; the spoon keeps tapping even when the cup tilts. Roots and thorns impede her while something much larger passes overhead.
5. At the hedge she can go back toward the inviting window or force herself through the narrow badger gap established earlier as a daylight nuisance. She puts her lantern through first, crawls after it, and loses her coat to a long pale joint reaching from the wood. She frees herself from the coat and crawls beyond its reach into the open field. The false window remains suspended behind the hedge; light reveals no cottage around it.
6. Tessa reaches her actual dark cottage, locks the door, and refuses to light the kitchen lamp. Through the real window she sees the counterfeit one still shining in the trees. It now has her discarded coat hanging beside it. End on a concrete detail that shows the hunter continuing to improve its invitation, leaving the wood frightening after the immediate escape rather than explaining or defeating it.

The possibility to protect is the moment familiar domestic safety becomes a hunting lure, followed by a physically legible escape through genuinely frightening woods. The chief risk is turning dread into a neatly solved monster puzzle or making the final imitation a decorative twist; observations must remain partial, Tessa's route must cost her something, and the closing light must belong to the same pursuing presence.
