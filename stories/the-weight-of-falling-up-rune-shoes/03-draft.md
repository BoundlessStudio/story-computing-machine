# The Weight of Falling Up

By the time Examiner Dovek called my name, three people had already left the roof in three sensible ways.

The first leaned over a broom and cut around the western chimney in a polished brown arc. The second rose upright inside a levitation working, coat hems hanging as calmly as if the sky had agreed to become a drawing room. The third went past between the lifted wings of a pale horse, hands buried in its mane while each downstroke sent a warm gust rolling over the slates.

Then there was me.

“Juni Wex,” Dovek called again.

“Present. Still regrettably attached to the building.”

The Collegium of Nine Eaves occupied most of the view below us: black roofs pitched at improbable angles, bridges stitching tower to tower, windows bright as chips of amber. Beyond it, the city fell away in terraces toward a silver bend of river. The launch roof was so high that birds passed beneath the gutters. Prestige, I had learned, was often ordinary terror with excellent masonry.

I sat on the warm slate and unlaced my shoes. Mine were scuffed brown leather, bought without enchantment, one still bearing a pale water mark from a badly judged fountain.

I pulled them off, set my stockinged feet on the roof, and turned the right shoe sole-up across my knee.

Dovek crouched opposite me. Their examination coat snapped once in the wind and settled. “Begin.”

The ink stylus came from my cuff. I pressed its blunt nib directly to the heel and drew the first stroke, black and wet, toward the arch. The second crossed it near the middle. The third opened toward the toes like a little bent door. Nothing happened. Ink was only ink until someone living asked more of it.

I cupped the shoe between both hands, breathed, and let warmth run out through my palms.

The three lines drank it. For an instant, black deepened beyond black.

I drew the mirror on the other sole, charged it, and put both shoes back on. My fingers trembled at the laces. Apparently fear was mostly an audience with opinions.

Dovek tapped one gloved finger beside the right-hand mark. “No closing stroke.”

“I’m fond of steering.”

“And your stationary count?”

“Quick feet.”

I traced an imaginary bar across the open end of the rune. “I can close one mark in the air. While the bar stays drawn, every time I put weight and power through that shoe, its step fixes in place for up to three breaths. It vanishes the instant I release it or push off. I can run on it, but while I’m standing there, that foot is planted. No steering.”

“A limitation.”

“A feature with an unfortunate posture.”

“A limitation,” Dovek repeated. “You are required to remain inside the stationary boundary for a count of three. Demonstrate the limitation. Do not conceal it.”

The broom candidate flashed past the far eave, coat streaming straight behind. Above, the levitation candidate hung with the insulting ease of a portrait on a wall. The winged horse climbed through a patch of sun, every pale feather briefly edged in gold.

I imagined myself balanced on one leg in the middle of all that grace, bent-kneed and wobbling, like a heron reconsidering its profession.

“Understood,” I said.

I did understand. I simply had no intention of doing it.

* * *

Dovek raised a hand.

I took three running steps over the slates.

On the fourth, there was no roof.

I drove warmth down my left leg and committed my weight. The mark under my sole shaped the answering force, and empty air went hard beneath my foot.

Not everywhere beneath me. Not around me. Only there: one glass-bright footprint hanging past the gutter, exactly the size and slant of my shoe.

My stomach dropped. The rest of me did not.

I pushed off.

The shining step vanished behind my heel, shedding a few black motes into the blue. I lifted my right knee high and planted the next sole above the first. Another bright patch took my weight. Then left, then right, and the roof began to sink away.

There are respectable ways to fly. I had watched them practiced from the lower courts for years: the long broom bank, the serene rise, the powerful bargain between a rider and a winged animal. They all looked as though the sky were a country one might enter with the correct papers.

I arrived as a trespasser running up the walls.

Wind opened my coat. Bells chimed somewhere below, nine notes thrown loose among the roofs. Each step appeared only when I chose it and gave it weight. Each one rang silently through my bones, firm as stone for the fraction of a stride and then gone. The air did not carry me. It let me strike it, and I climbed on the blows.

I laughed.

That nearly failed me at the first height marker.

The course turned around a tall iron arch strung with blue streamers. I planted my right foot too flat, expecting support to gather beneath my hips as it would in a levitation working. The foothold formed square to my sole. My ankle shot ahead; my shoulders went backward; sky filled my vision.

“Excellent,” I told the clouds. “A view.”

My left foot swung through. I crossed it hard over the right, angled the sole down and inward, and fed the mark. Light flared under it. The new step caught me sideways. I twisted from the hips, pushed, and came around in a dancer’s turn so sharp my coat tails slapped my knees.

The broom candidate banked beyond the streamers by leaning into the curved handle. The levitation candidate rose through the arch without so much as bending at the waist. Higher still, the winged horse folded its wings for one clean heartbeat, then opened them and surged upward.

I had no lean to borrow, no spell around my shoulders, no second set of lungs beating the air for me. I had knees, soles, and nerve. Fortunately, I had brought all three.

I lengthened my stride.

The blue streamers snapped beside my ears. Left caught, right caught, left caught higher. The city wheeled below in slate and copper and green garden squares. My calves began to burn, but the rhythm carried me past the burn: gather, plant, strike; gather, plant, strike. Every footprint flashed under me, a black-rimmed pane of light, and dissolved as I sprang from it.

At the top of the arch hung the high-course bell.

I kicked off my right step, tucked both knees, and entered one glorious second in which I had no support at all. The bell rushed close. I slapped it with my palm.

Its note burst out bright enough to shake my teeth.

Then gravity collected me.

I reached low with my left foot, angled the sole, and sent power down the leg. The mark printed a step under my fall. My knee folded to my chest. I caught, compressed, and rebounded sideways through the rain of the bell’s sound.

Below, Dovek wrote on the examination sheet. Naturally, I assumed it meant *astonishing, perhaps historic*.

The stationary boundary waited ahead.

* * *

It was a square of yellow ribbons stretched between four slender poles on neighboring roofs, enclosing a piece of perfectly good empty air. The levitation candidate already occupied it.

They hung upright in the center while Dovek counted from the launch roof below.

“One.”

No movement. Not a dipped shoulder, not a restless toe.

“Two.”

The candidate’s whole body rested in the working. They could have taken tea.

“Three.”

They drifted out of the square with a small, satisfied turn.

I landed on a step outside the ribbons and bounced once. My calves had opinions now. Both were unprintable.

The safe thing was waiting in my cuff. I could take out the stylus, lift one sole, draw the short closing bar I had shown Dovek, and plant a standing step. For three breaths I would be supported at one foot, knee bent, the rest of me balancing around it. Ungainly, but secure.

Instead, I stepped into the square and began to shuffle.

Left-right-left-right. Each plant was barely longer than a blink. Tiny luminous footprints fluttered one over another, a stack of vanishing glass scales. If I moved quickly enough, I reasoned, I would appear almost still.

“One,” Dovek called.

My ankles churned. I checked a drift toward the upper ribbon with three sharp steps backward, toes pitched down. A spray of black flecks spun away under my heels.

The broom candidate swept along the outer course. The winged horse crossed the sun above. Both were moving. Moving looked dignified.

“Two.”

My left foot landed a thumb’s width too low. My right hammered down to correct it. The foothold flashed hot through the worn sole, and my calf cramped so abruptly I made a sound like a startled kettle.

I smiled toward Dovek. Dovek did not smile back.

The right mark was leaving more black behind than the left. I could feel the answer growing less crisp at the heel, a fractional softness before the air resisted. I lifted the foot higher on the next exchange and saw, between shoe and shining step, a gray nick in the first dark stroke.

“Three.”

I stumbled out of the square with my chest heaving. Behind me, the crowded footprints faded in no rhythm worth admitting to.

“Juni,” Dovek called.

I curved back toward the nearest eave. Dovek had crossed to its ridge, the examination sheet tucked under one arm. Their attention fixed on my feet.

“Your right heel line is thinning.”

So much for altitude protecting a person’s secrets.

“It has another descent in it.”

“You may declare a repair pause. One-point deduction. Inspect both soles, then continue.”

One point. A small price, if points were the only things being weighed. But the rest of the candidates were clean silhouettes against the sky, and I was sweating hard enough to drown a modest insect. A repair on the ridge would make the shoes look provisional. It would make them look like something I had drawn on ordinary leather that morning.

Which, inconveniently, they were.

The rooftop-survey roster closed today. I pictured a term copying elevations from other apprentices’ notebooks while they measured the world with their own eyes.

“I can complete,” I said.

Dovek’s expression did not change, but their questions became precise when they were worried. “Have you inspected the break?”

“I saw it.”

“Is that an inspection?”

The right heel landed. The step arrived a fraction late.

“It is enough.”

That was a lie, although not one I spoke very loudly.

Dovek held my gaze for another beat, then pointed toward the descending course and the white landing ring on the launch roof.

“Proceed.”

* * *

I proceeded too quickly.

Broad descending strides would have spent less ink. I managed four sensible ones.

Then I saw the broom candidate arrowing toward the landing roof below, and my good judgment, already bruised, developed a competitive fever.

I slanted both soles and ran down the sky.

The roofs rose around me. Wind shoved hair into my mouth. I spat it out, crossed my left foot behind my right, and used the angled foothold to slice between two stone finials. On the far side I took a series of fast steps to make up the seconds I had lost at the stationary square.

Each print flashed. Each print carried away a little ink.

The landing ring came into view: a white circle on dark slate, Dovek approaching along the ridge beside it. I was high and fast. I should have begun braking then, with long heel-first steps and room to let each impact spread through my legs.

Instead I took three more strides.

The ring widened below.

I stamped my right heel forward.

For the smallest instant, the air resisted. I felt the failing line catch like a fingernail on cloth.

Then the last black pigment tore away.

My right foot passed through empty air.

The left still held.

All my weight swung around that single bright point. My hip wrenched sideways. The city rolled up one edge of the sky, and I pinwheeled off the left foothold into open space.

I kicked down with my right foot by instinct and fed it power. Nothing answered. Shoe, ink, intention: the chain was broken in the middle.

My left foot struck lower air. Its mark caught.

The impact folded my knee and snapped me around so hard the towers changed places. I pushed off because staying on an open step was impossible, then fell again with my right side leading.

Right: nothing.

Left: a glass-bright blow, one heartbeat of resistance, another twist.

I was not drifting downward like the levitation candidate. No gentle working cradled my ribs. I was descending in a crooked series of one-legged collisions, and every catch scraped more black from the only mark I had left.

I could aim for it by hopping left-footed across the gap. The thought lasted exactly one more impact. The left mark answered soft and ragged. At that rate, it would spend itself long before I reached the gutter.

Dovek stood at the roof’s edge. One hand was raised, ready for my declared stop.

I could call it and fail cleanly. I would be alive, unqualified, and indoors for a term.

Wind clawed tears from my eyes. The roofs below looked small enough to fit in a box.

“Call your stop, Juni!” Dovek shouted.

The shoes had not betrayed me. The mark had announced its wear in black dust and a gray break. Dovek had offered me time. I had spent both warnings to purchase the appearance of ease.

There was no graceful way out.

That helped.

Grace was what had put me here.

My left foot caught again. As the step struck through my leg, I hauled my right knee toward my chest. The sole faced me, close enough to show the wreck of the mark: two strokes intact, the long heel line rubbed open to bare brown leather.

I tore the stylus from my cuff.

The left step vanished.

I dropped, curled around my lifted right shoe, and dragged a black line across its sole.

The city lurched behind it. The nib skated. I had to look at the leather, not the distance below, not Dovek at the roof edge, not the pale horse turning far above with wings spread wide.

My left foot reached.

Plant.

Light cracked under it. My knee buckled. I used that instant to redraw the crossing stroke on the right sole.

Push.

Fall.

I had practiced with a shoe in my lap, never while it was attached to me and I was attached to nothing.

Left again.

The foothold formed crooked because my sole was crooked. It flung me shoulder-first into another drop. A burning fleck peeled from the left heel and sailed upward past my cheek.

I drew the third stroke toward the right toes, opening the little bent door.

The mark lay complete.

It was not enough. Ink waited. Leather waited. Neither could decide what I needed.

I flattened my palm over the sole and sent the warmth of my living hand into the wet strokes. They darkened beneath my fingers.

Then, before fear could bargain with me, I drew the short bar across the opening.

The closing stroke.

The ugly answer.

* * *

My left foot caught once more, faint as thin ice.

I pushed away from it and drove my right sole downward.

Power went from my chest to my hip, down the shaking leg, through the newly charged mark. I committed my weight.

The right shoe struck a fixed step.

Glass-bright resistance burst beneath it and held.

My fall stopped so abruptly my teeth clicked. I hung over one bent knee, right foot planted on a pane no wider than its sole, left leg dangling into all that height. The closed mark gripped only where I had placed it. It did not straighten me. It did not steer me. Every trembling part of my body had to arrange itself around that single point.

Three slow breaths.

That was all this particular foothold could give me.

I dragged in the first.

“Right mark wore through!” I shouted.

The admission flew out across the roofs. It sounded embarrassingly small. It also sounded true.

I kept feeding the fixed step with my attention. Light trembled around my right sole. My thigh began to shake.

One.

I folded over the planted knee. The movement tipped my shoulders past the step, and for a sick instant I felt the whole geometry of my body try to peel sideways. I threw my left arm out, seized my dangling left ankle with the right hand, and hauled its sole toward me.

The mark was almost gone.

I took the second breath and put the stylus down.

Heel to arch. Cross. Open toward the toes.

Three strokes, quick but whole. My lungs strained to release the breath too soon. I held it long enough to press my thumb over the lines and send warmth into them.

The left mark blackened.

Two.

I let the leg drop. The right step quivered under me. Its edges had begun to go transparent.

The third breath came in tasting of ink and cold air.

Below and ahead, the landing roof waited. I could not turn from a closed step. I could only choose the direction in which I left it.

So I stopped trying to look effortless.

I swung both arms behind me. I bent deeper over the fixed right foot until my thigh screamed. I chose the line to the roof, broad and early and honest.

Then I drove forward.

My right sole left the shining patch. I released my attention, and the fixed step dissolved at once behind me.

Three.

For a heartbeat there was no support, only velocity and air. Then my left foot came down, and the refreshed mark struck a clean pane of light.

I pushed into a long right stride. The still-drawn closing bar made another fixed step when I weighted it, but I did not linger; I sprang from it at once and let it vanish. Left, right. Left, right. No fluttering. No imitation of stillness. The distance crossed under the reach of my legs.

The broom candidate circled wide of my path. The levitation candidate watched from above the yellow square, still held as one quiet shape. The winged horse beat upward with the rider low against its neck.

And I ran.

Not like any of them. Not tidily. My renewed footprints shone in a broken staircase over Nine Eaves, some high, some low, each appearing under a foot and nowhere else. The wind shoved me; I answered with a crossed step. Speed pulled me forward; I opened my hips and used the next sole like a slanted wall. When my calves burned, I lengthened the rhythm instead of hiding it.

The landing ring swelled beneath me.

I began braking while it still looked too far away.

Left heel, gentle.

Right heel, gentle.

Each foothold faced forward and caught a portion of my speed. The force climbed through my ankles, knees, hips. I let it. I descended in broad, deliberate steps, spending the marks slowly.

The roof rose to meet me.

My last luminous footprint formed one handspan above the slate. I pushed from it, tucked my knees, and landed with both ordinary shoes inside the white ring.

Momentum carried me three stumbling paces. I windmilled, recovered, and remained upright.

For several seconds, this seemed an unreasonable amount of success.

Then my legs sat me down.

* * *

Dovek came to stand at the edge of the ring.

I lay on my back with my knees up. “If that was the posture component, I request a merciful interpretation.”

“Your stationary-control component failed.”

“Ah.”

“You identified visible wear and declined an offered repair pause.”

“I remember that less fondly.”

“You continued into a high-load brake without an adequate margin.”

“Are we approaching a point?” I asked.

“We are circling it to ensure you recognize the terrain.”

I pushed myself upright. Both soles were visible beyond my knees: the right mark dark and closed, the left newly opened, the leather around them scuffed and dusty. They looked homemade because I had made them. They looked temporary because they were. Neither fact seemed quite as shameful from the ground.

Dovek crouched and set the sheet on the slate between us.

“You also diagnosed an asymmetric failure while under load,” they said. “You restored both supports without external intervention. You stated the fault, used your stationary measure correctly, altered your stride to preserve the repair, and completed a controlled landing.”

The wind lifted one corner of the paper.

Beside *stationary control*, Dovek had written a mark I did not enjoy.

Beside *judgment under failure*, they had written one I did.

At the bottom was a pass.

I read it twice, in case the second reading revealed an educational cruelty.

“The rooftop-survey practicum?” I asked.

“You are qualified.”

Dovek held up a finger before I could become unbearable. “For long crossings, you will carry enough physical margin to finish with half the drawing intact. You will practice declared repairs. And you will not confuse hiding effort with demonstrating control.”

I looked at the failed mark beside the stationary component.

Earlier, I would have argued that mark upward. Now my right thigh was still shaking from three breaths on nothing.

“That seems fair,” I said.

Dovek gave me the sheet.

Out beyond the roof, my path was disappearing. The crowded blur inside the yellow square had already gone. Lower down, the desperate left-foot catches faded one by one, a crooked drop written in panes of black-edged light. Last to vanish were the broad steps of my return, climbing and slanting toward the ring in an irregular rhythm no broom, levitation working, or pair of wings would ever have made.

I had wanted a smooth line across the sky.

What remained was better: an honest record of every place I had found something to stand on, and every place I had not.
