# The Blue Fires of Bittermouth

I took a clean shirt and the money in my sock drawer. Anybody who spends a shift at Bittermouth has yellow cuffs and the sort of smell you take into a house on a visit and get called on the following morning to account for. I wanted the first person with a car to think I was just some guy. I folded the shirt around the money and put it in my bag. I left my brother Rian’s boots where he could find them, which was optimistic of me, given that the earthquake had put half a staircase on top of him.

“Take the radio if you’re going for help,” Merrin had said. It was still on her desk. I’d thought I might go as far as the first town and get something to eat before I did any talking. At twenty-two I had still never bought lunch in a place where you sat down first and they brought you a glass of water. Rian had said we’d do it together, as soon as we saved enough to go somewhere that took a second bus to get to.

At one in the morning I could see the camp lights down behind me, so ordinary among the blue fires where the sulfur burned. I was trying a shelf under the road. The quake had taken out a whole bend, and above the gap, where I’d tried climbing before, there was forty feet of smooth black rock. The shelf looked possible. I could still make out the white paint where the road picked up again. On my watch it was Wednesday, 1:17.

I had barely moved my other boot onto the shelf when it tipped. Below me the slope ran toward a dark stand of trees outside the crater. I saw brick among the rocks as I went down, an arch in the outer face of the mountain. Two rings showed at the top where they’d hung the old freight doors. On the maps in the cage house the bore was shown as sealed at both ends. In the light of my headlamp I could see that the arch stood open.

When I came to rest my chin was pressed against the inside of my thigh. I tried to sit up and felt my ribs move, one edge scraping past another with a horrible small click you could have made with a fingernail. I could see one hand and the blood on the cuff behind it. There was hardly any distance between it and my mouth. I put everything I had into bringing my fingers up to my lips so I could warm them. I wanted that one thing badly enough that the next breath slipped my mind. I tried to take it when I remembered. I died there trying to get my mouth to do what I meant.

I woke in the cage house at 6:12 on Tuesday morning. The shift roster lay whole under Merrin’s mug. At noon she would tear the wet corner trying to peel it off the desk. I got my head and both elbows up in time to see the coffee go over its edge.

“Where’s my charger? I’ve left Rian five messages. Yours doesn’t fit my phone.”

I lifted my elbows again to make sure. There was blood in neither sleeve. Through the window I could see the camp on its ledge, the cage hanging just outside its top stop, and the ragged end of the main stair where the quake had torn it out twenty minutes before I woke. The landing the cage passed on its way down was still there. People were moving about on it in the dust. Above our roofs the saddle looked as solid as it always had from here.

“In my bag,” I said. She pulled it over by the strap. The shirt was still in my drawer. The bag held her charger and the three breakfast rolls she’d bought just before the quake.

There were a couple of regular ways out of Bittermouth. Across a narrow reach of the crater lake lay an old landing with a path that wound up a fold in the wall and over the rim. I’d tried taking a boat straight across and could tell exactly how far the boards of the landing stuck out of the rubble. A strip of bare bank showed beyond it when the blue fires were high. I thought I could put in there if I came around the side. After seeing the open arch from the road, I wanted a look at what the inner wall hid, too.

I’d lost a good many afternoons waiting for help by that lake. We could get through to people by radio. Sooner or later they’d tell us their trucks had come as far as the break. The cage only went as high as camp, but I’d climbed above it until there was nothing above my hands I could hold. One night I’d stayed with Merrin in the cage house, the highest room in camp. We’d watched the far shore of the lake go away in the haze. By the time we couldn’t see the cage outside the window, her eyes were running. She kept wiping at them with the dry corner of the roster. The air burned the whole way down when I drew a breath. Merrin held my face when my jaw started to jump. I died in the chair with her thumb on my chin. I woke with my mouth full of spit and her coffee still in the mug.

The man who kept the workboat lent it to me that evening. Years ago he’d offered to teach me a card trick while we sat waiting for a message from outside. By now I knew all his tricks. Sometimes I let him get as far as dealing out the queens, because he liked to explain what he was about to get wrong. He didn’t ask when I would be back with the boat. He came down and set my bag in it himself.

I kept close to the wall after dark. The shadow of the broken stair crossed the inlet when the fires flared, and I could see the bare bank beyond the ruin of the landing. Under the overhang I let the boat drift. I could hear water coming in under a piece of loose rock somewhere above me. Then came two strokes of metal on stone. Rian used to hammer twice on the rail when he wanted me to put my shoulder under the other end of a basket.

Somebody answered from close to the water. “Again, Rian! We can hear you down here.” It was a grown man’s voice, hoarse enough to keep breaking. Another joined it. I’d spent hundreds of nights on the stair with those two blows above me, and I’d never heard anybody answer.

I leaned out to look. The hull struck hard enough to lift the bag off the floor. I remember the wood splitting and the cool slap of the water in my left ear. By the time I got my eyes open I was looking up at the underside of the overturned boat. The air there let me take one breath with my neck turned painfully to the side. I tried to make a fist around the edge of a board and lost my grip when I coughed. Water came in under my tongue. I kept trying to swallow it away so I could breathe, even after I couldn’t tell the lake from the inside of my mouth. My watch shone for a while down there. It was 2:54 on Wednesday when I drowned.

On Tuesday morning my cuff was dry. Merrin set the mug down wrong again, and coffee ran between Rian’s name and mine.

***

I went back to the stair late that night by the old footpath from the upper landing. In some lives I’d gone straight down at dawn, and in some I’d let the searchers go first and listened to them come back with everybody’s name except Rian’s. Once he tried to ask me to go down to the weighing shelf. I said I’d go once I’d got him out. Most of the time I kept asking him what he could feel under the rock. When he knocked, I still hurried. He’d been twenty-four for nine days. I’d bought him a birthday card with a miserable little dog on it and kept leaving it different places where he’d have to see it again.

Below the break, where the path forked, the old stair rose into a blind notch in the rock. The lower path went around the shoulder out of sight. I’d always climbed up toward the hammer. Its handle stuck out past Rian’s wrist now, in the light of my lamp, where he’d laid his arm along the edge of the slab. He could get about three inches of swing on it. The effort he made to pull it out of my way told me that he thought I was going to try to get under the slab with him.

“Leave your arm there,” I said. “Have you got room for your head if I move this piece?”

“Come round the other side. I want a drink before we start on all that.”

I knew what the rock would do if I got it to move. I’d seen the whole hanging length of stair come down, and known from the noise when it reached the bottom of the fork. Even so I put my hand under a piece of it and looked for somewhere I could stand to take the weight. Then I unscrewed the bottle and went around to him. I had to pinch a fold in my sleeve and soak it for him to suck, because he couldn’t get his head far enough up to drink.

“Who was answering you down there?” I said.

“You heard them?” He let go of the sleeve. “Three of them got past me when the stair went. I sent them down to the old weighing shelf, out of the stones. Two of them had to be pulled along. I told the one on his feet to come back up and get word to Merrin. He said he wasn’t leaving the others where he couldn’t see them. I could hear him calling while the stairs were still coming down.”

The light on the cliff went blue and faded. I put a boot against the wall to see if I had enough footing to do anything with the stone above his shoulder. Rian watched me. His fingertips kept moving on the handle of the hammer, as if he were trying to put something back exactly where he’d found it.

“You should go while you can see the path,” he said. “The beam on the scale’s got a bend about halfway out. Go down under it. There’s a cold draft coming up from the old track. I wanted the three of them to keep going once they got down there.”

“I’m going to bring somebody back with me.”

“If you’re going up to Merrin, tell her about the money. All my bit’s in the tin with the spare buttons, including the lunch money you gave back. She keeps talking about that room where she could shut the door. I want her to get it if she likes it. Say that part, please. Don’t tell her I want her to get it because it’s cheap.”

“You did say it was cheap.”

“Yeah, it is. Say the other part.”

He let his fingers slide off the hammer. I could get down close enough to put my hand on him now. I wanted to tell him how many times I’d made that last climb and what I would have given for him to say I’d done the right thing coming. He was trying hard to swallow and I found myself making the same movement with my throat. I said I’d tell her. I said it twice because I couldn’t see his face the first time. It was 4:09 on Wednesday when he died. I could still feel the sweat along the edge of his hair where his temple had been against my thumb. I rubbed it off on my trousers and found my hand going back to touch him in the same place.

I’d used up time I should have been listening to the air. The smoke was coming along the wall and the fires were showing blue through it, low down where the path went under the shoulder. I left the hammer by Rian and went that way. Below the bend, the scale beam stuck out over the path at a slant. Under it there was a freight door hanging across an open arch, with a ring of bricks the color of the new dust on my clothes. I knew where the other end of the bore came out. A lamp swung behind the door, and I heard the man from the lake call to the others to look.

I tried to call out to them. I got my mouth open and took in something so sharp that for a few seconds I thought I’d swallowed my tongue. I put my hand into my mouth to get it out of the way and bit down on my own thumb. My knees hit rock. I kept looking at the lamp. It was close enough that if I could get up I’d only have to take perhaps seven steps, and I kept thinking that as the smoke thickened and I began to lose the light. I didn’t get any farther. I died with both hands on the path and my mouth full of blood.

When I woke, I put my hands on the desk and breathed all the way in. The air smelled of bread. Through the open neck of my bag I could see the three rolls, still pale underneath where they’d touched each other in the oven.

***

I took out the charger before Merrin asked. She made room for it by picking up her mug, and the coffee stayed where it was. Outside, the morning search party was waiting beside the cage. From her window she could see the rails run down to the upper landing, the first stop below camp. The cliff bulged out under it. All she could see of the bottom half of the crater from her desk was a strip of green water.

“There’s two coming up from the upper landing,” I said. “One of them’s lost a boot. See if you can find him something to put on before he walks through the yard.”

“They’ve only just started looking down there.” She took the charger from me. “You’re doing that thing again. You got that face on my birthday and made us all stand out in the rain for twenty minutes. Then the truck came through the wall where we’d been eating, and I never even got to ask you how you knew.”

The radio clicked. A man down at the upper landing had two for the cage. One of them was missing his right boot, he said, and Merrin reached for the handset when he asked if we had a spare. She had him give her both names. She ticked them off on the paper and sent the cage down. From the rollers outside came the thin high squeal I knew would last a bit longer than either of us wanted to wait.

“Can you see Rian coming up?” she said.

“I need you to send me down below the broken stair. There’s an old way out under the weighing shelf. We can get people through it today. We’ve got until the air goes bad.”

“There’s a great deal of rock between me and the weighing shelf. What I’m asking is whether you can see Rian coming up in my cage.”

“My super power isn’t actually clairvoyance. I don’t see the future, I live it. Whenever I die I can Groundhog Day and wake up the previous morning in a time loop.”

“Why are you telling me this now?”

“Because I’ve been stuck on today for three hundred years!”

I said it loud enough that somebody outside the door turned round. I had meant to put it more quietly. Looking out past her at the yard, I could have pointed to the patch of wall where I’d spent a year of afternoons trying to teach myself to whistle with two fingers, before admitting that there was somebody in the yard who already knew how and asking him. Two winters before the quake that man had turned me down for a job, and in all the time I’d spent learning to whistle I still hadn’t forgiven him. I caught hold of the desk. It mattered enormously that my hands could hold onto it.

“What happened to you the last time you died?” Merrin said.

“There’s three deaths I have to tell you about. I tried the road first. There’s a shelf under the break that looks as if it goes round to where there’s road again. I tried it and fell, all the way down to the outer slope. From where I landed I could see straight into the freight bore. Maybe there was a door on it before the earthquake. It’s open now. I could see trees below where I was lying. Then I died and I was here and you were trying to get coffee off the roster.”

She picked up her mug and put it down farther from the paper. “Were you going to try to get help?”

“I told you that I was.”

“Would you have?”

I looked down at the bag. There was a small patch of wool where she’d mended the strap after it had frayed. I could remember exactly how soon I’d stopped thinking about her as I climbed. It was around the time I’d started to think about lunch. “I’d packed all my money,” I said. “I wanted to go somewhere and hear nobody ask me where Rian was. I probably would’ve called you when I got there. I felt different about that at different places along the road.”

The rollers stopped squealing. Merrin slid open the hatch in the wall and told the waiting searchers that the cage would be coming up with two in it. She asked one of them to look in the locker for a boot. She left the hatch open when she sat down again. You could smell the rock dust on the man’s coat as he went by.

“I took the boat the next time,” I said.

“Was the landing still there?” she said. “Somebody said they saw it go down.”

“I went to see if I could get round the wreck of it. I kept the boat close to the side, trying to see if there was anything on this face where the outside arch had been. I heard people from under the broken stair calling to Rian. Then I hit a rock and drowned. I started Tuesday again. I went to him on the footpath from the upper landing and he told me he sent them down to the old scale when the stair came down. He’d felt the air coming through underneath. I went to see. There’s a door hanging off the brickwork down there, a proper freight door. I got to see past it before I died. I want you to take the cage down to the far end of its run, with enough people to help carry the two who can’t walk.”

“You’ll go past Rian to get there,” she said. “Can you go up and get him first?”

“It’s the same path up to the fork. I’ve tried lifting the slab that’s on him. The whole stair goes down when you shift it. It goes right through the fork.”

“Leave the stair for a second. How was he when you got there?”

I’d been getting ready to tell her what he said about the tin. I hadn’t decided what to do about the bottle and the wet sleeve, which seemed like terrible things to hand over. I drew up my chair. “I tried to give him a drink of water. He couldn’t get his head up, so I put some on my sleeve. He asked me to leave while I could see the path. He could still hold the hammer at first. By the end I had my hand on his head, and I was telling him I’d speak to you. He died while I was there with him.”

She kept her hand on the charger cord. Her eyes dropped to where the end of it lay between us. “Has he had water this morning?”

“He’ll have the bottle he took down on his shift. It was lying below him when I got there. He couldn’t reach it.”

“All right. I want two people with you. Send somebody back as soon as you’re out and tell me there’s air. I can start sending everybody in the camp down.” She slid the charger into her pocket and took her bottle off the shelf. “Get this to him on the way. Tell him I’m here. He’ll be waiting for me to come down.”

I reached for her hand. She set the water bottle in mine. She looked as young as I could remember her looking, which in my case was a long way back. The last time she asked when I was moving out of our room I’d told her it was when the company went out of business or I’d made a fortune at cards. She’d gone out and looked at a room for herself that same afternoon. She was twenty and she’d paid for the key in installments.

“I can tell you what he said about your room,” I said.

“Tell me when you’re outside. I’d like to hear it then.” She picked up the radio.

***

It was still cold enough for a jacket when we rode past the upper landing. I’d never had three people in the cage so early, all going to the bottom. The two searchers had brought folding litters. There was a wet place between my shoulder blades by the time the cage stopped, because I’d had my back against the bars and my head turned to look up. I’d been in water with about that much room above my face.

At the fork I heard the hammer. There were the same two blows as on the other nights, with about the time between them it took me to turn toward the stair. I’d found Rian with blood all down his collar there. I’d found him in the afternoon saying I was late with his lunch. It was morning now. If I went up, he’d have plenty to say, and I could go on standing by his head while he said it. I was far enough up the first step to see the tip of the hammer head.

“I’ve got water for you,” I called.

“Put it on my side. Mine’s down at the bottom of the steps somewhere.” His voice carried so well that morning I could tell how irritated he was. I stood with my foot twisted on the first step. I wanted to go up and ask him if he could move his head for me this time. I wanted to turn and ask the people behind me to wait for as long as he could talk.

The woman with the litters took the bottle from me. “I can bring that to him. Where do I meet you?”

I showed her the lower path, and turned down it with the other searcher. The lower I went, the farther my brother’s knocking carried, until I could hear it all the way out under the overhang. There was somebody calling back. Without the flame to show it up, the scale beam looked like any piece of old iron sticking out of the wall, until I got sideways to it and could see the bend.

The man who’d called from the weighing shelf stood up when he saw us. He straightened his yellow hat and pointed to a woman lying on an old sack. The other hurt man kept trying to stand up until the woman from the cage came down and told him he could ride. She had the bottle with her again, nearly empty. “I told him it was from Merrin,” she said. “He asked me to bring it back to her.” I put my side of the first litter down beside the sack. When the man from the shelf bent to help, I recognized his voice at speaking distance and could still hear how it had broken over the water.

We went through the open door under the scale. The tunnel was colder than the shelf, even at that time of morning. Far inside I could hear a sound I’d never heard in the crater, the movement of a great many leaves in wind. There were two iron rings, a patch of bright air, and the roots of a tree growing out across the arch. I could see the place where I’d landed on the outer slope. I’d got in among the rocks almost close enough to put my hand on a root. At the sight of it, I missed my end of the litter and grabbed for one of the iron rings. The broken bottom of it cut through my palm. The man from the shelf got the litter up before it could knock against the wall. We carried it down below the wash of loose rock and came back for the other one.

The searchers took word to Merrin and she sent everybody down in the cage, a few at a time. She came out with the last of them, with my bag under one arm. The woman with the litters went back with me as far as the weighing shelf. The hammer had stopped by then, and white air was coming through the stairs. She called Rian by his name twice. It was hard going back through the bore with her. I’d never gone back through a place I knew would get me out while I could still hear somebody try his name.

Some of the workers kept on down the slope to find a road. Merrin and I stayed with the litters that night. From our side of the ridge I could see one end of the saddle, until the haze rose high enough to hide it. I put my hands on my knees whenever I thought of an excuse to get up. Merrin had done up my cut with a strip the searchers gave her. She fell asleep with her knees against mine. At 1:17 I had my hand in the leaf mould beside her. At 2:54 I could still feel the dried blood pull where my hand had swollen. When my watch said 4:09, I put my forehead down on her shoulder and she woke up. I told her about the button tin and what Rian had said about the room. I could remember which words he’d asked me to use and I said them the way he had.

The sky through the trees was the color of the water at the bottom of the lake. I watched the numbers change to 6:12, then again to 6:13. Under the dressing the cut throbbed. Merrin opened my bag and brought out the rolls she’d packed the morning before. She set them between us. She picked up two and left the third in the paper, and when she tried to give me mine she had to wait a while with her arm held out. The sun came up outside Bittermouth while I was still trying to take it from her.
