# Opus review: The Mercy Circuit

Independent audit for a later editing pass. It is not a `review.md` verdict,
does not change canon state, and does not authorize edits by itself.

## Verdict

- Dialogue: REVISE — two Ground failures at key beats, and Serren, Metta and the technician all talk in the same clipped clause-and-terms register.
- Prose: PASS — concrete and well paced; only minor glossing, reversal templates and a few staging slips.
- Continuity and prompt: PASS — the prompt is fully delivered; three minor internal slips (offstage caster release, cordon minutes, a release that doesn't dump).
- Edit scope: heavy-dialogue
- Existing review.md said: Verdict PASS; "Dialogue: PASS". I disagree: it credits each speaker with a different topic, but their voices are the same, and it missed the "Already local-zeroed" and "Mine." failures.

The best dialogue here is the smallest: "No hands," the test-pulse call-and-response, the pause where the technician won't act on an ambiguous sound, the pre-fire checklist, and Bevro's counter talk. The trouble is that the story's central idea, consent handled as a series of explicit checks, has become how everyone talks. Serren, Metta and the technician all speak in verbless rulings, itemised terms and readbacks, so a woman in pain with her dawn pay at stake sounds like a second inspector. On top of that there are two Ground failures (the zeroing exchange when Metta arrives and the inverted "Mine."). Metta voices the outline's thesis once and acts as an explanatory chorus once, the arm-ownership point is stated three times, and Bevro's running minute count loses track of its numbers in the middle. The edit pass should keep every decision, price and number. It should leave the telegraphic compression with the technician alone, give Serren a worker's plain speech that pain can break, give Metta full institutional sentences, cut the thesis lines, and fix the two Ground failures first.

## Dialogue findings

### D1 · Blocking · "Already local-zeroed" contradicts the narration

- Where: L119–L131, Metta arrives during the foil false-clear test
- Text:
> Metta Orun arrives before you can cancel the test.
> "Already local-zeroed."
> "Say when."
> You hold up the deadman key. "Now."
> The glyph vanishes.
- Problem: Ground/uptake. The narration says the test was still live, yet the technician claims it is "already" zeroed. Metta's "Say when" doesn't respond to "already": it asks to be told when something is about to happen. And the glyph only vanishes at "Now," which shows that nothing had been zeroed. The line could be a bluff, but nothing marks it as one, and it would be out of character for the most scrupulous person in the story. The reader can't tell what was zeroed, when, or whether the technician lied to an inspector.
- Fix: Settle one fact. If the authorization was still open, the technician kills it immediately and says so, and Metta checks it on her meter. If something was zeroed earlier, show that in narration before Metta arrives, change "before you can cancel the test", and make Metta's reply a request to see it rather than "Say when". Drop the "Say when"/"Now" call-and-response unless it is a real protocol the story sets up. Illustrative only: "Killing it." You thumb the deadman key and the glyph folds away.

### D2 · Blocking · "Mine." answers the opposite of the question

- Where: L279–L283, shoulder open behind the privacy curtain
- Text:
> "Local display or mine?" you ask.
> "Mine."
> She brings the raw sensory log up as colored bands along her forearm.
- Problem: Ground/referent. In the technician's question, "mine" means the kiosk's display. Serren's "Mine." therefore literally chooses the one place she has refused all night, and only the narration that follows shows she meant her own arm. This technician refused a nod (L53) and an ambiguous sound (L313–L319), yet here accepts a word whose literal reading is the reverse of her wish. That happens at the privacy beat the story's ethics depend on.
- Fix: Make the options unambiguous and let her answer with the noun, or with a gesture the technician confirms aloud. Illustrative only: "Your arm or my screen?" / "Arm."

### D3 · Major · Serren, Metta and the technician share one clause-trading register

- Where: throughout the three-way scenes; e.g. L55 (tray), L143 and L153 (cordon), L181 (harness result), L255 (socket consent); also L91, L229, L245, L399
- Text:
> "Guide the caster. I move the arm. Stop on tone."
> "Live caster with a false-clear reading," Metta says. "Until sealed or bounded."
> "Shielded, no ignition, isolated flex harness. Six minutes."
> "Fine. Prove the rest."
> "Open the shoulder. Don't copy the log."
- Problem: Identical Voices plus uniform fluency under pressure. With the tags covered, the verbless rulings and two-clause imperatives could come from any of the three. Serren nearly collapsed at L59 and could lose her dawn pay, yet she reads terms back like an inspector. She never loses a word, and the only sign of strain over money is the flat "I have two hundred ten." Metta's rulings are interchangeable with the technician's estimates. The outline assigns over-compression to the technician; here it is everyone's default. Correction ping-pong adds to the monotony (L97–L99, L177–L179, L187–L189, L211–L215).
- Fix: Keep the telegraphic compression for the technician alone. Re-voice Serren from her work and her body: dock and cut words, impatience about dawn, plain full sentences between spasms and broken ones during them. Let her pay the cost of admitting she can't cover 330 in how she says it. Give Metta whole sentences that hedge and cite what she saw, as someone who writes reports would. Keep what each line decides (no raw copy, open the shoulder, delete the window); change how it is said. Cut the reflex corrections that only fix wording. Keep the ones that move evidence (L99, L189). Illustrative only: Serren at L55, "You hold the weight, I move it. If it beeps again I freeze." Illustrative only: Metta at L143, "I'm not letting anyone stand in front of a caster that reads foil as empty air. The lines stay until it's sealed or you give me a time limit."

### D4 · Major · The intake becomes a terms-of-service recital with a thesis line

- Where: L81–L93, intake after Serren sits
- Text:
> "If you copy the raw log?"
> "I don't unless you authorize it. If you show it to me, it stays on your arm unless you choose an export."
> "Yours. The caster can be sealed. The arm doesn't become shop property because it came through my curtain."
- Problem: Inert exchange plus Philosopher. L81 has already promised to ask before touching the raw log. Serren's "If you copy the raw log?" gets that promise back, so the pair changes nothing. "And the arm?" is then answered with a property doctrine, which is the story's personhood thesis stated at the first meeting. Metta restates it at L159 ("I don't have authority over your arm"), the narration at L237 ("Her arm is not stock"), and the receipt at L403. Serren's real fear, working at dawn, doesn't come up until L161.
- Fix: Cut L83–L85, or turn her question into one she would actually ask (who sees the log, or where it goes). Keep "Yours." as the answer to "And the arm?" and cut the shop-property sentence. Shorten L81 to price, the possible seal, and her right to stop.

### D5 · Major · Metta's consent speech repeats the thesis and ends on an unresolved "The answer can change."

- Where: L159–L169, Metta crouches by Serren before the harness test
- Text:
> "I can seal the detached caster if it comes off safely. I can also witness a bounded repair and certify tests. I don't have authority over your arm. Do you want to continue here?"
> "Then I continue."
> "The answer can change."
> Serren turns to you. "Harness."
- Problem: Philosopher plus Ground/referent. Metta lists her powers like a statute and repeats the technician's ownership point from L89. "The answer can change." has no stable referent. It could mean Serren can still withdraw, or that Metta's "No" about dawn might become a yes after the tests. Those readings pull the scene in opposite directions, and Serren's "Harness." takes up neither. The line exists to close the exchange with a last word.
- Fix: Decide what Metta is offering and say it concretely. If it is a path to dawn, name the condition. If it is the right to withdraw, the technician has already said so twice, so cut it. Replace "I don't have authority over your arm" with a limit tied to exposure, or drop it. Illustrative only: "Not sealed, no. If the tests hold, I'll look at a limited certificate."

### D6 · Major · Metta asks the story's design question

- Where: L133–L141, beacons set after the foil test
- Text:
> "Hot cargo can block empty space. Foil can clear an occupied cone. Poor contact and field noise corrupt the grip return."
> "Can any channel identify a person, permission, or need?"
> Bevro comes around his counter. "No. Those lines eat my whole front."
- Problem: Philosopher/Exposition. A safety inspector setting a cordon needs to know whether the caster could fire at someone in the cone. "Permission" and "need" are theme, lifted almost word for word from the outline's rule ("cannot recognize persons, hostility, permission, or necessity"). The answer before it is a tidy three-item catalogue of that same rule, and "Hot cargo can block empty space" is hard to parse. Bevro's "No." echoes the technician's "No." as a verbal rhyme, so for a moment he reads as answering Metta too.
- Fix: Let Metta ask the practical bystander question and let the technician answer about this caster. Cut "permission, or need." Trim L135 to the false-clear, which is what justifies the cordon. Start Bevro's objection with something other than "No." Illustrative only: Metta, "If somebody steps into that cone behind a sheet of foil, does it know?"

### D7 · Major · "Can you stop compressing?" is an explanatory chorus

- Where: L185–L193, reading the compatibility record
- Text:
> "Can you stop compressing?" Metta asks.
> Then the safe dump hurts her because its path crosses the arm's feeling path.
- Problem: Explanatory chorus/Exposition. The story already ran this beat at L107–L111, where it was earned: Serren is the layperson who is paying. Here the inspector, the one expert who should follow "socket return" and "ripple", asks for plain words so the reader can get the explanation. The technician then explains Serren's pain to Metta in the third person while Serren sits at the bench. "Compressing" is also the narrator's private word (L107), not a natural one for Metta.
- Fix: Cut Metta's request. If the plain version is needed, Serren should ask for it because it is her pain, and the technician should answer her directly ("hurts you"). Alternatively, keep only the compressed line and let Serren grab the one part she understands. Keep "A dash isn't a fault." Illustrative only: Serren, "Which part of that is the pain?"

### D8 · Major · Bevro's minute count loses its referents mid-story

- Where: L195–L199, after the record; L331–L339, the ten-minute warning
- Text:
> Bevro, from behind the beacon line, says, "Is that six minutes?"
> "Write seven."
> "Seven," you call back.
> "You said six last time."
> "Seven to first result."
> "I wrote seven."
- Problem: Attribution plus Ground/uptake. The six/eight/seven count is Bevro's best trait and sets up the last line, but its middle beats don't track. "Write seven." at L199 is untagged, so the reader can't tell whether Metta is telling Bevro to log it, Bevro is talking to himself, or the technician is conceding, which means no one can tell who won. At L333–L339 the technician raises the estimate, and Bevro objects "You said six last time" as if seven were the low figure. "I wrote seven" could mean last time or now. The result is correction ping-pong over numbers with no point anyone can recover. "You'll always say six" (L155) is also an odd tense for "You always say six."
- Fix: Tag "Write seven." and pair it with a writing beat. Rebuild L331–L339 as two or three lines where Bevro's reply follows from the new estimate, for example padding it because of the last miss. Fix the tense at L155. Keep "I am writing down ten." exactly as it is. Illustrative only: Bevro, "Seven. I'm writing nine."
