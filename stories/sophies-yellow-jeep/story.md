# The Two-Day Repair

The first part I ever changed on the Jeep was the starter. The engine caught when I turned the key, and by the time I took my hand off the horn my neighbour was out in his socks. You can get a tremendous amount of courage out of something that takes two seconds.

Three weeks out of Halifax, on a concession road near Desbarats, I came off the gas and heard a hiss under the hood. The temperature needle was right up at the far end of its little arc. I'd been watching it climb on the theory that it would come back down when the road levelled out. I could see now that the road went on rising. There was a flat patch where a tractor had been turning in and out of a field, and I pulled two wheels onto it and shut the engine off. The hiss went on. The smell of hot coolant came up around the hood, sweet enough to sit at the back of my throat.

There was still enough afternoon to get to Pancake Bay, if I could get going again. For my first road trip alone I'd booked the campsite from my kitchen floor, kneeling on a map that wanted to fold itself back up. In Quebec I'd gone six kilometres back the way I'd come for a paper cup of cherries. There were two dark stains on the map where I'd put the pits down and left them. The next person to unfold the map would be me. After Superior there was the whole rest of the country and then there was Chesterman Beach, where I wanted to put a foot in the Pacific. That afternoon I wanted to find out if a lake you couldn't see across had a smell.

The hiss let up. I lifted the red toolbox onto my thighs and the marks the seat cover had pressed into my skin went pink above the hems of my cutoffs. A corner of the tray caught on the lip of the box and a socket ran off my leg and out the open side of the Jeep. It dropped into grass as high as my shoes. I'd cut my hair to my chin before the trip and there was still a black strand that came across my eyes every time I bent over. I pushed it away with my wrist just as the shadow of a pickup came across the grass.

“Did you lose something?”

“A half-inch socket. I've about got it.”

The pickup stayed where it was. “I saw you from back at the bend,” the man said. “That's quite a yellow.”

I knew how far away you could read yellow. At the sign shop we used it for the things you were meant to catch at ninety with a truck coming the other way. I had even painted the spare to match. I got to my feet and looked back along the road. From where he was I must have been visible in the grass for half a minute.

“You've got a wheel on the road,” he said. “There's a wagon comes over that rise this time of day, and the fellow driving it can't see this patch till he's on top of it. Can you move up any?”

“I think it's a hose. I was going to wait for it to cool.”

He put his arm on the window ledge. “What did it do?”

I told him about the hiss first, in some detail. When I got to the needle he stopped looking up the road and looked at me. I had to say how long I'd watched it, and there was time to hear what I was saying because he let me finish.

“Vern,” he said, and pointed over the field. “I kept my milking shed when I retired. It's on the other side of those bales. I can pull you up to the gate for now. The fellow with the flatbed can come out after he's taken his supper and put it right by the door for you. I've got grass for a tent, if that's what's in the back there.”

“I'm Sophie,” I said. He leaned out the window to hear me and asked how long I wanted the shed. Through his window there was a round bale with the spiral of hay still green in the middle. By then I'd stopped thinking about the socket. “Two days,” I said.

* * *

By Monday morning I was under Vern's maple with cold grass against my shins and a square of plywood to set things on. I'd taken the hose off and held it up to the light and it was sound. It had stayed cold when I ran the Jeep in the yard to find the leak, even after the gauge said the engine was hot. Vern had put his money on the thermostat. I found the same answer in the cooling section of my Haynes and was pleased it had come off a page I owned.

The first bolt came out with a rim of rust just below the head. The second took enough force that I got down lower and put my shoulder into it. My knuckles hit the radiator shroud and the wrench came away with the head of the bolt in it. I set it in the lid of the toolbox. From where I sat it looked almost exactly like the good bolt, as long as I kept my eyes on the part with the wrench marks.

I started a hole in the stub with Vern's drill. I'd bought a set of screw extractors for the trip, five of them in a line in their own plastic case. The smallest one fit. I went to put a bit more force on it and felt it give so suddenly that I thought I'd got the whole thing loose. When I held up the wrench it had the top of the extractor in it. The other end was down inside the hole I'd made.

The shade had moved off my knees and the parts on the plywood were warm to the touch. A wasp worked its way along the wet streak left by the hose. I kept putting my thumb over the place where the bolt had been, and I was doing that when Vern called to ask if I had hands for coffee.

“In a bit,” I said. “I have one that won't come out.”

He came around the open hood with the cups. His overalls had two buttons down the side, and one of them made a little knock against the fender. I sat up. There was a space in the extractor case and the loose end wouldn't stay in it. I held it out to him.

“I broke this off inside it.”

He put the coffee down on the plywood and lowered himself far enough to see the front of the engine. I held my hair back for him to look, as if it was in his way, and got a black mark on the yellow strap of my top when I lowered my hand.

“How long have you been there with that?” he said.

“I don't know. I got everything else off in about an hour.”

I pushed the wrench toward him. He slid it aside to put my coffee in front of me. “There's a machine shop in the Sault that got a piece out of my baler once. I'd had the baler since I was twenty-six and they gave me a card to fill in, wanted to know how long I'd owned it. I had to work it out on the back of the card. I stood there doing the long subtraction with the fellow behind the counter watching. Well, I was thirty years the owner of a machine I couldn't get one piece out of.” He picked up his cup. “Do you want their number?”

I wanted to get it out before anybody at a machine shop saw the hole I'd made. I looked down into it. There was a bit of the broken spiral I could see by putting my face almost to the radiator. It was shiny at one edge. I couldn't get any closer to it than that.

I called the shop from the gate. The man asked if there was any of it he could get hold of. I went back to look again, though I already knew. When I got back to the gate and told him, he said, “I'll need the cylinder head on my bench. It'll be two weeks before I can look at it.”

I'd meant to see if I could move my booking at Pancake Bay as soon as I had the phone out. I stood with my hand on the gate and counted two Saturdays, and then it came to me that he'd said look at it. He was still on the line.

Vern drank his coffee while I told him. When I got to the part about the head, he said I'd want somebody across the fender from me to lift it.

“Could you help me do that?” I said. He went for the key in his pocket with both hands, hooking one thumb in the cloth to get the other hand all the way in. He had a bench inside the shed door where we could set the head down.

The pushrods had to go back where they'd come from. I marked their places on a strip of cardboard with the pencil I used to mark cut lines at work. I held up the cardboard for Vern to pass me the first rod and he took it out so slowly that I could see the oil running down it.

* * *

The head went to the Sault on Tuesday. For the first few mornings I took my coffee down to the gate, where I had enough signal to call the shop. Vern would come down to the gate himself around the time I'd stopped being able to find anything else to ask. We'd carry our cups up to the shed, where he had two chairs. I began to take the one with a rung missing and leave him the one he could hook his heel over.

The drops hitting the shed roof had got farther apart by noon on the second Saturday. I had the map folded to a stretch of the North Channel. If I went straight there I could still get to the market before it shut. Vern came out with his coffee and his keys and said he was getting groceries in Bruce Mines. He could give me a lift to the corner. I told him I'd walk.

When I got to the gate, he was still at the truck. He'd set his cup on the hood to get the door open, and it was steaming in the last of the rain. I turned back and set the two chairs on the dry strip just inside the shed door. Then I went up to the house and poured myself a cup.

“Can you sit with me before you go?” I called.

He sat down with his keys still in his hand. A tractor came up from the fields. I waited for the two bangs the hitch made where the wagon came up onto the road. Vern put his keys down on the floor long enough to wave, although all we could see of the driver from there was the top of his cap. We stayed till the rain quit dripping off the roof. By the time he got back to the truck, there was only time for the short walk to the market. I put the map back in the tent.

At the Johnson Farmers' Market I found Marla eating an apple behind a stack of loaf tins. I'd bought bread from her the week before and watched her wedge a price card under the tins to keep the damp cardboard upright. I set a new one by the till, a scrap of white Dibond I'd lettered in black enamel. “Two brown,” a woman called from the other end of the table, and I started bagging the whole wheat I'd bought last Saturday. Marla caught my eye over the apple. She took another bite.

I stayed through the last of it. A boy counted quarters into my hand and had one left. He told me he was saving it, and went off in the direction of the jam stand. The vendors called to one another as they folded their tables under the pavilion. Somebody backed a buggy up to the end and the horse rattled one of the bowls of water people had put out for dogs. Marla picked up the price card and tried it out on the end of the table.

“That's the first time anybody'll have known the bread was five dollars before they asked me,” she said. “I may have to get a new sentence.”

She brought out a loaf with a shoulder sunk in on one side and started sawing it in two. The bread was still soft enough to bunch under the knife. I thought she meant me to take some to Vern, and was getting my bag out when she drew up a second chair. There were threads of baked-on flour in the folds of her apron. She brushed at the worst of it and sat.

“Do you stay for lunch with him?” she said.

“Vern? We have coffee.” I moved a canvas bag out of the way of my chair. There was a towel coming out of it.

“That's my bag for Superior,” she said. “I'm taking the car up after this. If I take the van I spend half my time on the beach telling people I'm out of bread.” She put a heel of bread on my knee. “Give me a minute before you go. I have to sit down before I get in the car, which is going to sound ridiculous to you.”

I liked the burnt taste at the part where the loaf had sunk in. Marla asked when I'd last driven anywhere. I said it depended if she counted being towed.

“With the whole day in front of you,” she said. “Where'd you go?”

“There was a man selling cherries in Quebec and I went six kilometres past him before I turned round. He kept finding a place for another cherry in the cup. By the time he handed it to me I had to put one in my mouth to get as far as the Jeep. I put the pits on the map because it was right there. The next afternoon when I got in, the whole Jeep still smelled of cherries.”

I had never told anybody that I could smell those cherries in the car for a day and a half. She took the knife back out to cut the other heel and I moved my chair a little closer to hers because by then a man with a broom was going round us.

* * *

By the third Saturday after Vern found me the head was back on. There were fresh threads in the hole where I'd broken off the bolt and a new thermostat inside the housing. I stood in the grass trying to remember what I'd done with the key. The smell of coolant came to me under the maple. There was a thin green line at the seam of the housing, right under the new bolt.

The wrench was on the plywood. I picked it up. I thought of going into the shed and saying it would be another day, and how easy it would be to say that. There was a clean shirt on the line between the tent and the maple. If I stayed I would have to move the line up because the bottom of the shirt had a little green stripe where it touched the grass. I had a good idea by then where to find Vern's stepladder.

I put the wrench back down and bent to wipe the seam. My hair slipped out from behind my ear. Vern came up from the shed with the key, which I had left on the bench.

“It's wet at the place I broke it,” I told him. “Can you see if it comes back while I start it?”

He leaned down to see where I pointed. From the seat I could see the sole of one of his boots below the front fender. I waited till it moved back. The engine turned over a few times before it caught, and the mirror on its long bracket trembled until I could see the whole length of the shed shaking in it. The wheel shook against the heel of my hand. After a while the smell of warm rubber came up from the floor of the Jeep.

The needle moved. I watched it come to rest where I wanted it and tried not to keep looking at it. When Vern came round to the open side, he was smiling.

“How is it?” I said.

“Dry. Come and see.”

We went back around the front. The fresh grey line of the gasket ran right round the side of the housing. Vern held the hood while I let the prop down.

“There's a place to turn at the junction over the hill,” he said. “That's as good a place to go as any.”

I left the tools spread out and drove up the concession. When I got to the patch where I'd stopped, the tractor had pressed two new tracks into it. I stayed on the road and put my foot down a little. I'd forgotten how it felt in the Jeep when the engine took hold and the whole front of it went up with you. Beyond the rise there was more hay, a line of white posts where the field gave out, and the crossroad. The needle was steady. I got as far as putting my hand on the gearshift to go on up to third before I remembered I was supposed to turn.

I parked out of the shade and raised the hood with the rag in my hand. The rag came away dry this time too. As I brought the hood down, Marla came across the grass from her car with a paper bag, which she put on the passenger seat.

“You missed a man who wanted to know who'd made my price sign. I told him I'd known you all summer. He asked why I hadn't got you to make it sooner.”

I stuck the rag into my back pocket and put my hand in the bag. The bread was warm in the middle.

“Do you want to come to my place next Saturday before the market?” she said. “I can get breakfast on the table at seven. There's no sense making a whole pan of eggs for myself.”

“It depends what it does in the morning,” I said.

“Whatever's easiest.” She took the bag back to fold down the top. I stood with a piece of crust between my finger and thumb and thought she might go back to her car.

Vern came out of the shed with the two chairs we'd been using for coffee. He set them down in the bare patch the Jeep had left. There was still room to sit with your legs in the sun.

“You can leave your tent up where it is, if you like,” he said. “I'll get my mower round it. The grass is slowing down by itself, anyhow.”

I was still hoping he'd say I could drive it as far as I liked. He shook one of the chairs by the back until all four legs were on the ground and left his hand on it. Marla tore a bigger piece of crust off the bread and gave it to him.

“After the cows went I came out with my coffee same as ever,” he said. “The second morning I carried it all the way to the back stall. There was a cow back there used to stand with a foot down in the gutter, and I could hear her shift about before I got up to her. I'd put my cup up on the sill before she went and set the foot down. I put the cup up and I stood there waiting. I must have stood quite a while. The coffee was cold by the time I went to drink it. There was a chair nobody was using in the house, so the next morning I brought that out.”

He bit into the bread and looked toward the open door of the shed. I sat down in the chair with the rung missing. I'd been in and out of that shed for three weeks with a drawer to open or a tool I was trying to find. For the first time I wanted to go down to the end of it and ask which stall he'd meant. Next Saturday I could walk to Marla's in my good shoes, come back with a loaf for Vern after market and ask him what he'd called that cow.

Marla leaned against the fender, working a crease out of the bread bag with her thumbnail. I pressed my heel into the grass. I could remember exactly how far down the pedal went before the Jeep pulled at the top of the hill. I'd come back to the yard still thinking about the road past the junction. I wanted the breakfast too. I'd driven out of campsites all the way from Halifax while the people in the next site were setting up their stoves, and I'd never known what any of them were making.

“I'm going to go in the morning,” I said. I could hear a mower starting up down past the house, where you couldn't see the next place for the trees. “I wish I'd said yes when you asked about the eggs.”

“You can say it now,” Marla said. “I still make them when it gets cold.”

I asked where she'd stop if she was going to Superior in the morning.

“Pancake Bay. Walk down the sand,” she said. “Keep going after you think you've gone far enough. You'll hear the trucks and the water together for a while, and then just the water. I always have farther to go than I think.”

She stayed to finish her bread. When she went, she left the rest of it in the Jeep.

Vern got up to take the chairs back into the barn. I asked if I could pitch my tent in his yard on the way back. He stood for a while with the two chairs in his hands. One of them had a bit of long grass caught in the foot.

“We'd put you closer to the barn by then,” he said. “Out of the wind.”

In the morning he brought two cups out before I'd pulled the last peg. We drank standing up. I packed the red box on the passenger floor and put the map on the seat. At the bend the sun was getting at the full length of Vern's shed. I waited at Highway 17 for a truck to go by, and when I turned west the sound of the tires changed under me. Cold air came in below my elbow and blew the ends of my hair against my cheek. It was a good long way before I had to lift my foot again.
