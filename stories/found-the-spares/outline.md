# Provisional direction

First person, brisk and comic, from Bea: an adult puppeteer putting on a paid show in her front room, with her cousin Ned helping. She wants these particular people to come back. After several thin audiences, tonight's advance bookings feel less like vindication than a chance she can still waste. Ned needs spare carved puppet hands; his return from the basement delivers the prompt's line. What frightens Bea about the discovery is Ned's evident interest.

The basement demon made a wager with Bea when she was young and delivered the promised "real theatre" as an absurdly small, empty puppet booth. He voluntarily swore to remain on its boards until a performance there earned real applause, confident she would never make anything worth watching. The pledge binds him, but Bea has offered release and he refuses charity. His pride is his own present choice. The booth is portable: Ned proposes carrying it upstairs, demon and all, so the paying audience and the pledged stage share one room. Keep the history to a short, funny exchange while they move it. No contract puzzle.

The demon can change the size and shape of his own hands. Ned invites a demonstration: fingers become puppet actors, a cupped palm becomes a palace, and a thumb becomes an enormous king whose opening hand-mouth can swallow the whole little court. Their shadows make the room a splendid, frightening theatre. Bea cannot produce this herself. She wants it enough to replace the familiar ending of her comic show, "The King Who Ate Everything," with this unrehearsed transformation. The demon wants the audience to feel his size and choose to applaud him. His spectacle is a real gift, not a morally wrong shortcut.

During the show, the king consumes the court and holds his magnificent, silent victory. Bea assumed the demon would restore her puppets for the closing dance; he thinks that would spoil his great finish. This disagreement comes from her earlier choice, and neither can quietly rewind it. An audience child wants the little dog back; an adult starts gathering coats. Bea must improvise instead of explain. A possible route: her bare hand, made into a rude little creature by Ned, interrupts the huge king. Bea gives up the script and exposes her own unfinished performance; the demon must choose whether to respond and risk looking ridiculous. He remains spectacular and essential while they turn his king's conquest into a comic unraveling. The laugh costs him his unassailable pose, not his talent. Applause can free him, but his hunger for an encore remains troublesome. A concrete audience request to return should also answer Bea's stake, with an untidy price such as being asked to repeat an effect she cannot do without him.

The pledge's wording, hand choreography, title, and last action remain open. The draft can find a better route. No universal demon hierarchy or afterlife geography is implied.
