# Provisional direction

First person, brisk and comic, from Bea: an adult puppeteer putting on a paid show in her front room, with her cousin Ned helping. She wants these particular people to come back. After several thin audiences, tonight's advance bookings feel less like vindication than a chance she can still waste. Ned needs spare carved puppet hands; his return from the basement delivers the prompt's line. What frightens Bea about the discovery is Ned's evident interest.

The basement demon made a wager with Bea when she was young and delivered the promised "real theatre" as an absurdly tiny, empty stage. He voluntarily swore to remain on it until a performance there earned real applause, confident she would never make anything worth watching. The pledge binds him, but she can offer to release him, and he has already refused a release he considers charity. Pride keeps him pressing for a spectacular rematch. He has hands, a voice, and frightening theatrical gifts; he also wants an audience to choose him. Keep this history brief and funny, with his present choices visible. It must not turn into a contract puzzle or a license to treat him as equipment.

Ned discovers that the demon's huge, dexterous hands can do something the small wooden hands cannot. He invites a demonstration rather than assuming obedience. Bea is wary, then genuinely tempted: perhaps tonight could be the show everyone talks about. As they test what can be done from the basement stage, the demon proposes enlarging the performance into an irresistible spectacle. Let Bea see and want a concrete glimpse of it. The cost is that her own show and the audience's ordinary freedom to respond could disappear into his display; the demon's version of applause is closer to conquest than delight.

The pressure comes during a performance, not a debate. A modest piece of stage business fails; the demon offers the grand takeover again. Bea has to keep the audience with an improvisation that requires her skill and Ned's playful nerve, while giving the demon a real part he can accept or refuse. Possible ending: he chooses a smaller but riskier role, actually earns laughter and applause, and discovers how badly he wants an encore. His release or departure should follow the particular performance, with a last comic consequence for Bea. Neither he nor Bea needs to become good or declare a lesson. Preserve Ned's curiosity, the temptation's beauty, and the pleasure of a live show that can go wrong.

The pledge's exact workings, the puppet story, title, and ending remain open. The draft can find a better route. No universal demon hierarchy or afterlife geography is implied.
