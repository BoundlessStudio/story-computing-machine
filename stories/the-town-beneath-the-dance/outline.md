# The Town Beneath the Dance

Provisional direction: a second-person story in which the pleasure of being taught becomes a hunger the four friends cannot quite admit. You arrive in the seaside market town with Nort, Mysie, and Elspie wearing scarlet gowns pieced from the same length of cloth and marigolds in your hair. You have read the sky alone all your life; you want, fiercely, to show someone what you see and have her show you something back. The first midnight dance is funny, intimate, and astonishing. When Mysie teaches you flame and you teach her a constellation, she makes a little burning star. When Nort learns your sky, roots begin to follow it. Teaching changes the teacher as well as the pupil.

The girls of the town join. A generous market woman gives the four lodging. Her daughter asks you to help wake the root of an old fruit tree in their yard: it looks dead, but Nort can hear its living root. Teaching the daughter your awakening gift revives it. The mother gets a beloved part of her home back, and you get to feel another girl's astonishment. You and Mysie push each other toward bigger gifts. Nort then reports something enormous turning beneath the market; a stall has shifted and the roots around the sleeper are being torn. Your sky-reading, carried in Mysie's glowing river, confirms a moving presence below the town. You could stop teaching your waking gift. Instead, you convince yourself a stronger circle will understand it, and teach the daughter an old waking phrase, with Mysie helping carry it in river-light. The daughter teaches another girl before you recognize the consequence. A call answers from beyond the town.

Beneath the market, very ancient living creatures hear what they once heard at the beginning of spring. Their waking brings flowers and abundance, and breaks houses. There is no separate spell to cancel, but Elspie uses her wind to hold the first emergence back, and briefly succeeds. This gives the town a plausible hope that another enlargement of the circle can restrain or steer the bodies and save its homes. The creatures also hear the enlarged working; nobody knows whether the next leap in power will win or wake more. You ask Mysie to admit that uncertainty to your host. She instead says the girls can do it, from the confidence you helped create. You publicly contradict her. The host's home stands in the path; the wonder you gave her and the damage you helped cause pull her toward demanding one more attempt. Her decision to abandon the house and help get girls out must change who survives and who remains welcome. The town can forfeit a possible magical rescue of its homes rather than keep feeding the power, but some residents will reject that choice or leave without forgiving it. The emptying is human action under pressure, not a construction project or a unanimous civic verdict.

Possible ending: the four get their host and the last endangered residents out, but the market is lost. Mysie and you cannot immediately recover the ease of the first night. The living migration continues inland, beautiful, indifferent, and immense. The old words are being sung somewhere neither of you can see; the daughter's earlier lesson has already shown that the four cannot direct their spread. Another girl may ask you to teach her. The prose should discover an answer that changes your honesty toward this particular girl while preserving the dangerous attraction of learning; careful teaching cannot be presented as a cure.
