# The Rattlejaw

When you dislodge a stone on scree you're supposed to shout *Rock!* so the people below can get out of the way. When Delk and I had a slope to ourselves I used to bet him supper I could keep quiet longer than he could. It was generally Delk who paid. I liked to wait until he was well downhill and then roll a stone the size of a turnip straight at him. Since nothing could so much as bruise either of us, I thought this ought to make the game more enjoyable for him. “Some day you'll put that thing in my coat pocket,” he said once, “and I'll still have to buy you supper.” I remember telling him I was happy to play for lunch.

We went up to the Rattlejaw toward the end of summer, when the last of the evening sun came in through a fork in the far cliff and fell on the white stone at the top of the packers' cairn. I had a handsome picture of Delk finding me yawning with my hand on the warm stone, as if I'd been there long enough to fall asleep.

We stood on a ledge where the packers brought their ponies down to the ice. From our side a black rib of rubble curved a long way uphill before it bent back toward the cairn. The ponies kept to the rubble. I could see where a strip of old snow bridged a cleft between two parts of the bend, and how many steps it would save me.

While I knelt to tie my new bootlace a pop came up through the knee I had on the ice. On a warm evening there was no end to the noises the Rattlejaw made. The smallest ones were my favorites. They came from places people had walked over all their lives on the strength of never having heard a thing.

“Let me get there first,” I said. “You can come up to the cairn and find me doing anything you like. Asleep. Taking supper out of my pocket. Say what you'd most dislike to find me doing and I'll have a go at that.”

Delk had already taken the rope out of his bag. He laid it on the stone between us. Most of it was brown with use, but the last coil was still the gold of cut straw and smelled of warm hemp. “If I brace here I can let you out as far as the end of the snow,” he said. “Come put your fingers in this first.”

I got down on my belly and put a finger through the crease he'd found where the snow met the ledge. The snow was that thin. I leaned over the rock to see how far down the next bit of ice was. A thread of water came out of the snow, turned at a stone that would have fit comfortably in my pocket, and went ticking down the crease. I picked up the stone to see if it would be any louder going down. Delk held out his palm and I gave it to him instead. He set it back in its wet little seat.

He had first come over the Rattlejaw before the packers were even running ponies on this side of the range. I knew because four generations of Vessicks had sold him his lunch in the same yard. They were all in the habit of making change between the same two fingers, a trick he said he'd taught the first one. Once in a while he would wave me into the yard ahead of him so he could see if I could make them laugh. I could, though nobody there could be made to laugh so readily as Delk himself on a good day.

“Are you going to make me wear the thing all the way to the cairn?” I said.

“Only till you get to the stones again.” He looked straight out at the snow I'd been watching. “I know there's a nice flat bit right out in the middle. I went after a ewe once because there was a nice flat bit where she was. The ewe climbed right over me and went home, and I lay with a boulder against my leg, looking at my buckle. There were three birds on that buckle. The third one had a beak like a spoon. I used to think I'd get a bit of wire and scratch it to a point if I ever got my hands on it again.” He lifted the end of the rope. “That took me most of the summer. Come on, Marlis.”

I did want to know how he'd got out, and would ordinarily have asked. He had an extraordinary supply of afternoons he could tell you about while you stood halfway into your coat. I looked over his shoulder at the white stone. By the time he was done with me there would be one set of footprints over the snow and a rope running back to him, and if I put my hand on the cairn in the sun he could say he'd got me there. The really maddening thing was that he wouldn't say it.

I made a show of looking down the crease again. There are all kinds of things a person can pretend to look for when she wants someone to take his eyes off her hands. I picked up my end as if to tie it on, slipped it back onto the ledge, and went off across the snow.

The surface was pocked and firm, with a rind of last night's freeze still on it in places. It crunched beautifully underfoot. Delk called from the ledge and I was so busy thinking what a good story I was about to give him that all I took in was the sound of my name. I was going to call back and ask whether we were playing for supper.

Then the snow to the left of my next step sagged away in a smooth oval. I tried to put my foot farther to the right and the rest went down under me.

On the way down I struck ice hard enough to hear it ring. I came to rest sitting upright on a slant of blue ice, with one hand in my lap. There was some snow in the eye of my boot where the lace went through. I picked it out. The boot was still on and my toes could all go about their business inside it. Already I had an idea of how to get up. There was a shelf a jump away with a scalloped edge I could have put the ends of my fingers over. Above that the wall bulged over me. For the last few feet I would need Delk.

When the gold end of Delk's rope came turning over the rim, I stood up and reached to catch it, thinking I could give the whole line a good tug and make him call my name again, but the wall cracked by my ear and the ice under my boots slid sideways. I went down with it. A slab broke off the wall and tipped over me. I lost sight of the gold end first. The rope itself went on turning gently in a slot of blue.

* * *

I tried to get a shoulder under the slab. My boots went on sliding until my soles met ice. I pushed back with both legs. My shoulders rose and I thought I had it, but the slab had come to rest against the far wall and all I'd found was the last inch between it and me. I still had room to throw my head back and call to Delk. I got his name out three times. I heard it come out thinner each time, and on the third I was so angry at it I would have bitten it in two.

It grew dark slowly, the way it does in a room when you have left a door open onto another room. I heard an iron point strike, scrape, strike again. I tipped my head back to try his name again. My chin met ice, and the rest of his name went into my collar. I could feel in my throat how loud it ought to have been.

I had a fine thing to say when he got to me. *If I'd known we were going to take our time, I'd have brought a candle. It's practically level from here to the cairn.* There was a place to pause in the middle, so he'd have time to decide whether I'd be idiot enough to try it.

That evening the blows went on for minutes at a time. I pushed with the foot I'd laced up through every one of those stretches until the foot stayed exactly where I'd put it. The next time I tried I couldn't tell whether I'd pushed at all. I felt the knot of the lace against the top of my foot.

By the time the light above me went out altogether I'd arranged for us to be dining like packers under the ice, with two nosebags to dip into and a table Delk could lay out with the spoon from that bird's beak. The joke got worse and better each time I thought of it. It gave him so much work to do when he got there. The spoon would get him. It took even less on a day when he hadn't had to dig you out of anything.

I wanted to say *Delk* loud enough for him to hear before I went on to the rest. The tip of my tongue still reached the backs of my front teeth. I was putting it up there again when I heard a single hard blow.

I kept quiet for the next one, saving the whole of what I was going to say. I was careful about that because his chisel made so much more noise than I could. There was the small, irregular crackle a great way off that had been going on since the first fall. I had to shut my eyes to keep from listening to it. I opened them again and waited with my tongue against the back of my teeth, where it could stay comfortably for the length of time it took an ordinary person to draw a breath. I entertained a number of opinions about how long Delk ought to take to raise his arm.

It took a long time to let my tongue down.

I thought he might be bringing the rope in. I could see him on the ledge taking it up hand over hand in the dark, and then I could see him looking back at a light in the valley. By then there ought to have been a light over the door in the packers' yard. It was put there to keep latecomers from barking their shins against the hitching rail. Delk had the length of that rail by heart and could walk it in the dark. I tried to put him there, with the light over the door and his rope loose in his hands, but I couldn't get him to go in.

I began to count the mornings by taking a walk in my head. From the yard where we'd bought lunch, there was a stretch of path among alders, a split stone in a sheep pasture, and a rise where a tuft of black wool used to catch on the fence and travel along it a little on windy days. I'd get to the split stone and turn around because I couldn't remember if I'd passed the alders, or I'd find myself on the ridge still trying to spend the coin Delk had given me in the yard. I did worse with my eyes open. Once I watched what I made out to be a pale square of dawn for as long as I could hold the thought of watching, and when I began the walk again I found it occupying the same place in front of my eye. It went with me when I turned my eye.

There was only one morning I could be sure of and it belonged to the day I'd fallen. A fly at breakfast kept trying to get onto the inside of my cup. I could make that fly walk clean around the rim. I could turn the cup in my hand and the fly would always walk the same way.

There was a piece of slate in the ice beside my mouth. I knew it first by a corner that brushed my upper lip whenever I tried to say something. Later I could rest against it without speaking. It worked along my cheek to the corner of my eye. The first time I felt it there I wanted to shove away and get clear. When the slate moved on, there was a fresh patch of cold to learn.

Once there was a sound under me that went on until I could feel it in all my teeth at once. I thought the whole side of the valley was coming away. It left me with my left ear folded against my head. The ear stayed folded. When the ice shifted a little again I felt the tip of it stir and then the rest came slowly unfolded, a sensation I was glad to have to myself for as long as it lasted.

I caught myself looking forward to coming up against the slate again, and by then I could no longer have said which way it had gone. I tried to think of a thing I knew that took a year. A lamb used to take a year to become the sheep on the hill, and a pine would make a ring, and the packers from the yard would put up the red shutters in time for snow. I could say those things in my head and get from one to the next, but I couldn't make a lamb any older than it was at the beginning of the thought. If I wanted to see it bigger I had to remember a different lamb.

At last there was water on my face. The drop ran in at my ear where the ice had drawn back. I made what I meant to be a rude noise at the second drop, and heard it faintly come back. There was a space about the size of a folded coat beyond my head. When I lifted my chin, a new drop ran across my mouth and I could follow it over my skin clear to my shoulder. It tasted of ground stone. I wanted another so badly I shoved my cheek hard into the ice.

In the next stretch of thaw I got my right thumb free. If I hooked it over a lip in the ice I could pull until one shoulder rose. I had expected to be able to do anything as soon as I could move. The first time my shoulder came back down I lay there for a while with my thumb sticking straight up. It looked as though I were signaling to someone that I'd found the place entirely to my liking. I drew a breath to laugh and the cold went down so far inside me I forgot what I was laughing at.

A slit of light opened just above my head. It narrowed and widened, and through it I saw a long white thread of cloud that held its place however much the ice moved. A small fly went across it with its legs down. There was a shelf of ice just below the slit, and I saw the climb all at once, the way I'd see a line up rock while Delk was still getting his pack down. From the shelf I could work my way out sideways. When I put the heel of my hand on the shelf it gave a little. It was no more than the give you get on a branch when you reach off the trunk of a tree. I'd climbed on worse.

Water crossed the back of my neck. I put my hand down where it was running and found a rough grain that came away under my nail. My fingers went into a pool with a gritty bottom. I spread my whole palm and leaned on it till my shoulder lifted. The rock held where it was. At the end of my reach the water slipped out of the pool past my fingers. I heard it drip farther off in the dark.

I could still get my hand back up on the shelf. I did it once to be sure. There was a whiteness beyond the slit which I knew was sun on something outside, and I thought if I went out that way I could stand up facing the sun. I lifted my chin to see if the fly was still there. I tried Delk's name. I shut my mouth to listen for the short scrape of his chisel.

I pulled myself up till my face was in the light. There was a space behind the shelf that would just take my knee. Once I put it up there I'd have to come out the far side or back the way I'd come. I held my head still and watched the ice on the far side creep closer, until a black fleck in it passed the end of my thumb. Then it went back the way it had come. There was room for a knee the whole time. I watched to see if it would go farther next time. I still had my hand over the shelf when the water began to drip right through my sleeve.

The shelf was still in the light when I turned my face down to the water. I put my hand back where it had held me up and pulled myself the length of my fingers before my boot hung up. I lay on my side trying to draw my knee up without knocking ice down over the boot. The water ran into my sleeve and I found it was warmer than the ice. I turned my wrist and held the underside of it in the current. When I tried again I could get an elbow on the rock. The boot came with me.

There was a run of brown water spreading thinly over the rock when I got low enough to see it. There was room for one shoulder at a time. I went the whole length of my arm without finding anything to grip, then felt a shallow dish in the stone just wide enough for my palm. I took my weight on that hand and got one knee out in the open.

* * *

I had come out where there was still evening sun on the rock. I lay flat on it. One ear was full of water and when I turned toward the warm stone the water ran out and the wind went up inside it.

The open part of the valley had got much bigger. There was a wood on a rise below me where I remembered nothing but the pitched and dirty snout of the Rattlejaw. Beyond the wood somebody had planted rows of pear trees right down to a line of sun on the river. The wind smelled of resin, and after several breaths I got to the smell of cut grass behind it. I sat up and reached around to see how far my arm would go. My fingers came to rest against a wet scrap of coat at my other shoulder. I brought the arm back around and did it again.

There was still a river, at least. There was still a fork in the far cliff. At first I couldn't believe the pile of stones up there was the packers' cairn. It stood at the top of a stripe of bare rock twice as high as the tallest trees below me. The ice had reached that high the last time I saw it. The white stone was gone. About half of the pile was still there, with a long scatter of stones underneath it on the ledge.

I had gone up and down parts of that valley with Delk for the better part of my life before I went into the ice. He had done the same thing without me for longer. I looked for the ledge where we had stood that evening and found it by the dark rock above it, clear across a tumble of boulders that had been under the glacier. It would have taken me hours to get back to the place he had put the rope. From there he could have watched the ice draw down away from him until all he was looking at was rock. I wondered how much of the rock he'd seen. I put my hand down beside me to stand up, and stayed where I was, bearing down hard on the stone. A woman in the valley had brought her flock down to the river that evening, and from where I sat I could hear her call it a little at a time out of the trees. One white back after another moved out into the light.

I could still cross the Rattlejaw. Below the fork, the rock on the other side slanted gently into the ice. Between it and me the lower tongue was broad and clean, with a long lip of gray ice and just beyond it a strip of old snow. I got up to see better and took a few steps out. The snow had that hard, stippled look it gets when it has thawed and stood all night in the cold. I could see how to use it. I could be most of the way across before I lost the sun, and I'd know how to finish even if the other wall went dark. I wanted to get to the Vessicks' yard. If the door was still there I'd go in and hand over whatever I had in my pocket for a bowl of something hot. I'd watch the hand that gave me my change.

There was a path around the lower end of the ice. It went up the flank by rough steps in the rock and would take hours. I went down to the path, where a spruce with its roots lifted lay across the ground the ice had covered. Two young spruce stood upright in the earth between the roots.

Someone had cut through the fallen tree, and I put my hand on the fresh yellow wood. I counted rings with the point of my nail, starting at the bark. I got to fifty and stopped to see how far I'd come. I was a quarter of the way to the middle.

I put one foot up on the first step and looked back into the valley. I still wanted the ice. The first long stride over it would have swung my whole body into that clean light. I could hear what the snow would do under a boot. I put my weight on the foot on the step and reached up to see if the next hold would take me.

From there I could watch the last of the sun on the shortcut. It was the color of the rope when I saw it turning. I took my hand off the rock and put it higher, and the light went off the ice altogether.
