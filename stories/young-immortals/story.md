# The Rattlejaw

Delk and I used to play a game on scree in which the first person to shout *Rock!* owed the other supper. It was generally Delk who paid. I liked to send a stone bowling down first and get the full length of the slope out of it. Since nothing could so much as bruise either of us, I thought this ought to make the game more enjoyable for him. He said I had got it the wrong way round. I remember telling him I was happy to play for lunch.

Late that summer we went up to the Rattlejaw. There was still a little sun in the fork of the far cliff when we got there. The packers had built their cairn there and put a round white stone on top of it. I wanted to get there while the stone was warm. I had a handsome picture of myself greeting Delk with my head on it, contriving to look as though I had been waiting hours.

In a good year the ponies went across on a rib of black stones the glacier had brought down from somewhere higher on the mountain. Their track ran from the ledge where we stood, made a long uphill bend, and came back down to the cairn. A strip of old snow cut straight across that bend, wide enough to take me onto the stones again well ahead of Delk. I knelt to do up the lace on my left boot and a little pop came up through the knee I had on the ice. On a warm evening there was no end to the noises the Rattlejaw made. The smallest ones were my favorites. They came from places people had walked over all their lives on the strength of never having heard a thing.

“Let me get there first,” I said. “You can come up to the cairn and find me doing anything you like. Asleep. Taking supper out of my pocket. Say what you'd most dislike to find me doing and I'll have a go at that.”

Delk had already taken the rope out of his bag. He laid it in coils on the stone between us. The last coil smelled of warm hemp. “If I brace here I can let you out as far as the end of the snow,” he said. “Come put your fingers in this first.”

In the lee of the ledge he had found a crease in the snow. There was ice on one side. On the other, even with my cheek against the rock, I couldn't see the bottom. A thread of water came out of the snow, turned at a stone that would have fit comfortably in my pocket, and went ticking down the crease. I picked up the stone to see if it would be any louder going down. Delk held out his palm and I gave it to him instead. He set it back in its wet little seat.

He had first come over the Rattlejaw before the packers were even running ponies on this side of the range. I knew because four generations of them had sold him his lunch in the same yard. They were all in the habit of making change between the same two fingers, a trick he said he'd taught the first one. Once in a while he would wave me into the yard ahead of him so he could see if I could make them laugh. I could, though nobody there could be made to laugh so readily as Delk himself on a good day.

“Are you going to make me wear the thing all the way to the cairn?” I said.

“Only till you get to the stones again. I know there's a nice flat bit right out in the middle. I went after a ewe once because there was a nice flat bit where she was. The ewe climbed right over me and went home, and I lay with a boulder against my leg, looking at my buckle. There were three birds on that buckle. The third one had a beak like a spoon. I used to think I'd get a bit of wire and scratch it to a point if I ever got my hands on it again.” He lifted the end of the rope. “That took me most of the summer. Come on, Marlis.”

I did want to know how he'd got out, and would ordinarily have asked. He had an extraordinary supply of afternoons he could tell you about while you stood halfway into your coat. I looked over his shoulder at the white stone. By the time he was done with me there would be one set of footprints over the snow and a rope running back to him, and if I put my hand on the cairn in the sun he could say he'd got me there. The really maddening thing was that he wouldn't say it.

I made a show of looking down the crease again. There are all kinds of things a person can pretend to look for when she wants someone to take his eyes off her hands. I picked up my end as if to tie it on, slipped it back onto the ledge, and went off across the snow.

The surface was pocked and firm, with a rind of last night's freeze still on it in places. It crunched beautifully underfoot. I could hear Delk call from the ledge and had time to think what a good story I was about to give him. Then the snow to the left of my next step sagged away in a smooth oval. I tried to put my foot farther to the right and the rest went down under me.

On the way down I struck ice hard enough to hear it ring. I came to rest sitting upright on a slant of blue ice, with one hand in my lap. There was some snow in the eye of my boot where the lace went through. I picked it out. The same boot had made it all the way down with me, and I could move every toe inside it. Already I had an idea of how to get up. There was a shelf a jump away with a scalloped edge I could have put the ends of my fingers over. Above that the wall bulged over me. For the last few feet I would need Delk.

Delk's rope came over the rim, the end turning in the evening light. I stood to reach for it. The wall cracked close by my ear and the slant of ice under my boots let go. I slid down sideways into a tighter part of the crevasse. A slab fell after me. As it rolled toward me it cut the end off from sight, leaving the upper length turning gently in a slot of blue.

* * *

I tried to get a shoulder under the slab. It was the kind of move I could make easily with something firm to push against. My boots went on sliding until my soles met ice. I pushed back with both legs. My shoulders rose and I thought I had it, but the slab had come to rest against the far wall and all I'd found was the last inch between it and me. I still had room to throw my head back and call to Delk. I got his name out three times. I heard it come out thinner each time, and on the third I was so angry at it I would have bitten it in two.

It grew dark slowly, the way it does in a room when you have left a door open onto another room. I heard an iron point strike, scrape, strike again. I tipped my head back to try his name again. My chin met ice, and the rest of it went into my collar. I could feel in my throat how loud it ought to have been.

I had a fine thing to say when he got to me. I'd say I'd found a line under the glacier which would suit us both since he liked to go the long way round. I worked out how I'd say it, with a little pause to let him decide whether I'd be an idiot enough to try. That evening there were stretches of a few minutes when the blows came one after another. I pushed with my left foot through every one of those stretches until the foot stayed exactly where I'd put it. The next time I tried I couldn't tell whether I'd actually done anything. I could still feel the knot in the new lace against the top of my foot.

By the time the light above me went out altogether I'd arranged for us to be dining like packers under the ice, with two nosebags to dip into and a table Delk could lay out with the spoon from that bird's beak. The joke got worse and better each time I thought of it. It gave him so much work to do when he got there. I could still put my tongue into the gap between my teeth. I was getting it into position, for about the fiftieth time, when I heard a single hard blow.

I saved the whole of what I was going to say for the next one. I was careful about that because his chisel made so much more noise than I could. There was the small, irregular crackle a great way off that had been going on since the first fall. I had to shut my eyes to keep from listening to it. I opened them again and waited with my tongue against the back of my teeth, where it could stay comfortably for the length of time it took an ordinary person to draw a breath. I entertained a number of opinions about how long Delk ought to take to raise his arm.

It took a long time to let my tongue down.

I began to count the mornings by taking a walk in my head. From the yard where we'd bought lunch, there was a stretch of path among alders, a split stone in a sheep pasture, and a rise where a tuft of black wool used to catch on the fence and travel along it a little on windy days. In my head the path went from the yard to the ridge and took until dinner at the pace I ordinarily walked. I'd get to the split stone and turn around because I couldn't remember if I'd passed the alders, or I'd find myself on the ridge still trying to spend the coin Delk had given me in the yard. I did worse with my eyes open. Once I watched what I made out to be a pale square of dawn for as long as I could hold the thought of watching, and when I began the walk again I found it occupying the same place in front of my left eye. It went with me when I turned my eye. There was only one morning I could be sure of and it belonged to the day I'd fallen. I knew that because there had been a fly at breakfast that kept trying to get onto the inside of my cup. I could make that fly walk clean around the rim. I could turn the cup in my hand and the fly would always walk the same way.

There was a piece of slate in the ice beside my mouth. I knew it first by a corner that brushed my upper lip whenever I tried to say something. Later I could rest against it without speaking. It worked along my cheek to the corner of my eye. The first time I felt it there I wanted to shove away and get clear, with about as much room to shove as I'd had to reach the rope. When the slate moved on, there was a fresh patch of cold to learn. Once there was an immense sound beneath me, drawn out until it had as little to do with a blow as the dusk had with somebody snuffing a candle. I thought the whole side of the valley was coming away. It left me with my left ear folded against my head. The ear stayed folded. When the ice shifted a little again I felt the tip of it stir and then the rest came slowly unfolded, a sensation I was glad to have to myself for as long as it lasted.

I caught myself looking forward to coming up against the slate again, and by then I could no longer have said which way it had gone. I tried to think of a thing I knew that took a year. A lamb used to take a year to become the sheep on the hill, and a pine would make a ring, and the packers from the yard would put up the red shutters in time for snow. I could say those things in my head and get from one to the next, but I couldn't make a lamb any older than it was at the beginning of the thought. If I wanted to see it bigger I had to remember a different lamb.

At last there was water on my face. The drop ran in at my ear where the ice stood a little way back from it. I made what I meant to be a rude noise at the second drop, and heard it faintly come back. There was a space about the size of a folded coat beyond my head. When I lifted my chin, a new drop ran across my mouth and I could follow it over my skin clear to my shoulder. It tasted of ground stone. I wanted another so badly I shoved my cheek hard into the ice, as if there were a tap in there I could turn by pressing.

In the next stretch of thaw I got my right thumb free. If I hooked it over a lip in the ice I could pull until one shoulder rose. I had expected to be able to do anything as soon as I could move. The first time my shoulder came back down I lay there for a while with my thumb sticking straight up. It looked as though I were signaling to someone that I'd found the place entirely to my liking. I drew a breath to laugh and the cold went down so far inside me I forgot what I was laughing at.

A slit of light opened on my left. It narrowed when the ice shifted and then opened far enough that I could see from wet blue ice straight through to pale blue sky. A small fly went across it with its legs down. Below the slit were two shelves, one easily within reach of the next. I took it all in at once, the way I'd take in a line up rock in the time it took Delk to get his pack down. I just had to drag my elbow up onto the high shelf, and once I had a little weight on it I could bring a knee up. I'd stand in the slit and work my way out sideways. Then the gap narrowed and hid the low shelf. I watched until it widened and the shelf was back in the full light. There was room again.

For a while I tried to hear the iron point out there in the light. I could hear the chisel perfectly well if I thought of it, the short scrape after each blow. I wanted Delk all the more for being able to remember exactly what it sounded like when he was nearly there. I got my mouth open to call, and whatever sound I made went straight up through the slit without coming back. Delk would have left the rope out if he knew I could see the sky. The high shelf was closer than the rope had been. It was smooth and slanted toward my hand.

Water crossed the back of my neck. I let my hand down to where it was running and found a rough grain that came away under my nail. There was more of it a little lower. I could reach lower than I could see, away from the light, and when I swept two fingers through that space I felt a pool with a hard, gritty bottom. If I could follow the rock there was room coming open beside it. I hooked my thumb over the lip and felt for the edge of the pool. At the limit of my reach I felt the water slip past my fingers and out.

I pulled my hand out. It would only take one motion to go the other way, up and into the sky. I lifted my elbow and settled the heel of my hand on the low shelf. Above it there was a whiteness that I knew was the sun on something outside. I could still climb. I could still have the whole thing over before the sun went behind whatever was taking the light.

The high shelf was still in the light when I turned my face down to the water. I took my hand off the ice and spread it on the gritty bottom of the pool. It took my weight. I could pull myself the length of my fingers before my boot hung up. I lay on my side trying once in a while to draw my knee up. The water ran into my sleeve and I found it was warmer than the ice. I turned my wrist and held the underside of it in the current. When I tried again I could get an elbow on the rock, and the water ran straight out along the ends of my fingers. The boot came with me.

There was a run of brown water spreading thinly over the rock when I got low enough to see it. The gap above it admitted one shoulder at a time. I went the whole length of my arm without finding anything to grip, then felt a little dish in the stone. It wasn't deep, but the whole of my palm would fit in it. I took my weight on that hand and got one knee out in the open.

* * *

The open part of the valley had got much bigger. It took me a while to decide whether it was the same valley. There was a wood on a rise below me where I remembered nothing but the pitched and dirty snout of the Rattlejaw. Beyond the wood, in the flat parts, somebody had planted trees in rows right down to a line of sun on the river. The wind smelled of resin, and there was a sweeter smell which I had to be in the air a while before I could put down to cut grass. I turned my face that way and found I could get an arm all the way around myself. I sat down like that, with my hand wetting a scrap of coat still caught at my shoulder.

There was still a river, at least. There was still a fork in the far cliff. It was so high up now that it was some time before I could believe the pile of stones I could make out in it was the packers' cairn. It stood at the top of a stripe of bare rock twice as high as the tallest trees below me, and all the way across the stripe I could see shelves and slopes where we'd have been able to stand if we'd been standing on ice. The white stone was gone. About half of the pile was still there, with a long scatter of stones underneath it on the ledge.

I had gone up and down parts of that valley with Delk for the better part of my life before I went into the ice. He had done the same thing without me for longer. I looked for the ledge where we had stood that evening and found it by the dark rock above it, clear across a tumble of boulders that had been under the glacier. It would have taken me hours to get back to the place he had put the rope. I tried to see what he would have seen from that ledge if he had come back this evening to look for me. The light fell a long way down the rock and lay across a stand of spruce. Some of the big ones had gone down and left their roots standing, and there were trees growing in the earth the roots had carried up with them. The oldest spruce below the mark the glacier had left were more than two hundred years old. I found that out later. A woman in the valley had brought her flock down to the river that evening, and from where I sat I could hear her call it a little at a time out of the trees. One white back after another moved out into the light.

I could still cross the Rattlejaw. Below the fork, the rock on the other side slanted gently into the ice. Between it and me the lower tongue was broad and clean, with a long lip of gray ice and just beyond it a strip of old snow. I got up to see better and took a few steps out. The snow had that hard, stippled look it gets when it has thawed and stood all night in the cold. I could see how to use it. I could be most of the way across before I lost the sun, and I'd know how to finish even if the other wall went dark. I wanted to come into the yard on the far side as I'd meant to come into it that first evening. If the yard was gone I wanted to stand where it had been and see what there was to see.

There was also a way along the flank above me, over rough steps in the rock and around the lower end of the ice. It would take hours. I could see where it entered shadow and where it came out again and what might count as a path after dark. I put one foot up on the first step and looked back into the valley. I still wanted the ice. The first long stride over it would have swung my whole body into that clean light. I could hear what the snow would do under a boot. I put my weight on the foot on the step and reached up to see if the next hold would take me.

From there I could watch the last of the sun on the shortcut. It was the color of the rope when I saw it turning. I took my hand off the rock and put it higher, and the light went off the ice altogether.
