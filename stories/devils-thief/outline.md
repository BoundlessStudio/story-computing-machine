# Promised experience

The explicit premise is a first-person narrator whose parents used the Devil’s Thief, a figure connected to their family tree, to make them behave; the supposed story arrives personally for them at a Halloween college party. I infer pleasure in an ordinary party becoming privately, unmistakably wrong, followed by a consequential encounter that changes what the family warning meant. The phrasing permits rueful humour alongside fear. The Thief’s precise kinship, allegiance, purpose, the scale beyond the party, and a romantic or nonromantic relationship are unspecified.

# Provisional direction

A first-person supernatural caper with an initially frightening ancestor, not a romance. Laurel Penrose has gone to her first college Halloween party after telling her parents she is studying. Her family’s old warning says their ancestor Ephraim, the Devil’s Thief, will carry off children who lie, steal, or answer voices after dark. She intends to have one evening beyond their supervision.

- The party’s apparently excellent eighteenth-century highwayman knows Laurel’s private childhood nickname. His face is the face scratched out of the family tree. He steals the music out of the room and holds it in a closed fist so that only Laurel can hear him. He has come expressly to take her before midnight. Her first attempt to leave lands her back beside him: someone else has stolen the house’s exits.
- Ephraim admits that he is her ancestor. He once stole his baby daughter out of the Devil’s possession; the price of that theft was his own death, which the Devil keeps locked away. He has spent centuries trying to steal it back. He needs the living hand of a descendant to open its hiding place. The childhood warnings preserve what happened to descendants who went with him and did not return. He is neither an innocent rescuer nor a disguised boyfriend.
- Laurel refuses to become another disposable helper. But the Devil’s collector has followed Ephraim to the party and identified her. In the kitchen mirror she sees her own name appearing among names hanging like fruit from the black family tree in the Devil’s treasury. Ephraim brought the danger to her; simply waiting or accepting the collector’s offer of safe passage would leave the rest of her family exposed.
- Laurel forces a bargain with Ephraim: she will help him enter the treasury through the party’s cellar, but he must give her his thief’s coat, whose pockets can carry what ordinary hands cannot. She enters with her own objective. Their uneasy partnership stays under pressure: he can steal sounds, doors, and objects, but cannot take back what someone knowingly gave the Devil, and he has not told her everything.
- Inside, Ephraim reaches for the small stopped watch containing his death. Laurel instead takes the entire family tree, roots and all, into the coat, including the branch bearing her own name. This alerts the collector. Ephraim must choose between the watch and the descendant he meant to use. He abandons the watch to steal the pursuing collector’s footsteps, buying Laurel the run back upstairs. She returns for him rather than accepting an escape purchased with another relative.
- They use the noisy, costumed party as cover for the final theft: Laurel swaps the watch out of the collector’s hand while Ephraim gives himself up as bait. The watch starts; the stolen exits return. Ephraim finally ages into his death without graphic detail. Laurel leaves with the family tree and the coat, having outstolen the man she was raised to fear. In a brief morning call, she answers her mother’s demand that she never behave like him with the truth about whose daughter he stole first. The last image is the coat’s impossible pocket moving beside her as she goes back through campus: the family boogeyman has left her both freedom and a dangerous inheritance.

The chief uncertainty is whether the shift from frightening abductor to compromised ancestor gives enough continuing force to the boogeyman encounter. The intent is to earn that shift through his attempted exploitation and costly final choice, while keeping Laurel’s theft and return decisive. The treasury’s exact theft rules and the collector’s pursuit remain provisional; they must be legible in scenes rather than resolved by a last-minute loophole.
