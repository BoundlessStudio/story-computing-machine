# Promised experience

The explicit premise is a first-person narrator whose parents used the Devil’s Thief, a figure connected to their family tree, to make them behave; the supposed story arrives personally for them at a Halloween college party. I infer pleasure in an ordinary party becoming privately, unmistakably wrong, followed by a consequential encounter that changes what the family warning meant. The phrasing permits rueful humour alongside fear. The Thief’s precise kinship, allegiance, purpose, the scale beyond the party, and a romantic or nonromantic relationship are unspecified.

# Provisional direction

A first-person supernatural caper with a dangerous ancestor. Laurel Penrose has gone to her first college Halloween party after telling her parents she is studying. Her family’s warning says their ancestor Ephraim, the Devil’s Thief, will carry off children who lie, steal, or answer voices after dark. She intends to have one evening beyond their supervision. His eventual alliance with her must not make that warning harmless or false.

- An apparently excellent eighteenth-century highwayman knows Laurel’s private childhood nickname. She recognizes his face from the old portrait beside the crossed-out branch of the paper family tree. He steals the music out of the room and holds it in a closed fist so only she can hear him. He has come expressly to take her before midnight. Her first attempt to leave lands her back beside him: someone else has stolen the house’s exits.
- Ephraim admits he is her ancestor. The Devil stole his infant daughter; Ephraim stole her back. In retaliation the Devil seized his death, held in a stopped watch, and grew a black tree from the blood he left behind. Its branches identify and trace his descendants. Ephraim has repeatedly tried to recover his death. He chooses Laurel on her first Halloween away from the family household: a living descendant can open the inner cabinet, and a student who thinks him imaginary is easier to isolate than the adults still afraid of him. She is personally selected, not the only conceivable helper.
- Ephraim has set a stolen treasury doorway against the party cellar’s wall. It reaches the outer treasury, but his deathless hand cannot open the inner cabinet containing watch and tree. Opening that outer crossing has already let the Devil’s collector follow him into the house. The tree supplies names, not unrestricted access to every world; this door makes pursuit possible tonight. The collector takes the two house exits, visibly keeping them as folded miniature doors on a red ribbon at his belt. A kitchen mirror lets Laurel glimpse her living branch through the open crossing. Ephraim brought this danger; escaping alone would leave the family traceable when another crossing is made.
- Laurel refuses to be another helper who disappears. At the cellar Ephraim closes his hand on her wrist and threatens to take her. The collector is searching the crowded rooms for him and has offered Laurel safe passage if she identifies him. She threatens to scream his location and bring the collector down before he gets near the cabinet. Ephraim can force a struggling hand against a lock, but he cannot make that noisy struggle into a successful burglary; he gives her his coat in exchange for quiet cooperation. Its impossible pockets can hold stolen things, including things too large to carry. These, the living-hand lock, and Ephraim’s demonstrated ability to steal what ordinary people cannot are the operative magic; there is no unused rule about freely given property. Laurel declines the collector’s narrow offer because it protects neither the other students nor her traceable family. Closing the doorway now would strand that pursuer in the house with them. She enters with an objective of her own. Music and footsteps continue overhead; the cellar doorway remains their visible return to the party.
- At the opened cabinet Laurel recognizes two withered branches: the vanished young relatives whose blank places appear on her parents’ tree. She makes Ephraim tell her what happened. On earlier attempts he knowingly shut the escape door on each while fleeing for himself. They died. His original rescue neither undoes nor excuses those choices. Laurel takes the whole tracking tree, roots and all, into the coat rather than surrendering another family member to his private objective. Removing it severs the means of tracing the living family; its withered branches preserve the dead names, without resurrecting their bearers. The collector can still pursue those already in his sight tonight.
- When the collector reaches the cabinet, Ephraim faces his old choice in concrete form: take the watch within reach and leave Laurel caught with the tree, or abandon it and release her. He chooses her. The collector takes the watch; Laurel gets back upstairs. Knowing what Ephraim did to her relatives, she returns for him anyway. This refuses his pattern of expendability; it is not absolution. The final theft happens among the dancers and mistaken costumes, with Ephraim deliberately surrendering as bait. While the collector fastens a chain between his own wrist and Ephraim’s, Laurel palms the watch and the ribbon of folded exits into the thief’s coat. Believing Laurel still trapped in the house and the exits still on his belt, the collector takes his principal prisoner downstairs and fully across the treasury threshold, intending to secure him before returning for her and the tree. Ephraim steals a link from the chain; Laurel, who has followed on the party side, catches his reaching hand and hauls him back across. She pockets the doorway as he clears it, stranding the collector in the treasury. Only then does she unfold the house exits where they belong. The tree can no longer trace the living family, and their pursuer has lost his crossing.
- Laurel starts the watch. Ephraim ages into his death without graphic detail; release does not turn him innocent. In a brief morning call she gives her mother both vanished relatives’ names and the truth of their loss, alongside the older rescue. The warning had a reason; Laurel has changed its outcome. She keeps the tree as evidence and protection and goes back through campus wearing the coat. The final impossible-pocket image carries freedom and a dangerous inheritance, rather than a declaration that everyone misunderstood a harmless ancestor.

The continuing risk is allowing his final changed choice to erase the victims or turn the encounter into a generic vault adventure. The victims’ revelation precedes Laurel’s decisive theft and return; the party remains their access, overhead pressure, and final arena. The thefts and pursuit must be legible in scenes rather than settled by a new last-minute rule. Exact action, voice, and length remain provisional.
