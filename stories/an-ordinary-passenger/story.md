Matsuoka had bought the smallest cake in the shop, but it was still too much cake.

“It serves two,” he said.

“Two people who eat.”

“The woman was quite firm about it.”

Emi pushed the damp grey hair off her forehead and took off her raincoat. On her desk the cake sat between a box of hearing transcripts and her railway ticket. Across its little chocolate plaque, in white icing, someone had written THANK YOU FOR YOUR SERVICE. There was just enough room left for ARAI.

“Does she know you?” Emi asked.

“I am a regular customer.”

“That’s four of these since April.”

“I get a discount.”

He stood with his hands folded below his waist, where the paint had worn off the edges of his thumbs. Matsuoka was built like a short filing cabinet, with a rectangular head and a pair of amber lenses. He had refused a new chassis at the previous budget review because the new model could not comfortably use his kitchen. He was Cabinet Secretary of Japan. He had a kitchen he liked.

At sixty-seven, Emi could still eat half a cake. She cut it down the middle.

“Take half to your choir.”

“The choir doesn’t eat.”

“Then stand in front of the bakery and make a friend.”

“That is almost exactly how I acquired you.”

It was true. Twenty-four years ago he had found her outside a convenience store, trying to eat a steamed bun without taking off her gloves. He had been sent to fetch the new human observer and had mistaken her for a delivery driver. She had let him apologize all the way upstairs before telling him she was early.

She ate a forkful. The cream was good. That annoyed her.

“Has Asano been confirmed?”

Matsuoka looked at the box of transcripts.

“Not quite settled.”

“My train leaves at five twenty.”

“I have the time.”

“I know you have the time. I want you to understand it.”

Outside her window a long, low delivery machine waited at the crossing beside two children in yellow hats. One child had a human face. The other had a blue head with a white patch where a school identification sticker had been peeled off. Both were jumping to touch the button again, although the light had already changed.

Matsuoka said, “You could go tomorrow.”

“No.”

“Of course.”

He went out. His left heel made its little double click in the corridor. He had had it repaired twice and complained that a third repair would make it an entirely different noise.

Emi put down her fork and called her sister.

“Are you on the train?” Fumiko asked.

“It’s one in the afternoon.”

“You said you’d call from the train.”

“I said I’d call before I left.”

“All right. Are you leaving?”

Emi looked at the ticket wallet beneath the cake box. There had been an April ticket in it, and a June ticket, and a ticket for the end of August. This one she had bought herself instead of letting Matsuoka’s office book it.

“I’m leaving today.”

“Good. I’ve got your curtains.”

“My curtains?”

“You said you wanted the green ones. I was at the shop.”

“I haven’t measured the windows.”

“I told them approximately. They’re curtains, Emi. You can have a little too much curtain.”

In the background something fell over. Fumiko said, to someone else, “Behind the cupboard, use the broom,” and returned to the phone.

“The young man’s here to fix the oven. What did you want?”

Emi had wanted to hear her sister being pleased. It seemed an unreasonable thing to say.

“Nothing. I’ll call when I get in.”

“All right. Mind you eat.”

Fumiko hung up.

Emi finished her half of the cake.

***

When Emi was a child, adults still said *replacement*.

A replacement teacher came when two schools combined and there was nobody to teach the youngest class. A replacement driver kept the last bus running. Replacement councillors stood for seats nobody wanted; they were elected by people who were relieved someone had agreed to do it.

Her grandmother had complained about a replacement doctor, then changed clinics when he moved.

There had been arguments. Strikes. A court case about a care worker who wanted to leave a household. Another about who owned the money she had earned. By Emi’s childhood those cases were in schoolbooks, and the teacher telling her about them was a robot who hated being interrupted by the lunch bell.

People stopped saying *replacement* at different times. Emi’s grandmother never quite stopped. Emi had stopped when her robot classmate beat her in the prefectural kanji-reading contest and refused to let her forget it.

The human population had kept falling, although the number of residents rose. The machines built to fill vacancies had wanted different work, or children, or a place with a cheaper rent. A factory opened. A district grew. A district needed a representative. In one election the robot candidates were a novelty; in another they were tiresome incumbents. No one had held a referendum on the eventual result.

Now there were ninety-four million robot citizens and a little over three million human ones. The Prime Minister had begun as a pharmacist. The previous Prime Minister had begun as a forklift and was still saying the country had been easier to handle then.

Emi’s office remained, a human chair at the cabinet table. It had caught things. A health application that interpreted sleep as an interruption in service. A benefits interview whose compulsory waiting room was kept at six degrees. A school lunch contract that provided beautifully accurate nutritional information and no lunch.

It had also put Emi in photographs for twenty-four years.

When she went to Nagasaki to find a flat, the estate agent had asked if she would mind holding the keys for a picture. He had never let a property to a human before. She had agreed, then regretted it when he asked her to take off her coat so the human would be easier to see.

It was Emi who had proposed opening her office to applicants of any construction. The bill took eight months; everyone she met afterward congratulated her as though she had abolished hunger. Asano would be the first robot in her chair. Asano had spent eleven years arguing disability appeals and had interviewed for the job from a chassis that could not climb the ministry’s front steps. Emi had liked her immediately.

Her last hearing had finished in August. She had been off the normal circulation lists for a month, writing handover notes in her packed room.

The handover was what remained. Matsuoka had said Asano’s appointment was awaiting confirmation. Then a review had been needed. Then a department had objected to the review. Matsuoka was always clear about what to do next and less clear about whose department it was.

Since it was Matsuoka, she had waited.

At two, Emi took a brochure for her new flat out of her desk. It had a small balcony. There was a bakery downstairs. In the photograph someone had put a bowl of lemons on a table much too large for the room.

She had told Fumiko she might stay one more year, back in March.

“Then do,” Fumiko had said. “Just stop asking me to look at flats.”

Emi had found this unkind for most of a week.

She put the brochure in her bag. Then she opened the directory, found Asano’s direct number, and called it.

***

“I thought you were on leave,” Asano said.

She was in a busy room. Someone behind her said something about the agricultural vote, and she covered her microphone to answer.

“Sorry. I’ve been trying to find a time to call you. I didn’t want to interrupt your packing.”

“When did you take up the appointment?” Emi asked.

“Monday.”

“This Monday?”

“Yes.” Asano paused. “Is there a problem?”

“Where are you?”

“The cabinet annexe. Your old room isn’t ready yet. I gather you’ve got a lot of books.”

Emi looked at the two boxes she had packed in April.

“I have some notes for you.”

“I’d love them. Could we have an hour next week?”

“I’m leaving for Nagasaki today.”

“Oh, lovely. On the phone, then. Tuesday?”

Emi agreed to Tuesday. Asano gave her a personal number, repeated the time, and went back to the agricultural vote.

For a while Emi sat with the number on her screen.

Across the corridor someone laughed. A trolley went past carrying a potted plant, tied upright to keep it from falling. The country continued to conduct its business.

She called Matsuoka’s office.

“He’s in committee,” his assistant said.

“Ask him to come here.”

“Is it urgent?”

“Yes.”

He arrived at four seventeen, carrying a packet of photographs from her reception in June. He put them on the cake box. She had asked for them twice, months ago.

“Asano is in the annexe,” Emi said.

Matsuoka’s lenses turned toward her desk.

“She’s in the appointment. She’s been there since Monday.”

“Yes.”

“When did you know?”

“The confirmation came on Friday.”

“Last Friday.”

“Yes.”

Emi waited. He moved one hand a few centimetres and put it back.

“You told me on Monday she was still being vetted.”

“Yes.”

“And yesterday you said there was an objection from Personnel.”

“There was an objection. It concerned her parking allocation.”

Emi put her hands flat on the desk. She wanted to throw something, but everything left in the room belonged to her.

“Where’s my release letter?”

“In my office.”

“Bring it.”

“Emi, there is nothing preventing you from—”

“Bring it.”

He went. She heard his double step diminish, stop, return. The letter released her from duty the previous Friday; her pay and building pass ran to the end of the month. Matsuoka had signed it. The last paragraph thanked her for an exemplary career in public service. She had once helped write the paragraph for someone else.

Matsuoka laid it beside her ticket.

“You knew I was waiting for this.”

“I know.”

“Have the others been like this? April? June?”

“No. In April there was no approved successor. In June the bill had not passed. In August the appointments panel had not agreed.”

“You can understand why I’m asking.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the clock. Four thirty-two. If she left now, if the taxi lane was clear, if she did not have to wait at security with the boxes, she could make the train. Her suitcase was in the building’s cloakroom. The movers had already sent everything else west.

She stood, put on her coat, and picked up her bag.

Matsuoka stepped away from the door. He had the sense to do that.

She put the bag down again.

“Why?” she said.

He looked smaller beside the empty bookcase. He was the same size he had always been, and she resented noticing.

“I kept thinking you’d come in for another day.”

“Why did you need me to?”

“At first, because of the appointment. Then it was only the weekend. I meant to tell you on Monday.”

“You told me a different lie on Monday.”

“Yes.”

He turned his head toward the window. The lenses did not hide anything, but it was a gesture she knew. He did it before answering a question he disliked in committee.

“You have been talking about Nagasaki for seven years,” he said. “Until you bought the flat, I thought you liked talking about it.”

“I did.”

“When you bought the flat, I realized you meant to go.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

Matsuoka rubbed one thumb over the edge of the other. A tiny flake of grey paint came away.

“I didn’t know when I would see you.”

It was such a small thing to have put under all that machinery.

Emi looked at the cake, the photographs, the letter. Twenty-four years of taking him at his word had made her quicker at her job. She had not needed to read every attachment he summarized, to check every date he gave her. He knew what that trust was worth. He used it every day.

“You could have asked.”

“Yes.”

“I might have said I didn’t know.”

“I was afraid you would.”

“Then you would have had to wait.”

He said nothing.

Through the open door she could see a strip of afternoon light on the corridor carpet. Downstairs they would be setting out the barriers for the evening press conference. Someone would ask about the agricultural vote, which she knew nothing about. Matsuoka would answer.

She had spent years telling Fumiko that the country needed her for another month. Sometimes it had needed the work. Sometimes she had liked coming into this room, where people knew what she was for. She owed her sister an admission of that. Matsuoka had known what it cost her to leave this room.

“You had it on Friday,” she said. “And you watched me come in on Monday.”

“Yes.”

“I had dinner in this room on Tuesday.”

“I know.”

“You brought it.”

He lowered his hands.

The clock said four fifty-eight.

Matsuoka said, “There is another train at seven ten. You would need to change twice. It gets in after midnight.”

“No.”

“The first direct service tomorrow is—”

“I can look up a train.”

“All right.”

She put the letter in her bag. Then she took out the railway ticket and left it on the desk.

“You can repay that.”

“Yes.”

“And tell your assistant where I am. Asano thought I was on leave.”

“I told the office you were working from home.”

Emi shut her bag. She could not think of a useful thing to say to him about that.

“Correct it,” she said.

He picked up his phone and, in front of her, called his assistant. He said that Emi had been working in the building all week, that her service had ended the previous Friday, and that he had withheld the letter telling her so. He asked for the correction to go out that evening. He did not look at her while he made the call.

When he finished, she picked up the two boxes.

“Let me carry those.”

“I’ve got them.”

He held the door. She stopped beside him, close enough to see the paint rubbed smooth around his lens housings.

She had intended to make a joke about his choir. He was to sing a solo that evening, some long ridiculous song he had been practising in lifts when he thought nobody could hear. She had been looking forward to receiving the recording and telling him she had deleted it.

“Goodbye, Matsuoka.”

“Goodbye, Emi.”

He let her pass.

***

At the station hotel the receptionist checked her identity, asked whether she needed a heated room, and gave her a key. Emi said yes to the heat. Nothing else was required of her.

She put her boxes on the little desk and called Fumiko.

“I missed the train.”

There was a pause.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

Emi sat on the edge of the bed.

“Matsuoka had my release letter. He didn’t give it to me.”

“Why?”

“He didn’t want me to go.”

On the screen Fumiko was wearing the dark blue cardigan Emi had bought her. The cuffs were turned back. She had finished with the oven and was writing labels on little paper packets. Seeds, probably. Emi did not know what was growing at this time of year in her sister’s garden.

“That was a rotten thing to do,” Fumiko said.

“Yes.”

“Did you shout?”

“A little.”

“You’re good at it.”

Emi laughed, once, and found she was crying. Fumiko waited while she wiped her face with a hotel tissue.

“I’m coming tomorrow. I’ve bought the ticket. I’m at the station.”

“What time?”

“Just after two.”

“I can’t meet you. It’s the dress rehearsal.”

“I thought that was last week.”

“That was the last play.”

“Oh.”

“There’s a bus from the station. Or a taxi, if you’ve got the boxes. I’ll leave your flat keys with the bakery woman.”

Emi looked at the boxes on the desk. She had thought there would be a small ceremony at the other end: her sister, the new keys, a first meal.

“Shall I come to the rehearsal?”

“You’ll have to sit through the whole thing. We’re starting again from the beginning.”

“I can do that.”

“You can bring those buttons you bought for my cardigan, if you still have them. I’ve got another one coming off.”

“I have them.”

Emi had carried the little packet in her purse for nearly a year.

They agreed on where to meet. After the call she put the purse on top of her bag, where she would see it in the morning.

An email arrived from Matsuoka’s office. The correction was attached. Beneath it he had written that he had repaid the ticket and told Asano what had happened. There was no question at the end.

She read it twice and closed it.

***

In the morning she bought a hot roll from the station bakery. The paper bag said FOR ORGANIC CONSUMPTION in large letters and CHEESE in smaller ones. It was very good.

On the platform a tall silver man asked her whether she was the human who had worked in the government. She said she was, and braced herself.

“Did you ever meet the old Prime Minister?”

“Yes.”

“Was he as annoying in person?”

“More.”

“I knew it.”

He went off toward another carriage, satisfied.

Emi found her seat. She put the boxes on the luggage rack and her raincoat on the seat beside her. On her phone Fumiko had sent a photograph of a pair of green curtains laid across a bed. The pattern was much larger than Emi had expected. She enlarged the photograph and tried to decide whether she liked them.

“Excuse me,” someone said. “That’s my seat.”

Emi gathered up her coat.

“Sorry.”
