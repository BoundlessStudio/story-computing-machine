# An Ordinary Passenger

Provisional direction; the prose may find a better route.

Close third person on Emi Arai, sixty-seven, the human observer attached to Japan’s robot cabinet. She has booked a train to move near her older sister in Nagasaki. For years Emi has put their actual, difficult company off until retirement. Her longtime colleague Matsuoka, a squat robot cabinet secretary with repaired paintwork and excellent political instincts, keeps postponing her retirement reception and the handoff it supposedly requires. Emi has stayed through previous delays because she believed her public duty unfinished. Booking the train is her decision to stop postponing.

Japan’s transformation enters compactly through a final working day and the national history Emi has lived inside. A school could not hire a teacher; a town could not find a mayor; a cabinet could not find enough human candidates. The replacements won civil rights, began families, changed professions, disliked governments, and built successors. Nobody voted to become a robot country. By now roughly one person in thirty is human. Outside the office, Emi meets minority conveniences as well as irritations: a standard service expects a machine body, a stranger treats her as a representative when she is trying to travel, and some things simply work. There are still millions of humans. The robots have a society, not a caretaker performance staged for those remaining.

Emi believes her office has one final constitutional duty: train and certify the successor to the cabinet’s only human seat. Faced with another postponement, she goes around Matsuoka and contacts that successor herself. She discovers the cabinet has already removed the human-only requirement and the successor was certified before she booked this train. Matsuoka has withheld the completed arrangements. The obligation holding Emi in place was discharged. His interference is personal: he fears that once she no longer has to come, she will stop seeing him, and he has used the country’s need for a human observer to avoid asking whether their friendship exists beyond their jobs. Show their specific mutual enjoyment and his independent life beforehand; this is one person’s dishonest act, not a species lesson about emotion.

They quarrel in a nearly emptied office with a retirement cake nobody there except Emi can eat. Emi’s time spent establishing the facts and forcing the handoff costs her the booked train. Matsuoka must surrender the completed information and name when he had it, without making her first promise a visit or forgive him. She recognizes the usefulness of his public-duty excuse because she has used it to defer her sister. That recognition sharpens the anger; she postponed her own time, while he took a further choice away from her. The country’s continuity was not at stake.

The departure should leave their personal quarrel unfinished. No cancelled second seat, immediate conciliatory invitation, or speech. Emi’s call to her sister reveals that closeness has not been stored intact for her arrival: her sister has an actual life and has not kept an empty room waiting. Emi must make the journey and begin, without being owed an uncomplicated homecoming. A last brief carriage encounter may let her leave as an ordinary passenger among the other Japanese citizens, if the prose needs it; stop before explaining that effect. The country will continue without requiring a human at its head or its elbow.
