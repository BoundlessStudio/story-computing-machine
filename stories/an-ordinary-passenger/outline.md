# An Ordinary Passenger

Provisional direction; the prose may find a better route.

Close third person on Emi Arai, sixty-seven, the human observer attached to Japan’s robot cabinet. She has booked a train to live near her older sister in Nagasaki. For years their conversations have consisted of putting things off until Emi retires; her sister has begun calling that a medical condition. Emi wants the remaining years to contain more of their actual, difficult company. The office keeps postponing her retirement reception for small, plausible reasons. Her longtime colleague Matsuoka, a squat robot cabinet secretary with repaired paintwork and excellent political instincts, appears to be making the postponements happen.

The country’s transformation enters through things Emi remembers and things she encounters on her final working day. One school could not hire a teacher; one town could not find a mayor; one cabinet could not find enough human candidates. Japan gave the replacements civil rights, and the replacements began families, changed professions, disliked governments, and built successors. Nobody voted to become a robot country. By now roughly one person in thirty is human. Their minority status is lived outside the office too: Emi is offered a seat she does not want, has to look for food among shops selling things robots enjoy, and is publicly treated as a representative of her species when she is trying to catch a train. The robots have a society, not a caretaker performance staged for the remaining humans.

Emi initially thinks a constitutional formality has trapped her: the observer is the cabinet’s last human employee. She discovers the cabinet has already removed the human-only requirement and appointed a robot successor. Her retirement is legally straightforward. Matsuoka has been withholding the completed arrangements. The obstruction is personal. He is afraid that once she no longer has to come to the office, she will stop seeing him; he has dressed the wish to keep his friend as the country’s need for a human witness. He can manage a national dispute but cannot bring himself to ask whether their friendship exists outside their jobs.

The confrontation should cost them something. Emi misses that day’s train while forcing the truth out of him, and she refuses to reassure him by pretending the obstruction was kindness. They have a proper quarrel in a nearly emptied office with a retirement cake nobody there except Emi can eat. Matsuoka tells her the actual departure time of the next train and lets her go. He neither makes a national speech nor receives one.

Possible ending: the next morning Emi buys two seats for a future visit to Nagasaki, then cancels the second before paying. Instead she calls Matsuoka and asks if he wants to visit, and when he is free. He has plans of his own. They find a date. On her train, a robot passenger asks whether the seat beside her is taken; Emi moves her bag and says no. She leaves as one Japanese citizen among the others. The ending must preserve the irritation and finite time under the warmth; the country will go on without requiring a human at its head or at its elbow.
