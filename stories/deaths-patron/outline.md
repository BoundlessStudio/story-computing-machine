# Outline

## Story

- Premise: When an unnamed former hospice nurse answers a midnight knock while caring for their dying father, the god of death says it has not come to take anyone; it offers to become the narrator's patron, lending a consent-bound way for dying people to make one final statement clearly heard by everyone in the room.
- Character want and pressure: The narrator wants to protect their father's agency without becoming death's recruiter or repeating the failure that drove them from hospice work, but the father's worsening condition and a frightened sibling's demand for hospitalization force an immediate choice before the offer can be safely understood.
- Turn: The god refuses to interpret the father's wishes or command the living, so the narrator realizes the patronage offers no escape from moral responsibility; they ask their father directly, receive his consent, and invoke the gift as a trial.
- Ending: After the father's clearly voiced choice to remain home is honored and he dies in comfort, the narrator accepts the god's patronage only under explicit limits protecting consent, refusal, and mortal choice; the closing image has the narrator wait at another doorstep until a dying stranger invites them in.

## Beats

1. Establish the narrator awake beside their father's sickroom, still carrying guilt from leaving hospice work after a family silenced a dying patient; a midnight knock makes them assume the feared moment has arrived.
2. On the doorstep, an unadorned, unsettling visitor identifies itself as a god of death but says it has come neither for the narrator nor for the father: it offers patronage because the narrator has repeatedly treated dying people as people rather than problems to manage.
3. The god defines its bounded offer: when a dying adult understands the request and freely consents, the narrator may let that person speak one intended statement so every witness hears it clearly; the gift cannot predict, hasten, delay, or reverse death, compel obedience, or decide what is right.
4. The narrator refuses, accusing the god of exploiting the father's last night and demanding proof that patronage is not ownership. The god remains outside and answers that its authority reaches the threshold, not the choices the living make on either side of it.
5. The father's breathing worsens as the narrator's sibling arrives and insists on sending him to hospital despite his documented comfort plan; his strained speech cannot settle the argument, and the narrator fears that invoking the offer would itself declare him lost.
6. The god will not translate or presume. The narrator asks the father a simple consent question he can answer, receives an unambiguous yes, and invokes the gift once while the sibling witnesses it.
7. The father clearly says he wants symptom relief at home, does not want his children to turn fear into treatment he declined, and does not want the narrator blamed for carrying out his choice. The statement breaks the stalemate without erasing anyone's grief.
8. The siblings call the existing care support, keep the father comfortable, and reconcile enough to sit with him until his quiet death near dawn; the god neither touches him nor explains what follows.
9. In the aftermath, the narrator asks whether their father is safe in the afterlife. The god declines to claim knowledge or authority it does not possess, confirming that its patronage is witness-work rather than ownership of the dead.
10. The narrator negotiates and accepts: consent is required every time, one statement is all the gift supplies, no life may be traded or scheduled, the narrator may refuse, and neither patron nor witness may command the living. Later, at another unnamed home, the narrator knocks and waits for an invitation before crossing the threshold.
