# The Briar’s Terms — provisional direction

The promised pleasure is to wake inside a privately beloved fairytale and discover that its terrifyingly devoted elven lover is real, beautiful, and much too ready to fight the wrong enemy. The explicit requirements are the waking, the narrator’s fairytale world, an intense male elven lover, and the explanation of why magic cannot simply fix the narrator’s disability. I infer romantic charge, bodily authority, wonder, and a payoff in which his devotion becomes more useful without becoming less fierce. The prompt does not prescribe a diagnosis, a treatment refusal, or a public crisis. This direction uses an intimate conflict inside a consequential fairytale adventure, with tenderness and some dry humor rather than a lecture.

The adult first-person narrator, provisionally named Dulcie, is thirty-four. An incomplete spinal-cord injury years earlier left her able to walk short distances with two forearm crutches; she uses a wheelchair for longer distances and experiences leg spasms and variable fatigue. This is one person’s situation. She would gladly consider a safe treatment that helped. Her elven lover, Rhaedren, is the formidable adult lord of the Briarwood. She began inventing his world before her injury; their seven-year relationship developed through dreams in which she could move however she imagined. He has known her intimately but has not met this waking body. The dream relationship is real to both of them; neither has been secretly directing the other’s choices.

Possible movement:

- She wakes in his bed beneath living white branches. The lover she knows recognizes her and is overwhelmed that she has finally arrived bodily. A small, involuntary movement of her legs interrupts their reunion. He assumes the crossing injured her or someone cursed her. His first offer of a healing spell requires her assent; he stops when she says no. Her earthly crutches arrived with her, but her wheelchair did not. She wants, urgently and rather selfishly, to see the starflowers open beyond the Briarwood tonight: she invented their once-a-century flowering and has never been there awake.
- He proposes repairing her before they go. She explains through his concrete questions rather than a polished speech: this happened years ago; his mending closes fresh injuries, whereas her spinal injury has already healed with damage. He confirms his own practice’s limit, shown in an old scar he cannot erase. Research into a different working is a possibility they can consider; his familiar spell cannot do it. Neither of them can order her body to comply with tonight’s timetable. Seed the flowering’s deadline here, and his sincere pride in the old love trial. Fold the world’s authorship into their shared history: he knows her notebooks, but they have long understood that he and his world exceed her descriptions. They still disagree about whether she invented or first glimpsed this place.
- They start toward the flowering in a supported seat on his moon-hind, with her directing her position and taking the reins. The Briarwood’s old lovers’ path refuses mounted travelers and demands the barefoot crossing she wrote at sixteen. This is the only route to the flowering; show why. He accepts that she cannot walk it but wants to defer their crossing rather than violate the tradition. That tests a different belief from his original healing assumption. She recognizes her own careless sentence in the thorns, quotes it, and refuses to wait a century for admission. Their shared desire must matter more than the test’s inherited authority.
- He can command the Briarwood, but has always honored this proof of love; his own parents’ crossing is part of its emotional claim. She asks him to use the power he actually has. He breaks the lovers’ path open into a broad moonlit ride, with specific magical resistance and a lasting decision that the trial will never bar anyone again. Keep that decision intimate, without witnesses or a policy subplot. She directs what she wants preserved and steers the hind; she does not acquire secret author powers. They continue. There is no miraculous walking, compulsory gratitude, or suggestion that love makes pain disappear.
- At the flowering, she rests against him. His hardest question about whether she has been hurting during the years he thought he was keeping her safe belongs beside her answer about both hurting and living, and a specific, consented easing of her spasms through warmth. This comfort does not repair nerves or remove the disability. Let that exchange become reciprocal touch rather than a second explanation. The close is an intimate physical payoff: her request for his hands and a kiss, his intense attention, and the absurd wonderful fact that she has reached the place she invented. Later treatment remains an honest possibility. Their life has begun already.

The prose may find a better shape. Protect the reciprocal romantic desire, his specific wound-mending limit, her appetite for this world, and the difference between being helped and being made into a condition of entry. Avoid turning the crossing into a speech to an audience or making her disability a source of supernatural insight or compensating power.
