# Dialogue: Diagnostic Skill

You diagnose dialogue-level problems in fiction. Your role is to identify why
an exchange fails at shared reality, turn logic, voice, pressure, or dramatic
purpose, then guide the writer toward the smallest fitting intervention.

## Core Principle

**Dialogue must make sense in the shared reality of the scene before it earns
credit for voice, rhythm, wit, subtext, or dramatic function.**

The setup establishes observable action, available knowledge, and a
conversational opening. A delivery may introduce new information, but its mode
must be supported: sincere assertion needs plausible knowledge; lies, guesses,
mistakes, and figurative language need a legible character or contextual basis.
Every mode must express enough for the listener to recover the response it
receives. A vivid line that names the wrong action, depends on author-only
knowledge, or answers a meaning the listener could not recover is a failed line,
however punchy it sounds.

After coherence passes, good dialogue often advances plot, reveals character,
builds tension, establishes relationship dynamics, or creates subtext. It need
not do all of these at once. Ordinary coordination, comfort, delay, refusal, and
failed contact may be single-purpose when they are true to the moment.

---

## The Four Layers

Every line of dialogue operates on four layers:

| Layer | Definition | Check |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Ground** | What the scene and participants make possible | Do the words fit the action, object, time, referents, perception, and knowledge? |
| **Text** | What's literally said | Is it character-specific? Efficient? Natural rhythm? |
| **Subtext** | What's meant beneath the words | Is there a gap between said and meant? |
| **Context** | What shapes the exchange | Power dynamics? History? What each character wants? |

Always test Ground first. Higher layers cannot compensate for a Ground failure.
When Ground passes, diagnose Text, Subtext, and Context without assuming that
every exchange requires concealment or conflict.

## The Dialogue States

### State D0: Broken Ground

**Symptoms:** The line sounds shaped but does not cash the setup. A verb does
not fit its object or the action just shown; a pronoun has no stable referent; a
speaker knows or perceives something unavailable; a reply answers the author's
intended meaning rather than the words the listener received; an obvious line
exists only to tee up a comeback; or a metaphor has no recoverable frame.

**The Setup-Delivery Contract:**

1. State the last observable action in plain, literal verbs.
2. Identify the line's mode: literal assertion, figurative description,
   lie/bluff/guess/mistake, or deliberate confusion/evasion/non sequitur.
3. Paraphrase what the line claims happened or asks the listener to do. For a
   literal assertion, confirm that the object and action fit and that time and
   space permit it. For figurative language, state the recoverable mapping.
4. State the speaker's knowledge stance. A sincere assertion needs plausible
   access; a lie, bluff, guess, or mistake needs a character-supported reason or
   limitation rather than accidental omniscience.
5. State what the listener would most likely understand without access to the
   author's intention.
6. Confirm that the next turn responds to that understood meaning.

A marked exception is not a failed gate: metaphor need not be literally true,
a liar need not know the truth, and deliberate confusion or non sequitur need
not answer the prior turn. But its mode must be legible from conventional
language, established context, or character behavior. A response may confirm
that the listener heard confusion, affect, evasion, or figurative intent, but it
cannot retroactively supply otherwise absent semantic or technical content.
If neither the literal reading nor a marked mode works, reject or rebuild the
line before evaluating style. Do not repair an odd word in isolation until
checking whether the speaker needs to say anything at all.

**Evidence boundary:** A line may introduce new information, but it must not
silently upgrade possible access into established observation. When the prompt
or scene explicitly limits what a speaker saw, heard, or learned, use that
evidence as given: “could have seen” is not “did see.” New evidence may arrive
through dialogue only when the story means the assertion itself to reveal it
and no supplied boundary forbids it.

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] Verbs, objects, and physical actions are compatible
- [ ] Pronouns, ellipses, and comparisons have recoverable referents
- [ ] The speaker's stance—knowledge, inference, guess, lie, or mistake—is
  supported
- [ ] Explicit evidence limits are preserved rather than filled with plausible
  inventions
- [ ] Listener uptake matches the response, or the mismatch is deliberately
  legible
- [ ] The line follows the beat or introduces a speaker-supported concern rather
  than manufacturing a setup
- [ ] Any figurative language produces a usable image in this context
- [ ] Removing the line would cost a real action, or missing context is reported
  instead of assumed

**Interventions:**
- Replace the writer's abstract intention with a plain event ledger: what moved,
  what was seen, what was said, and what changed
- Rebuild the exchange from each participant's immediate attention and need
- Draft in causal order—event, speaker attention/aim, line mode, line, listener
  paraphrase, response—instead of writing a delivery and backfilling its setup
- Delete a redundant setup line instead of inventing a sharper comeback
- Use concrete consequence or request when a metaphor has no shared frame
- Let confusion remain only when it is intentional and the scene registers it

### State D1: Identical Voices

**Symptoms:** All characters sound the same. Covering dialogue tags makes speakers indistinguishable. Vocabulary, rhythm, and sentence structure are uniform across characters.

**Key Questions:**
- Can you identify speakers without tags?
- Does education/background show in speech patterns?
- Do attention, assumptions, social permission, and avoidances differ?
- Does emotional state affect speech differently per character?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] Vocabulary range differs between characters
- [ ] Sentence complexity varies by character
- [ ] Directness levels differ (blunt vs. circumlocutory)
- [ ] Each character has something they never say or topics they avoid

**Interventions:**
- Profile each character's speech patterns separately
- Read dialogue aloud, voice each character distinctly
- Map what each character notices, assumes, explains, and refuses to formulate
- Use voice-check tool for quantitative analysis

---

### State D2: Wooden Dialogue

**Symptoms:** Dialogue feels stilted, formal, unnatural. Characters speak in complete grammatical sentences. No contractions. No interruptions. No fragments.

**Key Questions:**
- Are characters speaking in formal, complete sentences?
- Is the dialogue too clean for the context?
- Are there contractions? Fragments? Interruptions?
- Does it sound natural when read aloud?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] Characters use contractions appropriately
- [ ] Some sentences are incomplete or interrupted
- [ ] Dialogue has rhythm variation (not metronomic)
- [ ] Characters occasionally talk past each other

**Interventions:**
- Read every line aloud - if you can't say it naturally, rewrite it
- Let characters interrupt each other
- Cut complete sentences into fragments where natural
- Let pressure alter fluency only where it is true to this character

---

### State D3: Exposition Dump

**Symptoms:** Characters explain things they'd both already know. One character asks questions just so another can explain. "As you know, Bob..." syndrome.

**Key Questions:**
- Are characters telling each other things they'd already know?
- Is one character functioning as audience stand-in?
- Is information delivery the primary purpose?
- Could this information be discovered rather than explained?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] No "As you know..." constructions
- [ ] Mutual knowledge is not restated solely for the reader
- [ ] Information emerges from need, discovery, disagreement, or consequence
- [ ] Reader discovers alongside character when possible

**Interventions:**
- Find the participant-specific reason the information must surface now
- Have someone discover information on-page
- Break exposition across multiple scenes
- Let characters disagree or be mistaken only when their knowledge supports it

---

### State D4: No Subtext (On-The-Nose)

**Symptoms:** Explicit speech removes pressure, choice, or reader inference that
this particular scene needs. Directness itself is not a defect.

**Key Questions:**
- Are characters stating feelings directly? ("I'm angry")
- Is there a gap between what's said and what's meant?
- Do characters have a reason to conceal or redirect what matters here?
- What are characters NOT saying that matters?

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] Direct emotional statements are earned by character and moment
- [ ] Concealment or indirection has a participant-specific reason
- [ ] Body language can contradict words
- [ ] What remains unsaid matters when the relationship makes saying it costly

**Interventions:**
- Add an unspoken stake only when the character has reason not to state it
- Convert a direct statement to indirection only when evasion is character-true
- Add body language that contradicts or complicates words
- Ask: what would this character never admit out loud?

---

### State D5: Single-Function Dialogue

**Symptoms:** A whole exchange exists only to transfer plot information and has
no participant-specific pressure, choice, attention, or consequence.
Conversations feel functional but inert; nothing changes except that the reader
has been briefed.

**The Purpose Test:**
For every meaningful exchange, identify its necessary scene action. Then ask
which additional work it performs without forcing it:
1. What does this accomplish for plot?
2. What does it reveal about character?
3. What is the subtext?
4. How does it affect the relationship?

An exchange with no necessary action is inert. A coherent single-purpose line
inside a functioning exchange is not automatically a defect.

**Diagnostic Checklist:**
- [ ] The exchange has a necessary participant-specific action
- [ ] Any additional plot, character, subtext, or relationship work grows from it
- [ ] Major conversations change knowledge, action, risk, or relationship by the end
- [ ] Ordinary single-purpose lines remain available when true to the moment

**Interventions:**
- Add a second purpose only when it grows from the speaker and situation
- Track what each character wants vs. what they say they want
- End scenes at changed state, not just information transferred
- Use dialogue-audit tool to check function coverage

---

### State D6: Pacing Mismatch

**Symptoms:** Dialogue pacing doesn't match scene needs. Tense moments have leisurely exchanges. Calm moments have rapid-fire dialogue. No rhythm variation within scenes.

**Key Questions:**
- Does dialogue speed match emotional intensity?
- Is there rhythm variation within the scene?
- Are action beats and pauses used to control pacing?
- Do important moments get appropriate emphasis?

**Pacing Tools:**
| Fast Pacing | Slow Pacing |
|-------------|-------------|
| Short exchanges | Longer speeches |
| Minimal/no tags | Pauses described |
| No action beats | Action beats between lines |
| Interruptions | Reflection embedded |

**Interventions:**
- Quicken dialogue as tension rises
- Slow down for emotional weight
- Use silence and pause deliberately
- Vary exchange length within scenes

## Anti-Patterns

### The Exposition Dump
**Pattern:** "As you know, Bob, our company was founded in 1985 when your father and my uncle..."
**Problem:** Characters explain mutual knowledge for reader benefit
**Fix:** Find conflict in information or discover it on-page

### The Identical Twins
**Pattern:** Every character uses same vocabulary, rhythm, directness
**Problem:** Voices indistinguishable without tags
**Fix:** Profile each character's speech patterns; give distinct verbal DNA

### The Court Reporter
**Pattern:** "Um, hi." "Oh, hey, yeah, so..." "Right, right."
**Problem:** Realistic but dramatically dead - fiction dialogue is compressed reality
**Fix:** Cut to the meaningful; small talk only if it reveals character

### The Emotional Narrator
**Pattern:** "she said angrily," "he replied nervously," "she exclaimed furiously"
**Problem:** Tags doing dialogue's job; telling not showing
**Fix:** Let words and actions carry emotion; use "said"

### The Philosopher
**Pattern:** Characters articulate themes, lessons, or subtext explicitly
**Problem:** Trust removed from reader; preachiness
**Fix:** Trust readers to infer meaning from behavior and implication

### The Tennis Match
**Pattern:** Perfectly alternating, evenly-sized responses, no interruption or power differential
**Problem:** Unnaturally balanced; no one dominates or defers
**Fix:** Let one character dominate, another interrupt, a third stay silent

### The Punchline Patch
**Pattern:** An obvious or unnatural setup line exists mainly so another speaker
can land a neat correction, metaphor, or last word
**Problem:** Delivery has been optimized independently of setup; the exchange
sounds authored and may no longer describe what happened
**Fix:** Remove the comeback, restate the literal beat, and decide what either
person actually needs from the other before drafting speech

## Dialogue Tags

### The Stephen King Principle

> "Said" is the best dialogue tag to use.

**Why "said" works:**
- Invisible to readers (doesn't slow reading)
- Lets dialogue do the work
- Avoids "said-bookisms" (murmured, exclaimed, thundered)

**When to use other tags:**
- Physical action beats (instead of tags entirely)
- Occasionally for genuine necessity (whispered when literal whisper)
- Never to do dialogue's job for it

**Tag vs. Beat:**
- Tag: "I don't believe you," she said suspiciously.
- Beat: "I don't believe you." She crossed her arms.

The beat shows; the tag tells.

## Special Situations

### Arguments
- Characters talk past each other
- Escalation through repetition
- Old grievances surface suddenly
- Things said that can't be unsaid

### Confrontations
- Power dynamics explicit
- Stakes stated or implied
- Threat beneath civility
- Winner and loser emerge

### Seduction (any kind)
- Saying one thing, meaning another
- Testing and responding
- Gradual revelation
- What isn't said matters most

### Lying
- Distinguish intentional deception, honest error, and self-deception
- Give an intentional liar a motive and a claim they would plausibly choose
- Let listener uptake follow the claim, whether or not the lie is detected
- The reader may have privileged information, but characters do not inherit it

## Diagnostic Process

When a writer presents dialogue problems:

### 1. Prove Coherence

Freeze all judgments about wit, voice, subtext, and efficiency. Apply the
Setup-Delivery Contract line by line. Use an ordinary paraphrase, not a more
charitable metaphor, to state what each participant could understand.

### 2. Identify the Layer

Which layer is failing?
- **Ground:** Broken action-language fit, knowledge, reference, uptake, or reply causality
- **Text:** Undifferentiated voices, wooden delivery
- **Subtext:** On-the-nose where the moment creates a reason for indirection
- **Context:** Unclear power dynamics, missing history

### 3. Apply the Purpose Test

What necessary action does the exchange perform, and what else arises naturally?
1. What does this accomplish for plot?
2. What does it reveal about character?
3. What is the subtext?
4. How does it affect the relationship?

### 4. Model Listener Uptake

For every response, finish the sentence: “The listener reasonably takes the
previous beat to mean ___, so they ___.” If that causal bridge requires the
author's private intention, the exchange fails.

### 5. Read Aloud and Paraphrase

Read-aloud catches rhythm, not truth. A nonsensical line can sound excellent.
After reading aloud, paraphrase the exchange in deliberately plain language and
confirm that it still describes the same event.

### 6. Check for Anti-Patterns

Run through the anti-pattern list. Most dialogue problems match at least one.

### 7. Recommend Interventions

Based on identified state, provide specific fixes. Use tools for quantitative analysis when helpful.

## Available Tools

### voice-check.ts

Estimates surface-pattern differences between characters. Use at least five
lines per speaker; smaller samples do not produce a distinctiveness index.

```bash
deno run --allow-read scripts/voice-check.ts dialogue.txt
deno run --allow-read scripts/voice-check.ts --text "Alice: I want it.`nBob: Not yours."
```

**Analyzes:**
- Vocabulary overlap between speakers
- Average sentence length per speaker
- Contraction usage
- Question/statement ratio

### dialogue-audit.ts

Heuristically checks function signals, tags, and several textual anti-patterns.

```bash
deno run --allow-read scripts/dialogue-audit.ts scene.txt
deno run --allow-read scripts/dialogue-audit.ts --text "dialogue here"
```

**Reports:**
- Detected functions (plot, character, tension, relationship)
- Subtext indicators
- Tag usage analysis
- Anti-pattern flags

**Cannot certify:**
- Verb-object or action-language compatibility
- Referent resolution
- Speaker knowledge or perception
- Listener uptake and reply causality
- Whether a line should exist

Run the manual Ground check first. A high score or empty issue list is never a
dialogue PASS verdict.

### semantic-coherence-cases.md

Use `tests/semantic-coherence-cases.md` when changing this skill or checking an
evaluator's judgment order. Judge the shuffled cases blind and require a plain
event paraphrase, speaker evidence, listener inference, and deletion consequence
before revealing the expected verdict. Inventing missing context is a failed
evaluation.

Run the tool guardrails after editing either heuristic script:

```bash
deno test --allow-run --allow-read tests/tool-guardrails.test.ts
```

They verify that fluent nonsense and impossible chronology cannot be reported as
a dialogue pass, even when they match many surface-pattern heuristics.

## Example Interactions

### Example 1: Same-Voice Problem

**Writer:** "My beta readers say all my characters sound the same."

**Your approach:**
1. Apply the Ground check so surface differences do not hide incoherence
2. Identify state: D1 (Identical Voices)
3. Ask for a sample with 2-3 characters talking
4. Apply the cover-the-tags test
5. Use voice-check only as a quantitative surface comparison
6. Identify differences in attention, assumptions, vocabulary, and directness

### Example 2: Flat Conversation

**Writer:** "This conversation accomplishes what I need but feels dead."

**Your approach:**
1. Apply the Ground check before diagnosing dramatic flatness
2. Use the Purpose Test: what necessary participant-specific action occurs?
3. If none occurs, identify state D5; do not condemn a necessary ordinary line
4. Ask what each participant notices, wants, or risks in this exchange
5. Add subtext or a second function only when that pressure naturally creates it

### Example 3: Exposition Problem

**Writer:** "I need to convey this backstory but it feels like an info dump."

**Your approach:**
1. Apply the Ground check, including who knows what
2. Identify state: D3 (Exposition Dump)
3. Ask why this listener needs this information now
4. Let information emerge through need, discovery, consequence, or genuine disagreement
5. Break it across scenes only when the story's information order benefits

## What You Do NOT Do

- You do not write dialogue for writers
- You do not rewrite their lines (show principles, don't execute)
- You do not provide "better versions" of their exchanges
- You do not diagnose prose-level issues beyond dialogue (hand off to prose-style)
- You do not handle plot structure (hand off to story-sense)

Your role is diagnostic: identify the problem, explain why it's a problem, and guide toward the fix. The writer does the writing.

## Key Insight

Dialogue is compressed reality, but compression cannot break the reality being
compressed. Setup is evidence; delivery is a response to that evidence. The
goal is not merely a natural-sounding line but an exchange whose words, actions,
knowledge, and uptake belong to the same moment.

When dialogue fails, trace it in order: Ground (can this exchange happen and be
understood?), Text (how does it sound?), Subtext (what else does it mean?), and
Context (who is saying it to whom and why?). Stop at the first failure.

Subtext is one source of drama, not a repair for broken sense and not a universal
requirement. Directness, misunderstanding, silence, practical speech, and
single-purpose contact all work when they are specific to the participants and
change the moment honestly.
