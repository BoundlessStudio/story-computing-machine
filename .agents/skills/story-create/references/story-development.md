# Developing a short story

Use these questions as needed during the [workshop](../SKILL.md). Start with what the request makes distinctive and ask what a reader would feel the loss of if it never happened.

## From an idea to a scene

Consider different people through whom to meet the premise, different times to enter, and different places for it to happen. Try the premise at its requested scale. A familiar approach may give the strongest scene. A different approach earns its place through what it lets a character do or a reader discover.

For each serious possibility, imagine the scene you want to read. What can happen here that could only happen with this premise, in this place, to these people? Try the scene in prose. Change one meaningful choice when examining its effect, or try a substantially different direction when the current story lacks a center. Look for a move or answer neither version of the outline predicted.

## Know who is in the room

Give each principal an appetite, obligation, or curiosity they will act on. Consider what they notice first, what they know, what they have wrong, what they would never say in this company, and what changes under pressure. The same person may sound different with a sister and a superior. Try a few lines from each important voice if the speakers can trade dialogue. Draw distinctions from the people and the relationship.

If the user supplies a line, tune that character's speech to it. Treat a supplied or established character as casting. Read the source and study what expression or habit feels distinctive, and how their look and temperament show up in action. Keep a few telling details available through the story, especially at the turn. An established character keeps their identity and history.

### The conversation the story owes

A premise may make a reader wait for someone to ask where a person has been, to admit what they did, or to ask a friend to stay. Identify what those people need from each other and give them a reason to deal with it now. Before they speak, know what each could have seen, heard, or inferred. Give allies private wants that can meet or cross. Let a character give an account in their own idiom when the listener and stakes warrant it. If an action or a silence answers the question, make its effect visible.

## Set it somewhere particular

Choose a place that makes something easier or harder for this scene. Consider the work going on there, its light and sound, and what someone can see or overhear. Let the viewpoint determine which details matter. For a reference setting, use established geography, scale, light, and atmosphere. Show how the place changes what people can do.

Choose a teller with something at stake in the telling. The narrator's distance, vocabulary, and attention can change the entire scene. Try a second telling where that difference matters. Put the most attention where the feeling turns.

## Build to an ending the scenes earn

Once a scene works, ask what the reader must know when it begins and what choices, images, and feelings it leaves behind. Give an early action or observation enough weight to return differently at the end. Add only the scenes and bridges the form needs. An ending can be a consequence, revelation, refusal, or image. Check what makes that ending belong to the premise and this particular person.

Record the promised experience, the tested choice, and the provisional sequence in `outline.md`. Let discoveries in prose change its direction. Notice recurring destinations such as humble work, a tidy lesson, or maintenance when they crowd out the requested joy, danger, grandeur, or intimacy.
