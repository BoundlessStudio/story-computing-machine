# From prompt to outline

A `[WP]` prompt is a situation, not yet a story. The outline is where the writer finds the story in it, and most of what makes a finished piece good or ordinary is settled here: whose story it is, where it happens, how few scenes it needs, how its people talk. A draft can only be as good as these decisions.

This guide is for the writer, and for the outline readers who test the result. [The craft standard](craft-standard.md) says what the finished prose should be like; this says how to plan toward it. Work through it in order. The outline that results has five parts and should run a page or two:

1. **Promised experience** – what the request asks for, in the user's terms.
2. **Takes considered** – the stories this could have been, and the one chosen.
3. **Provisional direction** – the chosen story in a few paragraphs.
4. **Craft plan** – teller, cast and speech, the conversation the story owes, reference character studies, setting.
5. **Scene briefs** – the scenes the story will inhabit, often two to four, and the ending.

The promise and the fidelity comparison are covered in `story-create`. Everything below serves that promise; none of it licenses a cleverer story than the one that was asked for.

## Start from the sources

Read the request until you can say what someone would be disappointed not to get. Then look at each supplied image for a full minute before deciding anything. The user chose that picture. Its mood, its palette, the way the figure stands, and the weather behind them are all information about the story they are imagining, and the first plot idea will crowd that out if it arrives first.

Note what the prompt leaves open. The open questions (who tells it, where, what the people are to each other, how it ends) are where the writer's real choices are, and each deserves an actual decision.

## Find three stories, then choose one

The first story to arrive is the one any writer would find, set where this collection usually sets things and resolved the way it usually resolves them. Before committing, sketch three takes in two or three sentences each. They must differ in something structural, not in decoration:

- **Whose story it is.** The obvious protagonist; someone standing next to them; someone on the other side.
- **Where the clock starts.** Before the prompt's moment, at it, or long after, looking back.
- **Where it happens.** See "Choose the place" below. A change of setting often changes everything else.
- **What the central relationship is.** Rivals, siblings, employer and servant, old lovers, a creature and the one person not afraid of it.
- **What register it plays in.** Comic, elegiac, frightening, romantic, tall-tale.

Make at least one take play the prompt at full scale and straight, and at least one come at it from an angle. For each, name the scene a reader would turn to first. That scene is the test: choose the take whose central scene is strongest and in which the prompt's distinctive pleasure does the most work. An unexpected angle that pushes the requested experience to the margins is the wrong choice, however fresh it is.

Then check the choice against the collection's habits. Does it end in humble work, a therapeutic conversation, maintenance, a signature, or a procedure? Is its world an unnamed kingdom or a nameless modern city? If so, ask whether this prompt wants that or whether it is the default again.

Record the rejected takes in a line each. The outline readers can then see what was passed over, and sometimes they will rightly argue for one of them.

## Know the people

A character is someone who could surprise the reader and still be themselves. For each principal, usually two or three in a short story, decide:

- What they want in this story, concretely, and what they want that they would not admit.
- A contradiction: brave and vain, generous and controlling, devout and curious.
- What they would never do, and whether the story is going to make them do it.
- What they are good at, and how that shows in what they notice.
- How they talk (next section).

Give each principal their own wants, attention, and way of acting, including when they love each other and share a goal. Look for the points where those wants cross and where agreement itself costs or reveals something.

That want has to reach into each scene. For every pair who share one, name what each wants from the other that day and how it shapes the exchange. Thanks, an apology, permission, trust, company, or a moment alone can matter between allies. Let any friction grow from those people and the situation. The collection's talk is alive between opponents and goes flat between friends when a shared goal replaces individual attention and desire.

Keep the cast small enough to know. Everyone past the principals is functional. Name them only if they return, and resist giving each a single identifying trait; a chorus can stay a chorus.

### How each one talks

Decide this before writing a line of dialogue, and put a sentence or two per principal in the craft plan:

- How much they say. Who fills silence and who lets it sit.
- Where their words come from: a trade, a region, a church, a library, a barracks, the internet.
- What they do under pressure: joke, lecture, go formal, go quiet, change the subject.
- What they will not say, and what they say instead.
- A habit of syntax, lightly: someone who never finishes a sentence, someone who answers questions with questions, someone who narrates their own actions.

Then let each of them talk for six or eight lines about something that is not in the story (a bad meal, the weather, a relative). Do this off the page; it does not go in the outline. If the two samples could be swapped, the voices are not found yet. When the prompt quotes a character, that line is the tuning note: the off-page sample should sound like the same mouth.

### What each one knows

You have read the request and will have written the outline. Nobody in the story has. For each principal, note what they know when the story opens, what they do not, and what they believe that is wrong. Do the same for the reader: what has the page told them by the start of each scene?

Lines written from the writer's knowledge are the ones a stranger cannot follow. A character asks “How many?” and is given the casualty figure, because the writer knew which number was meant. Another says “He found me” to a friend who has never heard of him. Establish what the listener can recover from prior context and the current line, including ordinary inference. A line can introduce its own new information. If a character's reference is deliberately unclear to the listener, stage that confusion and its consequences. Later information cannot justify an earlier reply that required knowledge the listener lacked.

### The conversation the story owes

Most premises set up one conversation the reader starts waiting for as soon as they understand the situation. Where have you been, what did you do, why didn't you tell me, will you come? Write down in a plain sentence what these people have to say to each other and which scene will hold it. Give it the room its importance requires. If the requested form centres on solitude or leaves a relationship deliberately unspoken, identify the action that carries that expectation and payoff instead.

If the prompt quotes a line that raises a question (“Well, I did my half”), the owed conversation includes the answer, in that character's voice.

If someone will have to give an account (twenty years away, the terms of a bargain, what was done on the night it all went wrong), decide who tells it, why now, and why to this listener, and expect it to run a paragraph or two. Plan what the teller hurries past and what the listener most wants out of it. An account squeezed into three lines to keep the pace up is the most common source of talk nobody can follow.

## Study each reference character

For every character shown in a supplied image, and every character borrowed from [universe/characters.md](../../../../universe/characters.md) or an approved sheet, write a short study in the craft plan. An image is casting. The aim is that the person who supplied it meets, on the page, the character they were picturing.

1. **What the source establishes.** Silhouette, build, colouring, signature features, clothing, objects, surroundings. Be exact: which temple the bow sits over, which hand holds the rifle.
2. **What it expresses.** Read the face and the stance as you would a stranger's across a room. Amused? Hungry? Bored and dangerous? Trying to look taller? Write three or four verbs for how this person moves and one sentence on how they would enter a room.
3. **How they sound.** From that temperament, and from any line the prompt gives them, decide their speech as above.
4. **Signature details and where each is used.** Pick three or four. For each, plan at least one moment where it takes part in action or feeling, and decide which one returns at the turn or the ending. A detail that appears only in the character's first description is wardrobe.
5. **What to translate.** If the image is more revealing, violent, or stylized than the story's rating or tone will carry, keep the silhouette and the attitude and change the particulars. Say what you changed. A bold image answered with a subdued character is a miscasting, not an adaptation.
6. **What is open.** Name, history, and motives the source does not give are the writer's to invent, in keeping with the temperament.

For a group image, study the group as one presence (how they stand together, what they share) and then choose the two or three who will be people. For an established character, read the universe entry and at least one scene they already appear in; keep the name exactly, with their voice and history, and then give them something new to want.

When an image shows a place rather than a person, study it the same way: geography, scale, light, weather, what work goes on there, what lies just outside the frame.

## Choose the place

Setting is a decision, and usually the least-made one. Name three candidate places for the chosen take and test each:

- **Pressure.** Where would this premise be hardest to hide, most costly, most embarrassing, most dangerous?
- **Particularity.** Can you name it, and say what people there do for a living and what they complain about?
- **Action.** What does the place make difficult, and what does it make possible that nowhere else would? Can the decisive moment be one that could only happen here?
- **The senses.** What does it smell like at this hour? What is underfoot? What can be heard two rooms away?
- **Freshness.** Has the collection been here many times? Throne rooms, terraces, vaults, archives, small kitchens, and unnamed capitals are well worn.

When the prompt fixes the setting, the same questions apply one level down: which street, which season, which room, what hour.

Then write a short **place sheet** in the craft plan:

- Its name, and where it sits in the world. Check [universe/locations.md](../../../../universe/locations.md) for an established place that fits; do not invent a connection to one that does not.
- Season, weather, and the hours the story covers.
- The work that goes on there regardless of the plot.
- Five specific sensory facts, at least two of them not visual.
- One thing that is broken, odd, or locally famous.
- What the place makes hard for these characters, and what it offers them.
- How it will have changed, or be seen differently, by the last page.

If the place resembles a real kind of place or trade (a signal box, a tannery, a night ferry office, a bell foundry), find out how it actually works. Two true details carry more conviction than a page of invention. The style guide's boundary on default water infrastructure applies here.

Prefer one or two places fully rendered. If the story must travel, let one location be home ground and treat the rest briefly.

## Choose the teller

Decide who tells this, from how close, from when, and in what temper. The same events told by a participant the next morning, by the same person forty years on, and by a neighbour who never liked either of them are three different stories. The prompt's own person and tense do not bind the prose.

The voice is tested, not assumed. After the outline is approved, the writer drafts the opening two different ways for the voice trial described in `story-create`. Note in the craft plan which two voices you intend to try.

## Choose the scenes

Most stories of three to five thousand words are best served by two to four scenes. For each, write a brief of a few lines:

- **Where and when**, specifically, from the place sheet.
- **Who is present**, what each wants from the other in this scene, and what each knows going in that the other does not.
- **What gets said.** The one or two things that must be spoken aloud here, in plain words. Mark the scene that holds the owed conversation.
- **The turn.** What is different at the end: a decision, a discovery, a reversal of who has the upper hand.
- **What the reader comes to understand without being told.**
- **The detail to remember.** One image, object, or line this scene owns.
- **Its share of the length.**

Mark the **long scene**, the one the reader came for. Give it a third or more of the story, and plan for the talk in it to wander, for someone to change their mind, and for the room to interfere.

List what will be summarized in a paragraph and what is simply left out. If the briefs come to more than four scenes or three locations, check whether the decisive scenes have enough room. Combine or cut where the story is compressed, and retain the travel or ensemble the request needs. For each brief, ask whether it could move to a different room without rewriting, and whether two other people could speak its dialogue. Those answers reveal where setting and character still need work.

Keep any speculative rule simple enough to state in a sentence and to matter in one choice. If a scene exists to explain how something works, change the rule.

## Design the ending now

Decide the last act or image, and name the two or three earlier moments that load it with meaning. An ending is earned backward. The collection's reflexes are a final exchange of short lines, a reprised phrase, a quiet domestic gesture, and a character setting off; use one only if this story has made it the inevitable thing.

The ending should also deliver the promised experience. The scale of the last page should match the scale of the prompt.

## Before the outline goes to readers

Read it back and check:

- Could a stranger tell from the craft plan what this story will sound like, and where they will be standing?
- Does each reference character study describe a person, not an outfit?
- Is the setting somewhere, and does it do something?
- Is there one scene with room in it?
- Is the conversation the story owes named, and does a scene with room hold it?
- For each pair on the same side, what does one want from the other?
- Is anything here only because it was the first idea?

The outline stays provisional. The prose may find a better route, and a departure is recorded in `notes.md`. But a draft begun without these decisions will make them by default, and the defaults are known.
