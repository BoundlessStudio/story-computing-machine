# The craft standard

The fidelity gates and the promise keeper decide whether this is the story the user asked for. This reference is about the other half of the job: whether it is written well enough that a stranger would finish it, remember it, and pass it on.

The reader to write for has read a great deal of published short fiction and has Ursula K. Le Guin, Alice Munro, Ted Chiang, Kelly Link, George Saunders, and Terry Pratchett on the shelf. That reader is not asking for an imitation of any of them. They are the measure of how alive a page can be: a narrator with a stance, people who sound like themselves, a place that could be nowhere else, sentences that move at more than one speed. A story that delivers its prompt in flat, careful prose has only done the first half.

The writer reads this before outlining and again before revising. It describes aims and the reasons for them; none of it is a quota, and a deliberate voice can break any of it on purpose. Three companions go with it: [From prompt to outline](story-development.md) turns these aims into planning decisions, [Craft examples](craft-examples.md) shows seven of them on the page, and [Reader and editor briefs](reader-briefs.md) is how readers test for them.

## What this collection does when nobody decides otherwise

These habits were measured across the existing stories. Each began as sound advice (omit needless words, trust the reader, prefer subtext, use "said") and hardened into a house manner. Together they are why a story can satisfy its prompt and still read like a capable summary of itself.

- **One speed.** With speech set aside, narration averages under ten words a sentence. Nearly four sentences in ten are six words or fewer, and about three in a hundred reach twenty-five. More than half of all paragraphs are twelve words or fewer. With every moment given the same clipped weight, nothing is emphasized and the page reads like a storyboard.
- **One voice.** More than half of all spoken turns are five words or fewer, and in half the stories nobody speaks forty words at a stretch. Kings, children, monsters, and clerks all answer in the same dry, economical register, usually in strict alternation, often with one speaker echoing a word of the last line back as a question.
- **Too much story.** A four-thousand-word piece often crosses five or more scenes, several locations, and a large named cast. Each scene has room for its plot function and nothing else.
- **Places that are categories.** "The capital," "a palace roof," "the terrace," "the vault." Settings are seen; in six stories out of ten nothing is smelled or tasted at all. They rarely do anything to the people in them.
- **Feeling stepped around.** At the emotional peak the prose gets shorter and cooler, and a character says "Oh." Restraint only reads as restraint against a ground of fullness; used everywhere it reads as absence.
- **Mechanism in the foreground.** Rules, clauses, and procedures are worked out carefully, mostly through question-and-answer dialogue, and the middle of the story becomes an explanation.
- **Reference characters as wardrobe.** A supplied character is described once, accurately, on first appearance. After that the look, and more often the attitude, fades into the house manner.
- **Lines only the author can follow.** A speaker opens on a pronoun or a fragment with nothing behind it (“He found me.” “How many?” “Their hands.”) and the listener replies as if they had read the outline. The writer knows what is meant, and so does every reader who has seen the request. A stranger does not.
- **The conversation skipped.** The exchange a story has been building toward is cut off with a dash, closed with “I know,” or replaced by a sentence reporting that nobody could answer. “I know.” is a complete spoken turn 141 times across 235 stories.
- **Allies who only pass facts.** Talk between opponents is usually alive. Talk between two people on the same side is information and agreement, because neither has been given anything to win from the other.
- **Less talk every month.** In the earliest fifty-five stories about a quarter of the words are spoken. In the thirty most recent it is under a sixth, and in twenty-four of those thirty nobody speaks forty words together.
- **Thought as aphorism.** Where a mind should be working, the narrator supplies a polished general remark, or the character arrives at once at the verdict a fair-minded reviewer would give.

The last five were found by reading the newest stories for their talk in November 2026, and they point one way. The standing warnings against machine-written fiction (do not explain, do not state feelings, do not make speeches) have been obeyed here past the point of sense. The result is people who say less than a reader needs in order to follow them or to care. In this workshop the error to fear is the withheld line, not the spoken one.

Treat these as the gravity to write against, not as new rules to invert. Long sentences everywhere would be one speed too. `scripts/prose_profile.py` measures a draft against these figures; use it to find where to look, never as a target to hit.

## Design a short story, not a compressed novel

A short story earns its effect by staying somewhere. Decide these before the outline's beats, and record them in the outline's craft plan.

**Fewer scenes, fully inhabited.** Two to four scenes will carry most stories of three to five thousand words. Choose the moments where the premise presses hardest on a person and stay in them long enough for talk to wander, for someone to change their mind, for the room to matter. Cover everything else in a paragraph of summary or leave it out. One scene, the one the reader came for, should be allowed to run long. When the outline travels through many locations, check which scenes need more room and which can be combined. Let the request and the chosen form determine the final count.

**A cast the reader can hold.** Two or three people known well will often carry a short story. Choose the principals the request needs and give each room to become particular. Supporting characters can stay lightly drawn. When the prompt or a reference image supplies a crowd, choose the people through whom the reader meets it while preserving any individuals and relationships the request makes central.

**A narrator with a stance.** Decide who is telling this, from how far away, and how they feel about it: amused, grieving, defensive, besotted, looking back after forty years. That stance should color the description, not only the dialogue. Before committing, write the opening page two genuinely different ways (a different teller, distance, tense, or temperament) and keep the one with more life in it. The first voice to arrive is usually the house manner.

**One mechanism, lightly held.** A speculative rule needs to be clear enough for one consequential choice. If the outline takes a paragraph to explain how the magic, contract, or machine works, simplify the rule rather than staging a scene to teach it.

**An ending the story has been loading.** Close on an act or image that the earlier pages have given weight to. A final exchange of short lines, a reprised motto, or a quiet domestic gesture is the collection's reflex; use one only when this story has made it inevitable.

## Sentences and paragraphs

Let syntax follow attention. A long sentence can carry the reader across a room, through a memory, or along a chain of reasoning without letting go; a short one lands when something stops. A paragraph is a unit of thought that develops: it notices, complicates, turns. The one-line paragraph is a drumbeat, and several to a page is just noise.

Put a mind on the page. The viewpoint character wants things, misjudges people, remembers at inconvenient moments, and notices what only someone with their trade and history would. Narration close to them should use their words and prejudices. At the moments that matter most, move closer and slow down: give the peak more sentences than the approach to it, not fewer.

Prefer the detail that only this story could contain. The first image to arrive is the one every other story already used. Give things their proper names (the street, the ship, the dish, the hymn), because a world with names feels inhabited. Use figurative language rarely, exactly, and from the character's own world.

Watch for manner standing in for meaning: "for a moment," "something like," "seemed to," "That was all," "It was not X. It was Y," reflexive groups of three, a sentence that trails into a soft abstraction, a paragraph that ends by restating itself more quietly. When one appears, ask what the plain claim is and write that.

## Dialogue

The [dialogue](../../dialogue/SKILL.md) ground rules come first: speech has to make sense in the scene before anything else counts. Two faults are common here. One is sameness: every speaker dry and brief. The other is talk that cannot be followed: lines only the writer can decode, and the conversation the reader was waiting for left unspoken.

**Decide how each person talks before they speak.** For every principal: how much they say and how fast; where their words come from (a trade, a region, a book, a childhood); what they talk about when they are nervous; what they will not say; whether they answer questions or go around them. Then cover the tags on a page of the draft. If the speakers cannot be told apart, the scene is not written yet.

**Write each speaker from what they know.** The writer has read the outline and no character has. Before a scene, settle for each speaker what they know, what they have wrong, what they want from the other person in this room, and what they will not say. Write every line from inside that. Then check each reply from the listener's side. Given the words, shared history, and observations established on the page, could this person have answered that way? Ordinary inference and subtext need a recoverable basis. A reply that requires the author's private knowledge needs rewriting.

**Anchor every line.** A line that opens on “he,” “it,” “that,” or a bare question needs a referent recoverable from the words and situation available when it is spoken. A line can introduce a new name or fact itself. When a listener is confused, let their response register it and consider whether the speaker has a reason to begin that way. Repeated cryptic fragments followed by “Who?” can turn an account into staged exposition. Where that happens, let the teller name the person and give the account. Later revelations must not silently supply knowledge a listener needed for an earlier reply.

**Let people talk.** Real talk is lopsided. Someone explains too much, sells, confesses, tells a story with a point they do not reach, and the other person says three words. A character who gets a full paragraph of speech reveals more than twenty lines of fencing. Vary the length of turns the way sentences vary.

**When someone has an account to give, let them give it.** Sometimes a character must say what happened: where she has been for twenty years, what he did, what the bargain cost. Cut down to four clipped lines, an account stops being something a person could say or a reader could follow. People tell things in order, in their own idiom, hurrying past one part and lingering on another, and it takes a paragraph or two. The test is to read the speech alone: could a stranger who knew nothing else repeat back what happened? An account is different from teaching the reader a rule. A rule should still be simplified until it needs no lesson, or kept out of dialogue.

**Speech is something people do to each other.** In every exchange each speaker is trying to get something from the other, right now: forgiveness, a signature, a head start, to be left alone. Lines are attempts. If an exchange exists so the reader can learn the rule, give the explainer a reason to want the listener's belief, or move the information out of dialogue. All of this holds for people on the same side, and that is where the collection most often forgets it. Friends, lovers, a guardian and her ward, two builders on one bridge still want things from each other: to be thanked, to be forgiven, to be trusted with the hard part, not to be asked. Name what each ally wants from the other today and how it shapes what they say. Agreement can reveal distinct people through their attention, generosity, hesitation, and choices.

**Have the conversation.** When the premise and relationship lead the reader to await a question, apology, accusation, or offer, name that conversation in the outline and give it room on the page. Let the people say what the scene has made necessary. A dash, silence, or gesture can carry an understood answer or a deliberate refusal. Check what the reader can understand at that moment and whether the exchange delivers what the story has led them to expect. Repeated evasions need particular reasons and consequences. A story built around solitude or a deliberately unspoken relationship may fulfil its request through other actions.

**Say something else.** The first reply to arrive is the most likely one, which makes it the least alive: an acknowledgement (“I know.” “Yes.”), an echo of the last line (“Intact?” “Very much intact.”), or a question whose only job is to cue the next fact. Strict alternation of short lines and the dry topper that ends a scene come from the same reflex. When a draft line is one of these, ask what this person would say if they were not allowed to close the subject. People answer the question before last, pick at one word, change the subject to what they care about, make an offer, misunderstand, and say the wrong thing warmly.

**Let the line carry its meaning.** If narration must translate an unclear line before the reader can understand the exchange, rewrite the line or its staging. A viewpoint character's reaction can deepen, question, or misread speech that already makes sense. Check whether the narration adds that experience or supplies missing information the listener somehow used before receiving it.

**A quoted line is a casting note.** When the prompt gives a character's words, that is how they talk. Someone who opens with "You got so big! Anyway," does not then become laconic. Carry that voice into the hardest scene in the story, where it is most at risk.

## Thought

Interior passages go wrong here in two ways. The narrator produces an aphorism, a sentence about time or silence or love that could be lifted out and quoted. Or the character reaches a fair and final judgment on events an hour after they happen.

**A thought is a mind working on something it has not settled.** It uses the character's own words. It runs on particulars: a face, a sum of money, a remark from years ago. It is bent by what the character wants, so it can be unfair, petty, hopeful against the evidence, or wrong.

**Test an abstract sentence by putting it in plain words.** If the plain version is a fact about this person at this moment, write that fact. If nothing is left, cut the sentence. A first-person narrator with a temperament may generalize in character, as a joke or a boast or a defence. That is a voice, and it sounds like one.

**Let understanding come late.** Someone who has just behaved badly defends herself, blames whoever is nearest, or fastens on a side detail. If she sees the whole of it, she sees it late and in part. Often the reader should understand before she does. A character who recites the story's lesson has become the author.

**Give a thought an occasion and a direction.** Something in the scene sets it off, and it ends somewhere other than where it began: at a decision, at a memory that changes what the thing in her hand means, at a conclusion she does not like. Put it before the choice, where it is part of the story. After the choice it is a moral.

## Reference characters

A supplied image of a character is casting, not a costume list. The fidelity checkpoint rightly does not treat every pixel as binding; this is a different question. The user chose that picture because of who it shows, and the story should give that person back to them.

**Study the image before choosing a plot**, and put a short character study in the outline:

- What it establishes: silhouette, signature features, palette, clothing, objects, and the surroundings.
- What it expresses: the expression, the posture, how the figure takes up space. What kind of person stands like that? How would they enter a room, laugh, lose their temper, wait?
- Three or four signature details the story will *use*. A detail is used when it takes part in action, plot, or feeling: the bow gets retied, the tail gets stepped on, the white gloves come off. A detail that is only listed is wardrobe.
- Anything to translate. When an image is more revealing, violent, or stylized than the story's rating or tone allows, keep its attitude and silhouette and adapt the particulars. Do not answer a bold picture with a timid character.

**Write presence, not inventory.** Let appearance arrive through what the character does and how others react, spread across the story, and bring the signature details back at the turn and the ending. Attitude outranks wardrobe: a draft that keeps the bow and loses the grin has lost the character. If the image shows swagger, mischief, or menace, the character should still have it in the climax.

**Give a group a few faces.** When the image shows eight, the reader can know two or three. Keep the collective look and let the others be a chorus rather than issuing each a name and one trait.

**Established characters work the same way.** For someone in [universe/characters.md](../../../../universe/characters.md) or an approved character sheet, read the entry and at least one scene they already appear in. Keep the name exactly, and the voice, history, and habits; then give them something new to want.

The test a reader applies: could an illustrator who had never seen the image draw this character from the prose, and would the person who supplied the image recognize their temperament?

## Setting

Choose the place on purpose. Before outlining, name three candidate settings and ask of each where this premise would be most pressured, most surprising, most itself. The first to arrive is usually a category from the list above, or one this collection has used many times. Record the choice and the reason in the craft plan. A supplied image's environment is evidence here too: its light, weather, architecture, and mood.

A place is particular. It has a name, a season, an hour, a climate, work that goes on whether or not the plot arrives, things that are broken, food, and something the locals complain about. Two or three true details do more than ten plausible ones; when a setting resembles a real kind of place or trade, find out how it actually works.

A place acts. It limits, offers, interrupts, and takes sides. People lean on it, hide in it, and are inconvenienced by it. The decisive action should be one that could only happen here. If a scene could be moved to another room without rewriting it, the setting is a backdrop.

Render fewer places, fully. One location that changes over the course of a story is worth more than a tour. A sequence of marvels at a paragraph each is a slideshow.

Enter through the viewpoint character's body. Their trade and mood decide what gets noticed. Establish time and place early in each scene, and reach past sight (temperature, smell, the sound a floor makes) at scene entry and again at the peak.

The shared world offers real places in [universe/locations.md](../../../../universe/locations.md); use one when it fits, and do not invent a connection to one. The style guide's boundary on default water infrastructure and its note on repeating architecture still apply.

## Revising

Revise scenes, not sentences. When a reader reports confusion, the usual house repair is an added clause of clarification, and a story repaired that way ten times reads like minutes of a meeting. Restage the moment so it is clear, or cut what needed the explanation.

Keep repairs out of people's mouths. When a reader finds a gap in the logic (how the ship stays up, which gate, whose flag), the cheapest patch is a spoken line that states the missing fact. It will be said by someone with no reason to say it, to someone who already knows. Restage, move the fact into what the viewpoint character notices, or change the story so the question does not come up.

Before each handoff, read a page from the middle aloud. If every sentence is the same length, if two speakers could trade lines, if you could not say what the room smells like or what hour it is, the draft is still in the house manner. Then check that the longest scene is the one that matters most, and that each reference character's last appearance is as much themselves as their first. Last, go through every exchange once from each listener's side, as described under Dialogue.

After a round of discussion, reread the whole story for voice. Committee repairs are local, and each one is a place the narrator may have stopped sounding like anyone.

## How this is tested

Four readings hold a story to this standard, each with a brief in [Reader and editor briefs](reader-briefs.md):

- The **outline readers** test the craft plan before drafting: the take chosen, the shape, the cast, the reference character studies, the setting, the ending.
- The **cold and fresh readers** give a quoted craft reading after their account of the story: prose, dialogue, reference characters, setting, shape, and the gap between this draft and one a good magazine would take.
- The **plain reader**, given the prose and nothing else, lists every spoken line whose meaning or reference could not be followed on one hearing. Examine each finding against the quoted text and repair sustained failures. A line-level problem deserves an answer even when it affects only a few words.
- The **line editor** works through the structurally settled draft passage by passage and returns notes and a keep list.

Their findings are material. A story that delivers its prompt and fails these readings is not finished.
