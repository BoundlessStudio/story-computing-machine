# Craft examples

Seven short pairs that show the difference [the craft standard](craft-standard.md) describes. In each, the first version is competent and in this collection's usual manner; the second is the same moment written to the standard.

These are illustrations of principles. Do not reuse their names, objects, images, or sentence shapes in a story, and do not treat the second versions as a house style: they are in different voices on purpose. What carries over is the kind of decision each one shows.

## 1. Dialogue: two people who could not trade lines

A collector has come to buy a dead stage magician's props from his widow.

**House manner**

> “How much for the cabinet?”
>
> “It isn't for sale.”
>
> “Everything is for sale.”
>
> “Not that.”
>
> He looked at it. “He died in it?”
>
> “Near it.”
>
> “I'm sorry.”
>
> “You aren't.”

Every line makes sense, and the last one lands. But either speaker could have said any of it. The exchange is a fencing match between two equally terse people, and all the reader learns is the plot fact.

**To the standard**

> “Now that,” Mrs. Abernethy said, before he had got his hand as far as the cabinet door, “you'll want to be careful with, because the left hinge is original and the right one is off a wardrobe in Leeds. Gerald did that himself in 1961, in a tearing hurry, with the house lights already going down, and he never had it put right. He said the audience could hear the difference and it made them nervous in a useful way. Will you have a biscuit? There are plain, and there are some with jam that my sister sends, though I can't think why.”
>
> “I'm fine, thank you. Is it—”
>
> “It isn't, no.” She had the tin open anyway. “The rings are. The dove pans, and all that rope. You can have the rope for nothing. I've been trying to give that rope away since March.”
>
> “I would pay a great deal.”
>
> “I'm sure you would, dear. Have a jam one.”

What changed: she talks at length and he barely gets a clause out, which is the power in the room. Her words come from somewhere (forty years backstage, a sister, a tin). She refuses him by answering a question he has not finished and then by offering a biscuit, so the refusal is something she does. He wants the cabinet; she wants him to stay and to leave without it. Nobody states a feeling, and nobody is laconic for effect.

## 2. Setting: a place that does something

A woman has to carry a clock across a market to the one person who can mend it.

**House manner**

> The market was crowded. Stalls lined the square, and the air was full of voices. She pushed through to the far side, where the clockmaker kept his shop.

This is a category. It could be any market in any story, it is only seen, and it costs her nothing to cross.

**To the standard**

> Thursday market in Quellen was held on the Stair, because nowhere else in the town was flat enough to argue about. Two hundred and eleven steps climbed from the tannery gate to the church, and by long custom a stallholder kept whatever tread her grandmother had kept, so that the cheese was always above the fish and the wind decided which you smelled. Ines went up sideways with the clock held against her chest. It was noon, in August; the stone gave the heat back through her sandals. At the hundred-and-fortieth step a woman selling plums had spread her blanket clear across the tread and would not shift it, and for the length of that negotiation the clock ticked against Ines's breastbone like something that also wanted to be let past.

What changed: the place has a name, a day, an hour, a season, a custom, and a reason for being the way it is. It is smelled and felt through the feet. It obstructs her, so the setting is in the plot. The one figure of speech comes from the object she is carrying. A scene set here could not be moved to another town without being rewritten.

## 3. A reference character: presence, not inventory

Suppose the supplied image shows a very tall woman in a patchwork coat, a fox mask pushed up on her forehead, grinning, with a ladder over one shoulder.

**House manner**

> A woman stood in the doorway. She was very tall and wore a patchwork coat of many colours. A fox mask was pushed up on her forehead, and she carried a ladder over one shoulder. She grinned at him.
>
> “You're the bookbinder?”
>
> “I am.”
>
> “Good.”

Everything in the picture has been listed, accurately, once. After this paragraph the story has nothing left to do with the coat, the mask, or the ladder, and her grin has no voice attached to it.

**To the standard**

> The ladder came through the door first, and then a great deal of coat. She had to duck for the lintel and did it without looking, the way someone does who has been too tall for doorways since she was twelve.
>
> “Don't get up,” she said, to a room in which nobody had, and propped the ladder against his shelves as though it had always been kept there.
>
> The coat was made of other coats. He could pick out a brocade cuff, a square of postman's serge, something yellow that had been oilcloth once. Above all of it, pushed up into her hair like a pair of spectacles, a fox's face looked at the ceiling. Her own was looking at his sandwich.

What changed: the same details arrive through what she does and what he notices, a few at a time. Her attitude is on the page before her appearance is: she takes over the room. The coat now implies a history. And the story has been handed things to use. Later the ladder will have to be for something, and the mask can come down over her face at the moment she lies, so that a detail from the picture is doing work at the turn.

## 4. The peak: staying instead of stepping around

A man opens a letter and finds it is from his mother, who has been dead a year.

**House manner**

> He opened the letter. It was his mother's handwriting.
>
> “Oh,” he said.
>
> That was all.

Restraint, with nothing for it to be restraining. The prose gets shortest exactly where the reader wants most.

**To the standard**

> It was her handwriting, which he had not expected, having assumed without ever thinking about it that the dead would use some other hand. The same forward lean, as if every word were late for the next. The same refusal to cross a *t* until the end of the line, when she would go back and do them all at once. He had grown up believing that this was simply what one did, and had been corrected, at school, with a ruler. He read the first line four times. He could not have said afterward what was in it. He was looking at the *t*'s.

What changed: the prose slows and moves into his mind at the moment that matters. The feeling is carried by one specific, odd, true-seeming detail with a memory attached to it. He still does not say what he feels, so the passage is still restrained, but now there is something under it.

## 5. An account: told so a stranger could repeat it

A fiddler comes home nine years after he went up the hill to play at a wedding.

**House manner**

> “They kept me,” Tam said.
>
> “Who?”
>
> “The ones under the hill. Three years I couldn't leave the hall. Then I could leave the hall, but not the hill.”
>
> “And now?”
>
> “Now they owe me.”

Nine years have gone into five lines, and the listener exists to say “Who?” and “And now?” The reader cannot say why he was kept, how he got out, or what he is owed. It sounds like restraint and it is a summary with the facts removed.

**To the standard**

> “You'll remember I went up to play for a wedding,” Tam said, “and I'll tell you now it was a good wedding, and that was the trouble, because when I'd played the last reel the bride's father said, ‘Again,’ and I thought, well, he's paying. Three years I played that reel, Jess. I know it was three because they let me see the door once a year, to be fair to them, and each time the hawthorn was out. Then I'd a notion to play it badly. They can't abide a thing done badly. They put me out of the hall for it, and I'd have walked home that night, only the hill is bigger inside than you'd think, and it was six years more before I found a way down that came out anywhere I knew.” He looked at his hands. “I've not played it since. I'd have the tune out of my head with a spoon if I could.”
>
> “You might have sent word.”
>
> “By what, a rabbit?”

What changed: he tells it in order, in the idiom of his trade, and a stranger could now repeat it back: three years because he played too well, six more because the hill is large. He hurries past the part that shames him and stays on the tune. Her reply is the grievance she has carried for nine years, so the account is also the start of an argument. It took a paragraph, and nothing in it is a rule being taught.

## 6. Allies: two people on the same side who still want something

Two sisters are about to go into the assessor's office and lie about the age of a horse.

**House manner**

> “Ready?”
>
> “Ready.”
>
> “You know what to say.”
>
> “Nine years old. Sound in the wind.”
>
> “And if he looks at the teeth?”
>
> “He won't.”
>
> “I know.”

They agree about everything, so the lines can only pass the plan back and forth. Either sister could say any of them.

**To the standard**

> “You'll let me do the talking,” said Bea.
>
> “I always let you do the talking. I let you do the talking at Father's funeral and we came home with the wrong coffin handles.”
>
> “That was a misunderstanding about brass.”
>
> “I only mean I could say the part about the teeth. I've practised the part about the teeth.” Nell had, too, in the cart, under her breath, for six miles. “You never ask what I've practised.”
>
> “What have you practised?”
>
> “Well, I'm not telling you now.”

What changed: both want the horse passed, and that is not in question, so the scene is about what is between them. Nell wants to be trusted with a piece of it. Bea wants to manage, and not to hear about the handles again. The plan is still audible (the talking, the teeth) without being recited, and nobody says “ready.”

## 7. Thought: a mind at work, not a saying

A widow considers taking off her wedding ring.

**House manner**

> She looked at the ring. Thirty years was a long time to carry something that had never quite been hers. Some weights are only felt when they are set down.
>
> She took it off.

The middle two sentences could be printed on a card. They generalize, they flatter the reader with a feeling of depth, and they could belong to any widow in any story. Then the decision arrives without having come from anywhere.

**To the standard**

> She had meant to take the ring off at the funeral and had not, because Dora was watching, and then not at the lawyer's, because her knuckle had swollen in the heat. Now it was October and the knuckle had no excuse. She turned the ring a quarter-turn, the way she did when a shopkeeper was adding up too slowly. It had been his mother's. That was what she kept coming back to, like a tongue to a tooth: thirty years, and it had been bought for somebody else, by a man none of them had liked. She had been told so on her wedding night, as a compliment. She got it as far as the joint and stopped, and thought, quite distinctly, *Dora will want it*, and pushed it back on.

What changed: the thought has an occasion and a history, and it is made of her own particulars (Dora, the knuckle, the shopkeeper). It is not fair-minded; what decides her is a small meanness she does not examine. The passage ends somewhere it did not start, and the decision comes out of the thinking. No sentence in it could be lifted out as a saying.
