# Reader and editor briefs

Give the reader the assigned text and the material named in the brief. Preserve each first reading before discussion. In discussion, test the diagnosis against the passage. A writer's intention can explain a choice, and a first reader's reaction shows what the page actually carried.

## Scene comparison

Give both excerpts the same necessary context and identify them with neutral labels. Keep the writer's preference and experiment hypothesis private until the reader answers.

Read each piece once. What did each person want, and when did you realize it? Where did the exchange or action change what could happen next? Quote one line or moment that belongs especially to that character and place. Say which version you would keep reading, what made the difference, and what the other version does better. Say when the excerpts leave you without enough evidence to prefer one.

## Cold draft or fresh revision

**First read.** Give the reader the complete prose alone. Ask what story they experienced, where they started caring, where they lost the thread or stopped believing, and what the last scene did to their reading of the beginning. Get that account before giving the request or discussing repairs.

**Second read.** Give the complete request and relevant originals. Ask what the draft actually delivers through events and choices. Have the reader cite the requested experience and the specific scenes that fulfill or displace it. Distinguish what the user said from what the reader expected. Look for who controls the decisive action, what changes after a revelation, and what the ending pays off. Consider the scale the request calls for.

Ask for a few quoted craft findings from the whole story, with the most consequential weakness and something to protect. Consider voice, speech, setting, pace, and whether reference characters keep their temperament. Who could only speak that way? Where does a conversation the story promised fall away? Which place or detail is doing work? For a possible continuity concern, quote the relevant passages and account for chronology, viewpoint, and later revelations. Say whether the reader's first difficulty persisted after that context. A difference of taste can be useful to the writer.

## Line editing

Give the editor the whole structurally settled prose, the request, and relevant originals. Ask for an account of the voice at its best, a short list of passages to protect, and specific quoted notes in story order. Follow the sentence and paragraph rhythms, the words particular to a speaker, what each turn does, scene texture, and places where the writing draws away at the moment it should get closer. Offer a concrete way to investigate each major problem. Tell the writer when a passage needs restaging or a different shape. The writer makes the changes in the story's own voice.

## Focused dialogue check

Give the reader the exchange with the surrounding prose available to the reader at that point. Ask where they lost the thread or stopped believing the people. For each concern, quote the line and any words or action that make its referent and meaning available. Say what the listener could know or reasonably infer at that moment. Keep a later explanation visible as later information. The exchange may make an unresolved reference, evasion, or silence legible as a choice.

Ask what each person wanted from the exchange and whether a reply follows from what the speaker could hear or see. Look for a line that could belong to anyone, an account cut too short to retell, or a pivotal answer supplied by narration. Test what is gained or lost when an exchange becomes more direct or is allowed more room. Recheck repairs in the surrounding scene.
