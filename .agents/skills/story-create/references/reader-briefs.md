# Reader and editor briefs

These are the assignments the coordinator gives to readers. Use them as written, for Codex readers and for isolated Claude sessions alike, filling in the bracketed parts. Isolated sessions cannot open repository files, so paste the brief and every input it names.

Each brief says why the reading exists, because a reader who knows what the reading is for gives a better one. `story-create` governs who may hold each role, what each may see, and in what order. Nothing here loosens that isolation.

Four kinds of reading are used, and they should not blur:

- A **reader's account** is what happened to someone reading the story: where they leaned in, where they drifted, what they believed. Only a reader who has not seen the plan can give it.
- A **craft reading** is an editor's diagnosis of why: the prose, the talk, the people, the place, the shape. It quotes the text.
- A **line edit** works through a structurally settled draft passage by passage.
- A **plain reading** is a check that the talk can be followed. It is given by someone who has seen nothing but the prose, and it reports only what they could not follow.

Readers report honestly or the process is theatre. Competence is not the standard. A story that delivers its prompt in careful, flat prose should be told so, with the passages that show it, and a story that is alive should be told what must not be touched.

## A. Request reading

*For the promise keeper and the Claude outline reader, before either sees a plot. Inputs: the complete request with later directions, and the supplied originals.*

> You are one of two independent readers who will hold a short story to what its requester actually asked for. Below is the complete request, with any later directions in the requester's own words, and any reference images they supplied. You have not seen a plot or anyone's interpretation, and that is deliberate: your reading is the baseline a proposed story will be compared against.
>
> In a short account, say what you understand this person to want:
>
> - The distinctive pleasure of the premise: what would be disappointing to lose.
> - Tone and scale.
> - Any central relationship the request specifies.
> - The payoff a reader would anticipate.
>
> Separate what the request explicitly requires from what you are inferring, and ground each inference in the requester's words. Leave open what the request leaves open, and say where you are unsure.
>
> Then look at each reference image as casting, not as a costume list. In two or three sentences per character or place: what does it establish, and what temperament or mood does it express? Who is this person, judging by the picture? The writer will be asked the same question, and your answer is the check on theirs.
>
> [REQUEST] [IMAGES]

## B. Outline reading

*For the same two readers, separately, after their request readings are recorded. Inputs: the complete request, the originals, and the whole outline.*

> Here is the request again and the writer's outline. Read the outline's scene briefs and direction, and before you look at its statement of intent, answer from the beats alone.
>
> **1. The story these beats would deliver.** In plain language: who or what is at the dramatic centre, whose choices decide the conflict and the climax, what the main tension depends on, and what the ending asks a reader to value.
>
> **2. Compare that with your own request reading.** Cite the requester's words and specific beats, both for what is fulfilled and for what is not. Does the distinctive premise govern the developing conflict, the decisive action, and the ending, or does it appear in the setup and then give way to something else? Has an invented relationship, rule, or limitation moved the centre away from what was asked for? If the requester has already named a failure in an earlier attempt, say which changed beats avoid it. Give the strongest evidence that the outline delivers the request and the most serious risk that it delivers a different story. Where evidence is missing, say so; do not assume the prose will supply it.
>
> **3. Then, and only once the question of fulfilment is answered, read the craft plan** as an editor deciding whether this story can be written well at this length:
>
> - *Takes.* Is the chosen take the strongest of those sketched? Would you argue for a rejected one, or for one nobody sketched?
> - *Shape.* Do the scene briefs fit the length, with one scene given real room? Which scene would you cut or merge?
> - *People.* Is the cast small enough to know? From the notes on how each principal talks, would you be able to tell them apart on the page?
> - *Talk.* Is the conversation this story owes its reader named, and is it placed in a scene with room? In each scene brief, do the people want something from each other, including the ones on the same side? If someone will have to explain where they have been or how something works, has the writer said who tells it, why now, and why to this listener?
> - *Reference characters.* With the image in front of you: does each study describe the person in the picture, in temperament as well as appearance? Are the signature details planned into action, or only into a description? Has anything bold in the image been made timid?
> - *Setting.* Is this a chosen, particular place that will act on the story, or a category? What would you want to know about it that the place sheet does not say?
> - *Ending.* Is the last image loaded by what comes before, and is it the scale the request promises?
>
> Name the one thing most worth protecting and the one change that would most improve the story before a word is drafted.
>
> [REQUEST] [IMAGES] [OUTLINE]

## C. Voice trial

*For the same two outline readers, after the outline is approved. Inputs: the two openings, labelled only 1 and 2 in an order the coordinator chooses, with no indication of which the writer prefers.*

> Below are two openings for the story you have been reading the outline of, by the same writer, in two different voices. They are labelled 1 and 2 and you are not told which the writer favours.
>
> Read each as a stranger would read the first page of a story in a magazine. Then say:
>
> - Which one you would keep reading, and the sentence where you knew.
> - What each voice would make easy over the whole story, and what it would make hard.
> - Whether either sounds like a particular person telling this, or like capable narration that could belong to any story. Quote the line that decides it.
> - Whether the reference character or the setting is more present in one than the other.
>
> If neither has a pulse, say so, and say what a third attempt should try.
>
> [OPENING 1] [OPENING 2]

## D. Cold reading of a draft

*For a Codex reader and a Claude session that have seen nothing of the plan or its discussion. Inputs: the complete request, the whole prose, the originals wherever a character or place in them appears in the story, and the craft-reading questions below.*

> You are reading a short story cold. It was written to the request below. You have not seen its outline or any discussion of it, which is why your reading is valuable: you are the only kind of reader who can report what the story does to someone meeting it for the first time.
>
> Read it once, straight through, as a reader. Then answer in two parts, in this order, and do not let the second replace the first.
>
> **Part one: what happened to you.**
>
> - What held your attention or feeling, and where. Quote.
> - Where you drifted, got lost, stopped believing, or began to skim.
> - What you believe happened, in a few sentences.
> - Whether you got the distinctive experience the request promises, as opposed to its details merely being present.
> - The most consequential weakness, even if the story is good.
> - What is already working and must survive revision.
>
> Keep apart: what the request explicitly requires, what you infer it promises, problems you can demonstrate in the text, and matters of your own taste.
>
> **Part two: a craft reading.** Now read as the fiction editor of a good magazine who has seen ten thousand competent submissions and is looking for one that is alive. Quote the text for every judgment.
>
> 1. *Prose.* The two or three best sentences and the two or three weakest. Does the page move at more than one speed, or is it one length of sentence and paragraph throughout? Where does the narration sound like a particular mind, and where does it go generic? Does the prose slow down and move closer at the moment that matters most, or get shorter and cooler? When the viewpoint character thinks, is it a mind working on something unsettled in its own words, or an aphorism, or the story's verdict handed to the character? Quote one of each if both are present.
> 2. *Dialogue.* Which characters could you recognize with the tags covered, and which could trade lines? Is anyone allowed to talk at length? Is any exchange there only to brief the reader? Are scenes built from strict alternation of short lines, echoed words, and dry last lines? Quote every line you had to read twice to know what it meant or who “he” or “it” was. Is there a conversation the story avoids: a question cut off with a dash, an “I know,” a sentence saying that nobody answered, at a point where you wanted to hear the reply? When two people on the same side talk, does either want anything from the other?
> 3. *Reference characters.* With the supplied image or source in front of you: is this that character, in look and in temperament, from first appearance to last? Which details does the story use in action, and which does it only list? If the request quotes the character, do they still sound like that line in the hardest scene?
> 4. *Setting.* In your own words, where and when are we? What does the place do to the people in it? Could the story move somewhere else unchanged? What did you smell, hear, or feel underfoot?
> 5. *Shape.* Does the story have room, or is it hurrying through a plan? Which scene deserved more space, and which could go? Is too much of the middle spent explaining how something works?
> 6. *The gap.* Would you take this for publication? Name the single largest thing standing between this draft and that.
>
> Do not soften the second part because the first was favourable. Many stories here satisfy their prompt and fail the craft reading, and the writer needs to be told which kind this is.
>
> [REQUEST] [IMAGES] [STORY]

## E. Line edit

*For one fresh Claude session, or the Codex `story_editor` if Claude is unavailable, once the story's structure is settled and before the fresh final readings. The editor has seen no earlier discussion. Inputs: the whole prose; the request; the originals; the craft standard from "What this collection does when nobody decides otherwise" through "Setting"; and the prose profile for this draft.*

> You are the line editor for a short story whose structure is settled. Scenes will not be added or removed now. Your job is the sentences, the talk, and the texture, and your standard is published literary short fiction. The writer will make the changes; you show where and why.
>
> You are given the story, the request it was written to, any reference images, a description of this collection's habitual manner, and a measured profile of this draft. The profile is evidence about rhythm and dialogue, not a score. Use it to decide where to look.
>
> Read the story once without marking anything. Then work through it in order and return:
>
> **1. An overall note**, a paragraph: what this story's voice is at its best, quoting the passage where it is most itself, so the writer knows what to edit toward.
>
> **2. Passage notes**, in story order. For each: quote the passage, name what is wrong in a phrase, and say what to try. Cover at least:
>
> - Runs of same-length sentences or one-line paragraphs, and where a run should become one developed paragraph or sentence.
> - Places where the prose steps around a feeling or an event that the story has earned.
> - Each stretch of dialogue: who sounds like themselves and who has drifted into the common voice; strict alternation; echoed-word questions; lines that exist to explain; a speaker who should be given room to talk.
> - Replies that end a conversation instead of answering it: “I know,” “Yes,” an echo, a question that only cues the next fact; and sentences reporting that someone did not or could not answer. Say what the character might say if they were not allowed to stop there.
> - Lines that open on a pronoun or a bare question with nothing behind them, and replies that only someone who had read the plan could give.
> - An account of past events cut so short it cannot be retold from the speech alone.
> - Narration that explains what a spoken line meant or how to take it.
> - In the viewpoint character's thoughts: aphorisms, sentences that could be lifted out and quoted, and fair-minded verdicts that arrive too soon.
> - Each reference character's appearances after the first: where their look or temperament has faded, and which signature detail could return.
> - Each scene's opening: whether we know where and when we are, and whether anything but sight is engaged.
> - Details that are the first one anyone would think of, and things that should have a proper name.
> - Mannerisms: "for a moment," "seemed to," "something like," "That was all," "It was not X; it was Y," reflexive threes, a paragraph that ends by restating itself.
> - Clarifying clauses that read as patches. Say whether to restage or cut.
>
> You may offer a rewritten sentence or line to show what you mean, at most one for each kind of problem. The writer owns the prose and will find their own.
>
> **3. A keep list**: the sentences, exchanges, and images that are already at the standard and must not be smoothed away.
>
> **4. Anything that is not a line-level problem.** If the draft cannot reach the standard without a structural change, say so plainly and stop. Do not polish around it.
>
> [STORY] [REQUEST] [IMAGES] [CRAFT STANDARD SECTIONS] [PROSE PROFILE]

## F. Fresh final reading

*For a new Codex reader and a new Claude session that saw neither the outline nor any draft discussion. Inputs: the complete request, the current whole prose, the originals where relevant.*

> You are the last reader before this story goes to the person who asked for it. Everyone else who has read it knows what it was supposed to be. You do not, so you are the only one who can say what it is.
>
> Read it once as a reader. Then, before giving any advice, describe the story you actually experienced:
>
> - What you cared about, and when you started caring.
> - Where your attention or belief broke.
> - Whether the request's distinctive promise was fulfilled in the experience, not only in the details.
> - Anything in the choices, the dialogue, or the physical and temporal logic that did not hold.
> - Whether the ending earned its effect.
>
> Then give the same six-part craft reading as in brief D, with quotations: prose and thought, dialogue, reference characters, setting, shape, and the gap.
>
> Finish with two answers. If this arrived on your desk at a magazine, would you publish it as it stands? And what is the most consequential reason to hesitate?
>
> [REQUEST] [IMAGES] [STORY]

## G. Plain reading

*For one new, isolated Claude session each time it is run: once on the first complete draft, alongside the cold readings, and once more after the last change to the prose, before the final fidelity gate. If Claude is unavailable, a fresh Codex reader that has seen nothing of this story. Input: the whole prose and nothing else. No request, no outline, no images, no notes.*

*Why it exists: every other reader has seen the request, and a model resolves an unanchored “he” or a clipped fragment so easily that it does not notice doing it. The person the story is for cannot. This reading is built to make that work visible. The late run matters most, because repairs made in the last round are where opaque lines enter.*

> You are checking whether a stranger can follow what the people in this story say to each other. You have been given the story and nothing else, on purpose. Everyone else who has read it knows what it is supposed to say, and they can no longer tell which lines depend on knowing.
>
> You are a far better guesser than the person this story is for. From what comes later you can work out what almost any line must have meant. That ability is the problem being tested, so do not use it. A reader meets each line once, at reading speed, knowing only what came before it.
>
> **Part one: the ledger.** Go through the story in order and number every spoken line. For each, give three things, briefly:
>
> - *Speaker and listener.*
> - *Plain meaning,* in twelve words or fewer, with every “he,” “she,” “it,” “they,” “that,” “this,” and “there” replaced by what it stands for.
> - *Anchor:* the earlier words, quoted, that tell the listener and the reader what is meant. Earlier means before this line. If the only support comes afterwards, or you supplied it by inference, write NONE.
>
> **Part two: findings.** List each line that fails, quoted, under one of these heads:
>
> - *No anchor.* A pronoun, a bare question, or a fragment that points at something not yet named.
> - *The listener could not have said that.* A reply to something nobody said aloud, or one that uses knowledge the page has not given that person.
> - *Cannot be restated.* You could not put the line in plain words without guessing.
> - *Speaker unclear.* You had to count back to know who was talking.
> - *Translated by the narrator.* The narration after the line says what it meant or how to take it.
> - *Not answered.* A question or statement cut off, met with “I know,” or followed by a sentence saying nobody replied, where you wanted the reply. Say what you wanted to hear.
>
> **Part three: retelling.** From the spoken words alone:
>
> - Retell any account a character gives of past events or of how something works. If you cannot retell it, say what is missing.
> - Say what the two central people said to each other in the conversation that mattered most. If that conversation is not on the page, say so.
>
> Then quote any sentence of narration, anywhere in the story, that you could not restate in plain words.
>
> Do not judge the prose or the plot, say whether the story is good, or propose rewrites. Report only what could not be followed.
>
> [STORY]

**Answering a plain reading.** Its findings are demonstrated problems and are not weighed as taste. The writer answers each by rewriting the line or restaging the moment. Two responses are not available. Explaining what the line means confirms the finding. Adding a sentence of narration that explains the line fixes nothing, and neither does a new spoken line whose only purpose is to supply the missing fact. A finding may stand only when the confusion is the point of the scene and someone in it registers the confusion. Send the reporting session the rewritten passages in context and ask whether each can now be followed.

## In the discussion

When readers, writer, and keeper exchange findings, each reader should:

- Stand by an effect they experienced. A writer's explanation of intent can clarify a choice; it does not undo what a cold reader felt.
- Change their mind for reasons in the text or the request, and say what the reason was. Do not agree because someone else sounds confident.
- Test proposed repairs by asking what they will cost: which good sentence, which silence, which strangeness goes if this is fixed that way?
- Prefer restaging or cutting to explaining. When the proposed repair is an added clause of clarification, say so.
- Keep repairs out of dialogue. When the answer to a gap in the logic is a new spoken line, ask who in the room needed telling.
- Do not file a line-level finding under taste because it is small. “I could not tell what this meant” and “these two could trade lines” are demonstrated problems. Disliking a line is taste only when the reader can say what the line means and why that person would say it.
- Keep craft and promise findings both alive. Neither is polish.
