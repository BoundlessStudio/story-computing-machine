# Story workshop

The aim is a story a reader wants to finish and remember that delivers the experience the user requested. Use the worktree attached to the current chat through Codex's built-in workflow, then read [AGENTS.md](../../../AGENTS.md) for permission and files. An existing Codex worktree is ready for production without another checkout or update from `main`. Use this as a practice, not a sequence of verdicts. Make room for discovery and revise the underlying scene or premise when a line edit cannot solve the problem.

## Begin with the story

Keep the user's `[WP]` request verbatim in `prompt.md`, appending later user directions without overwriting the original. Separate the user's words from editorial interpretations. Record the requested audience, intensity, and content direction required by the style guide; General is the starting target when none is specified. General describes content intensity, not narrative ambition or scale: adventure, conflict, spectacle, and grandeur remain available within those limits. List every supplied reference-image display name; save the original file under that name in the story's `art/references/` directory and inspect it before creative work. If one cannot be accessed, restore access or ask for reattachment; never silently omit it. These input images do not establish canon. Read relevant [universe authority](../../../universe/README.md) and the short [style guide](../../../universe/style-guide.md); search only facts the story will use. For an existing story, verify its canon marker before reading it for an edit.

Before choosing a plot, start the existing Codex outline reader fresh as the continuing **promise keeper**, without inherited conversation (`fork_turns="none"`). Give it only the complete user request, including all later user directions, and relevant supplied originals, without the coordinator's interpretation, proposed plot, outline, or prior conversation. Ask it to record in its own words the distinctive pleasure, tone, scale, any specified central relationship, and anticipated payoff it understands the user to want. Ground inferences in the user's words, separate them from explicit requirements, and leave unspecified elements and uncertainty visible. Preserve that short attributed first understanding in `notes.md` before exposing it to the outline or editorial framing. This uses an existing reader position, not a new panel. For a substantial authorized revision, do this before showing the keeper the existing prose or proposed repair; retain the package's layout. Give the keeper later user directions in the user's words and let it update its understanding before relaying others' interpretations.

The writer and coordinator assess prompt fulfillment before choosing a plot: who or what matters, what experience is distinctive, what could change, and what scenes could deliver it. Every writer and reader assignment must preserve the complete request, including later directions, rather than replace it with a summary. Do not add unsupported constraints such as “keep the stakes small” or “avoid a major conflict.” Writers and keepers should challenge editorial directions that diminish the request. A story need not invent a public crisis, but do not suppress adventure, conflict, spectacle, or grandeur when appropriate. Judge familiar tropes by their specific, satisfying execution, rather than rejecting the “obvious” solution merely for being familiar.

For a new `[WP]`, begin `outline.md` with a short statement of the experience promised by the complete request: distinctive pleasure, tone, scale, any specified central relationship, and anticipated payoff, incorporating later user directions. Ground interpretations in the user's words, distinguish them from explicit requirements, and leave unspecified elements open. Put the writer's chosen relationship, conflict, limitations, and ending in the provisional direction. An invented relationship must not be presented as something the user requested. Identify concrete scenes that could deliver the promise through action, choices, turns, and an ending. Keep this short, in whatever form helps; it is a provisional thinking aid, not a scene quota or a contract. Preserve an early version in Git when discussion materially changes it. Let the writer choose length, voice, structure, and ending within the user's requirements; the prose may discover a better route. Record a meaningful departure in `notes.md` rather than rewriting the outline to pretend it predicted the story.

## Test the outline against the request

Use the existing Codex keeper and one independent Claude session for the outline discussion before drafting. If Claude is unavailable, use another independent reader and record the substitution, subject to AGENTS.md's authentication requirements. First give Claude only the complete request and relevant supplied originals, with the same request-reading assignment the keeper received before plot selection. Preserve its short independent understanding in `notes.md` before exposing it to the outline. Each reader now has an understanding formed without the proposed plot. Keep those original accounts visible; revise an inference only with a reason grounded in the request, and preserve the reason. A writer's explanation or agreement among readers does not supply that evidence.

Then give both readers the complete request and outline, separately, without each other's findings. Before offering repairs, each must describe in plain language the story the proposed beats would actually deliver: who or what occupies its dramatic center, whose choices determine the conflict and climax, what the main tension depends on, and what the ending asks the reader to value. Derive this account from the beats rather than adopting the outline's promised-experience paragraph. This makes a shift from the user's premise to the writer's preferred relationship or theme visible.

Each reader must compare that actual story with its preserved request reading. Support both fulfillment claims and objections with the user's words and concrete outline beats. If the user has identified a previous failure, address that correction explicitly and show which changed beats avoid repeating it; new names, mechanics, or declarations of intent cannot establish this. Examine how the distinctive premise governs the developing conflict, the decisive action, and the ending; a mention in the setup or one spectacular incident cannot establish sustained delivery. Check whether added rules or limitations deepen the requested experience or transfer its central agency and tension to someone else. Test whether the climax and aftermath reward the relationship, authority, discovery, dread, comedy, or other pleasure the request promises. A quiet ending can carry the consequences of grandeur; its intimacy must earn its place in that experience. When uncertain, name the missing evidence rather than assuming prose will provide it.

For example, an outline about a woman feared by gods can contain a kneeling emperor and a slain god while making the emperor's physical frailty the governing tension and his care the principal payoff. Those included details do not establish the woman's sustained authority. A review must identify the beats where her presence and choices govern the encounter and its consequences, or call for a change to the dramatic center. More destruction, an extra kneeling scene, or clearer instructions for containing her power would leave that displacement unresolved. Apply this reasoning to the particular request; it does not require every story to be grand or every powerful character to be invulnerable.

Each reader should identify the strongest possibility to protect and the most consequential risk, including the strongest evidence that the outline could be delivering a different story from the one requested. A favorable conclusion still needs scene evidence; inventing an objection is unnecessary. Preserve short, attributed first findings in `notes.md` before sharing them. Then relay each reader's actual comparison, objection, and evidence to the writer and the other reader. Discuss prompt fulfillment before refining the plot's plausibility. Let participants question inferred promises and test the writer's proposed direction against the user's words. Mechanical repairs can follow once the direction is supported; they cannot settle a mismatch in dramatic center or payoff.

Before drafting, the coordinator records a concise, dated comparison in `notes.md`: the request being served, the outline's actual dramatic center, the concrete scene and ending evidence for fulfillment, and how material mismatches were resolved. This requires the coordinator's own judgment even when both readers approve. Generic praise, absence of objections, authorial intent, and the presence of requested nouns are insufficient. If the evidence shows displacement, revise the relationship, agency, conflict, or payoff and have both existing outline readers test the changed beats against their request readings. Defer drafting until that comparison supports the direction. Interpretations remain open to reasoned challenge; an explicit user requirement cannot be waived. No extra reader panel, scorecard, outline PASS, fixed story shape, or fixed round count is required. These readers and Claude sessions have seen the plan and cannot be cold prose readers later. A substantial edit of an older package may discuss a new direction without adding `outline.md` or changing that package's layout.

The keeper continues through the draft and revisions, comparing the current dramatic center, agency, main tension, and ending with the request and the evidence used to support the outline. Check whether the premise's distinctive elements drive events and revelations change the story's possibilities or choices. Literal inclusion is insufficient. When grandeur is requested, powerful characters should exercise consequential power; a brief spectacle followed by an unrelated modest resolution may still miss the promise. Raise material failures at the outline or structural level before polishing, including drift from an initially sound direction. Better dialogue, clearer mechanics, or household-task symbolism cannot supply a missing experience. Keep the remit focused on the request rather than turning the keeper into another general craft reviewer. Its informed continuity cannot substitute for cold prose readers.

Use the collection-variation safeguard when choosing and revising the direction: flag recurring defaults, especially extraordinary premises reduced to humble work, therapeutic conversations, or maintenance. Ask whether that choice serves this request or displaces it. These approaches remain available; do not force every story toward spectacle or artificial novelty, or read the collection without a useful comparison in mind.

Give a Codex writer agent ownership of the outline and prose and room to make substantive choices. The coordinator owns editorial judgment, not the sentences or a replacement for the user's request. The writer may use `creative-writing-craft` for a specific craft need and `dialogue`, `story-sense`, or `prose-style` to diagnose a problem. Use those references only for the problem at hand; they do not add stages or separate reports. The writer reads the complete story aloud or skeptically in mind before handoff, watching fulfillment of the promised experience, reader knowledge, scene consequences, dialogue uptake, and whether the ending earns its effect. Commit the first complete draft before review so later changes can be understood.

The outline round-robin is required for a new `[WP]`: after independent first reads, the writer, Codex reader, and Claude must receive one another's actual concerns and respond before drafting. Do not substitute a bundle of separate reviews or a coordinator summary for their exchange.

## Read independently, then talk

For a new complete story or a substantial rewrite of a non-canon story, obtain a cold Codex reader subagent and a cold Claude reading. If Claude cannot be used, recruit another independent reader and say what changed. Use new readers who did not join the outline talk. Start the Codex reader fresh without the coordinator's prior conversation; paste the complete prompt and current prose into its assignment, or direct it to read only those two named files in the absolute worktree. Keep that same reader available for discussion. Give each reader only those inputs, without the outline, `notes.md`, prior reviews, the writer's explanation, or the other reader's response. If a supplied original image carries a binding detail or is needed to judge the story, provide that original to both readers as well.

For each independent Claude first read (outline, draft, or final), create a separate temporary directory under the active worktree's `tmp/` and start a new session of the normal authenticated CLI there. Use `--safe-mode` to disable automatically loaded `CLAUDE.md`, skills, plugins, and other customizations so repository or parent instructions do not contaminate the cold read; pin `--model claude-opus-5-5`, use `--tools ""`, `--strict-mcp-config`, and `--output-format json`. At the outline stage, send only the complete request and relevant originals for the initial understanding, then resume that session with the complete request and outline for its independent comparison. At draft and final stages, pipe the complete prompt with only the target whole prose through stdin. Use stdin rather than a Windows command argument for all review inputs. Set `TMPDIR`, `TMP`, and `TEMP` to the worktree's absolute `tmp/` path in the subprocess environment. When an original image is needed, copy it into that session's temporary directory and grant only the Read tool for that input. Keep each session ID and resume that session from the same directory with the same isolation flags for its discussion. Do not expose other files or feedback until each independent first reading is delivered. After both readings in a pair arrive, preserve short, attributed excerpts of their observations in their own words in `notes.md` before discussion; keep the coordinator's interpretation separate and do not rewrite those observations when opinions change.

Use the `claude.ai` subscription for these readings. Before the first call, clear inherited `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` from the Claude subprocess environment and check `claude auth status` there for `authMethod: claude.ai` and an active `subscriptionType`. If a call reports low API credit, correct the authentication and retry through the subscription; do not treat that billing error as Claude being unavailable.

Ask each reader what held attention or feeling, where the story lost them, what they believed happened, whether they experienced the prompt's distinctive promise, and which passages created those effects. Ask for the most consequential weakness even when the story is broadly successful. Have them distinguish explicit requirements, inferred promises, demonstrated problems, and taste preferences, and identify what is already working and should survive revision. A checklist, number, or PASS is not a substitute for a reader's account. The continuing keeper also reads the current prose against the complete request; keep its feedback separate until the cold first readings are delivered.

Then convene an actual exchange. Relay each participant's own words, including the keeper's focused findings, to the others instead of replacing them with the coordinator's summary. Resume Claude's same session for follow-up; if that session is lost, record the break honestly. Let the writer explain intent, challenge a reading, and propose or reject repairs. Ask the readers and keeper to clarify their evidence, respond to each other, and test proposed changes. The coordinator must answer evidence-based objections against the complete request and the text. Do not settle disagreements by vote: majority approval cannot override an explicit user requirement, and the keeper's inferences do not become requirements merely by being recorded. An explanation of authorial intent can clarify a deliberate effect; it does not erase an effect a cold reader experienced. Keep reasoned dissent visible when the choice is to preserve the passage.

Address material failures to deliver the requested premise or experience before surface repairs; resolve them in the underlying scenes or structure. Then focus on the issues with the greatest effect on the story: attachment and desire, causal movement, scene purpose, point of view, dialogue, revelation, and ending. Use specific passages. Widen a revision when the cause is structural and the authorized scope permits; protect strong scenes and lines that a proposed fix would damage. If a repair repeatedly fails, reconsider the scene's job or the story's premise instead of polishing the same surface.

## Revise and remember

The writer revises `story.md` directly. Commit each substantial editorial round with a plain description of the change. Reassess material choices against the complete request with the keeper during revision, including later user directions, and continue the conversation with the same readers to find whether the reported effect has changed and what the revision may have broken. After a structural rewrite that changes the premise, scene order, point of view, or ending, obtain a fresh cold reading; returning readers already know the intended repair. After the last substantial revision, require a returning independent reader to read the entire current prose from beginning to end, not just the diff. Use contextual checks for local repairs; minor wording changes do not restart the outline, draft, and final stages or need a separate keeper review. Repeat discussion and revision while a material issue warrants action; an unmet explicit requirement cannot be waived as editorial taste. There is no required round count.

## Read the finished story with fresh eyes

When the coordinator, keeper, and returning readers have examined material promise objections and the text appears to deliver the request, recruit a new Codex reader and a new Claude session that saw neither the outline nor the draft conversation. Give each only the complete prompt, including later user directions, and current whole prose, independently, with the same cold-read isolation as above. Do not include the keeper's interpretation or findings. Ask each to describe the story they actually experienced before advising on it: what they cared about, where attention or belief broke, whether the prompt's distinctive promise was fulfilled, and the most consequential reason they would hesitate to publish. Invite scrutiny of choice, dialogue uptake, physical and temporal logic, and the ending, but do not turn that into a box-ticking report. The aim is to expose assumptions the revision room has normalized.

Save their first effects in `notes.md` before sharing opinions. Relay each fresh reader's actual finding to the writer and the other fresh reader; include the keeper's final comparison with the request, then bring in earlier readers where useful. Ask them to test one another's evidence, the writer's intent, and proposed repairs rather than vote on readiness. The writer repairs at the scale the cause needs. After a substantial change, both fresh final readers reread the complete story and discuss whether the reported effect changed or another problem appeared; after a local change, the reader whose material concern prompted it checks the passage in whole-story context and the coordinator judges it. If distinct concerns prompted the edit, each reporting reader checks their effect. A structural reworking also needs another genuinely fresh reading, because the discussing readers know the intended fix. Continue while a material concern warrants work, not for a preset number of turns or unanimous agreement. Record the reason for accepting or declining a well-supported objection; do not decline an explicit user requirement because the room prefers another story.

Use `notes.md` as a compact, dated memory for the next writer or editor. Keep both outline readers' short request-only understandings, their comparisons with the proposed story, the coordinator's evidence for beginning the draft, prose readers' original effects and passages, fresh final findings, the writer's responses, the coordinator's reasons for resolving objections, what changed, what rereading showed, and important unresolved disagreement. Include short before/after excerpts where Git's eventual merge style might otherwise hide the actual repair. Record decisions and lessons, not a full transcript, scorecard, or separate process report. A later round can correct an earlier diagnosis without erasing it.

The final whole-story round-robin is required for a new `[WP]`: the writer, fresh Codex reader, and fresh Claude session must respond to one another's actual first effects before the coordinator declares readiness. Recheck revisions at the scale described above; independent final reads alone do not complete the discussion.

Before handoff, confirm that prompt fulfillment has held through the final revisions; it is an ongoing test, not a late check after deciding the prose works. Check relevant shared facts, names that might confuse readers, and internal time, space, knowledge, and object continuity. Resolve a canon conflict with the user; do not distort the story to satisfy a routine check. The coordinator states why the current version delivers the request and is ready, including any deliberate dissent, in `notes.md` and the pull request. No SHA-256 pin, script, test suite, or numeric review verdict certifies the story.

## Art and handoff

After the prose is ready, make the character and location references first for a new `[WP]` story unless the user asks for prose only. Identify the principal characters and the distinct locations important to the finished story before choosing a cover scene. Make reference sheets for those characters and location paintings for those places, including important settings that will not appear on the cover. A story with multiple important settings needs references for at least two distinct locations; different views or corrections of one place do not count as different locations. If the story takes place entirely in one setting, reference that setting without inventing another. The reference art should help a later artist recognize the story's characters and places without adding unsupported facts.

Make a separate character sheet for each principal character on a plain white background, as a landscape 1536×1024 WebP image by default, labeled with that character's name and the story title. Exactly one character belongs on each sheet; group or ensemble sheets do not satisfy this requirement. Multiple views, poses, and expressions of that same character belong together on its sheet. A multi-headed creature still counts as one character. Show the same identity and clothing across multiple full-body angles or poses, at least one face or expression study, and story-relevant props when useful. Adapt the views to a nonhuman character if needed; a single scene portrait does not count as the sheet. Location paintings should establish each place's story-relevant geography, architecture, layout, and light from the prose and supplied references. Use existing collection sheets or paintings as style references when useful, and inspect any existing art before reuse. Default to the collection's Group of Seven inspired oil style unless the prompt asks otherwise; follow the [collection art direction](../../../universe/art.md).

Complete and inspect the full character and location reference set before generating the cover. Use the relevant accepted references as visual inputs for `title-image.jpg`, a 9:16 portrait JPEG at 864×1536, with the exact story title rendered once as part of the image rather than added afterward. Compose a novel cover that invites the right story. The cover follows the references; do not derive the reference set from the cover or limit it to the cover's subjects. Use the built-in image-generation skill for all art, attach relevant inspected originals from this story's `art/references/` and accepted references, and inspect the saved pixels for story fidelity, coherent objects and anatomy, consistent character identity across sheet panels and the cover, readable sheet labels and cover title, and consistent locations.

Put accepted character art in `art/characters/` and location paintings in `art/landscapes/` or `art/interiors/`, using descriptive names for the actual characters and places. Keep only accepted final images in the package, along with supplied originals in `art/references/`. Do not create paired “Original” and “Selected” location images or a new `art/selection-notes.json`. Correct a candidate when needed and keep drafts, superseded candidates, and rejected images under the worktree's `tmp/`; Git history preserves revisions to previously committed art. Further art is optional. Before the rating and draft pull request, confirm that the principal characters and important distinct locations have inspected references and the cover agrees with them. No gallery capture or site build is part of `[WP]`.

**Required AO3 rating gate:** After the final whole-story discussion and all resulting prose revisions are complete, and after any selected cover is finished and inspected, rate the finished prose and cover together under the shared General, Teen, Mature, or Explicit definitions. If the user requested prose only, rate the finished prose. Record the classification, a brief reason, and specific content notes in `ratings.md` before handoff or a draft pull request; do not assign General automatically. Reassess the rating if either the prose or cover changes afterward. For a substantial authorized revision, review the existing rating after the revised work is final and update it if needed.

Commit the story, notes, and selected art on the story branch. Push and open a draft pull request for the user to review. Do not merge it automatically. A localized prose edit that changes meaning, action, or voice needs one independent contextual read of the changed passage and a brief note, rather than a full discussion round. The coordinator rereads a purely mechanical correction. A substantive rewrite deserves fresh whole-story reading. Preserve legacy package layouts and prior reviews as history.
