# Story workshop

The aim is a story a reader wants to finish and remember that delivers the experience the user requested. Use the worktree attached to the current chat through Codex's built-in workflow, then read [AGENTS.md](../../../AGENTS.md) for permission and files. An existing Codex worktree is ready for production without another checkout or update from `main`. Use this as a practice, not a sequence of verdicts. Make room for discovery and revise the underlying scene or premise when a line edit cannot solve the problem.

Delivering the request is half of the aim. The other half is the writing itself: voice, dialogue, characters who stay themselves, and a place that matters. [The craft standard](references/craft-standard.md) describes that bar and the collection's measured habits that fall short of it. The writer reads it before outlining and before revising, and every prose reader is briefed to test for it. A story that honors its prompt in flat, hurried, same-voiced prose is not ready.

## The process at a glance

Each step is described below. The order matters: decisions are tested before prose is written, structure is settled before sentences are edited, and every reading is independent before it is discussed.

1. **Intake.** Preserve the request and originals; read the relevant universe notes.
2. **Request readings.** The promise keeper, then Claude, record what the request asks for before any plot exists.
3. **Story development and outline.** The writer finds three takes, chooses one, studies the reference characters, chooses the place, names the conversation the story owes, and writes the outline with its craft plan and scene briefs.
4. **Outline discussion.** Both outline readers compare the beats with the request, then test the craft plan. Round-robin with the writer.
5. **Outline fidelity gate** (Decisions).
6. **Voice trial.** Two openings in different voices; the outline readers choose blind.
7. **Draft in scenes**, then the writer's own read-through and prose profile.
8. **Cold readings and a plain reading.** New readers give a reader's account and a craft reading; one more, given the prose alone, lists the talk that cannot be followed. Round-robin; structural revision.
9. **Line edit.** One fresh editor works through the settled draft.
10. **Fresh final readings.** New readers again. Round-robin; final repairs.
11. **Last plain reading**, then the **final fidelity gate** (Decisions), continuity check, readiness statement.
12. **Art, rating, pull request.**

The reading assignments for steps 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, and 10 are written out in [Reader and editor briefs](references/reader-briefs.md). Use them as written for Codex readers and Claude sessions alike.

## Begin with the story

Record the supplied original filenames as factual metadata under `## Supplied reference images` in `prompt.md`, one filename per bullet, or `- None` when there are no images. Keep this inventory after the verbatim request and separate from editorial interpretation. The fidelity helper checks it against the originals on disk; update the inventory when later images arrive without overwriting the user's original words.

Keep the user's `[WP]` request verbatim in `prompt.md`, appending later user directions without overwriting the original. Separate the user's words from editorial interpretations. Record the requested audience, intensity, and content direction required by the style guide; General is the starting target when none is specified. General describes content intensity, not narrative ambition or scale: adventure, conflict, spectacle, and grandeur remain available within those limits. List every supplied reference-image display name; save the original file under that name in the story's `art/references/` directory and inspect it before creative work. When an original shows a character, treat it as casting: the writer studies who the picture shows, not only what they wear, and carries that study into the outline as the craft standard describes. If one cannot be accessed, restore access or ask for reattachment; never silently omit it. These input images do not establish canon. Read relevant [universe authority](../../../universe/README.md) and the short [style guide](../../../universe/style-guide.md); search only facts the story will use. For an existing story, verify its canon marker before reading it for an edit.

Before choosing a plot, start the existing Codex outline reader fresh as the continuing **promise keeper**, without inherited conversation (`fork_turns="none"`). Give it only the complete user request, including all later user directions, and relevant supplied originals, without the coordinator's interpretation, proposed plot, outline, or prior conversation. Ask it to record in its own words the distinctive pleasure, tone, scale, any specified central relationship, and anticipated payoff it understands the user to want. Ground inferences in the user's words, separate them from explicit requirements, and leave unspecified elements and uncertainty visible. Preserve that short attributed first understanding in `notes.md` before exposing it to the outline or editorial framing. This uses an existing reader position, not a new panel. For a substantial authorized revision, do this before showing the keeper the existing prose or proposed repair; retain the package's layout. Give the keeper later user directions in the user's words and let it update its understanding before relaying others' interpretations.

The writer and coordinator assess prompt fulfillment before choosing a plot: who or what matters, what experience is distinctive, what could change, and what scenes could deliver it. Every writer and reader assignment must preserve the complete request, including later directions, rather than replace it with a summary. Do not add unsupported constraints such as “keep the stakes small” or “avoid a major conflict.” Writers and keepers should challenge editorial directions that diminish the request. A story need not invent a public crisis, but do not suppress adventure, conflict, spectacle, or grandeur when appropriate. Judge familiar tropes by their specific, satisfying execution, rather than rejecting the “obvious” solution merely for being familiar.

For a new `[WP]`, begin `outline.md` with a short statement of the experience promised by the complete request: distinctive pleasure, tone, scale, any specified central relationship, and anticipated payoff, incorporating later user directions. Ground interpretations in the user's words, distinguish them from explicit requirements, and leave unspecified elements open. Put the writer's chosen relationship, conflict, limitations, and ending in the provisional direction. An invented relationship must not be presented as something the user requested. Identify concrete scenes that could deliver the promise through action, choices, turns, and an ending. Keep this short, in whatever form helps; it is a provisional thinking aid, not a scene quota or a contract. Preserve an early version in Git when discussion materially changes it. Let the writer choose length, voice, structure, and ending within the user's requirements; the prose may discover a better route. Record a meaningful departure in `notes.md` rather than rewriting the outline to pretend it predicted the story.

## Find the story and plan the writing

The first plot to arrive is rarely the best one, and the decisions that most affect quality (whose story, where, how few scenes, how people talk) are cheap to change now and expensive later. The writer works through [From prompt to outline](references/story-development.md) and [the craft standard](references/craft-standard.md), with [Craft examples](references/craft-examples.md) for what the standard looks like on the page. After the promised-experience statement, `outline.md` carries:

- **Takes considered.** Three structurally different stories this request could be, two or three sentences each: a different centre, starting point, place, relationship, or register. At least one plays the prompt straight at full scale. The writer chooses the take whose central scene is strongest and in which the request's distinctive pleasure does the most work, and says why. A fresh angle that sidelines the promise is the wrong choice.
- **Provisional direction.** The chosen story, as described above.
- **Craft plan.** Decisions with reasons:
  - *Teller and voice.* Who narrates, from what distance and time, in what temper, and the two voices to be tried in the voice trial.
  - *Cast and speech.* The two or three people the reader will know, what each wants, and how each talks: how much, from what vocabulary, what they avoid. A quoted line in the prompt sets that character's voice. Add what each knows and has wrong when the story opens, and what each wants from the other in the scenes they share, named for people on the same side as well as for opponents.
  - *The conversation owed.* In a plain sentence, what these people have to say to each other before the story can end, and the scene that will hold it. If someone must give an account of past events, say who tells it, why now, and why to this listener.
  - *Reference character studies.* For each character shown in a supplied original or taken from `universe/characters.md`: what the source establishes, the temperament it expresses, how they sound, the three or four signature details and where each will be used in action, and anything translated for rating or tone. An image is casting, not a costume list.
  - *Setting.* The place chosen over two named alternatives, and why it presses hardest on the premise; then a short place sheet with its name, season and hour, working life, five sensory facts of which two are not visual, and what it makes hard and possible for these people.
- **Scene briefs.** The two to four scenes the story will inhabit. For each: where and when, who wants what from whom, what each knows that the other does not, what must be said aloud, the turn, the detail to remember, and its share of the length. Mark the long scene. List what is summarized or left out.
- **The ending.** The final act or image and the earlier moments that load it.

This runs a page or two. It is as provisional as the rest of the outline, but its questions are not optional: a draft begun without a chosen take, a decided teller, a small cast, a studied reference character, a particular place, and a conversation it means to have will fall back on the collection's habits, which are known and measured.

## Test the outline against the request

Use the existing Codex keeper and one independent Claude session for the outline discussion before drafting. Their assignments are briefs A and B in [Reader and editor briefs](references/reader-briefs.md). If Claude is unavailable, use another independent reader and record the substitution, subject to AGENTS.md's authentication requirements. First give Claude only the complete request and relevant supplied originals, with the same request-reading assignment the keeper received before plot selection. Preserve its short independent understanding in `notes.md` before exposing it to the outline. Each reader now has an understanding formed without the proposed plot. Keep those original accounts visible; revise an inference only with a reason grounded in the request, and preserve the reason. A writer's explanation or agreement among readers does not supply that evidence.

Then give both readers the complete request and outline, separately, without each other's findings. Before offering repairs, each must describe in plain language the story the proposed beats would actually deliver: who or what occupies its dramatic center, whose choices determine the conflict and climax, what the main tension depends on, and what the ending asks the reader to value. Derive this account from the beats rather than adopting the outline's promised-experience paragraph. This makes a shift from the user's premise to the writer's preferred relationship or theme visible.

Each reader must compare that actual story with its preserved request reading. Support both fulfillment claims and objections with the user's words and concrete outline beats. If the user has identified a previous failure, address that correction explicitly and show which changed beats avoid repeating it; new names, mechanics, or declarations of intent cannot establish this. Examine how the distinctive premise governs the developing conflict, the decisive action, and the ending; a mention in the setup or one spectacular incident cannot establish sustained delivery. Check whether added rules or limitations deepen the requested experience or transfer its central agency and tension to someone else. Test whether the climax and aftermath reward the relationship, authority, discovery, dread, comedy, or other pleasure the request promises. A quiet ending can carry the consequences of grandeur; its intimacy must earn its place in that experience. When uncertain, name the missing evidence rather than assuming prose will provide it.

For example, an outline about a woman feared by gods can contain a kneeling emperor and a slain god while making the emperor's physical frailty the governing tension and his care the principal payoff. Those included details do not establish the woman's sustained authority. A review must identify the beats where her presence and choices govern the encounter and its consequences, or call for a change to the dramatic center. More destruction, an extra kneeling scene, or clearer instructions for containing her power would leave that displacement unresolved. Apply this reasoning to the particular request; it does not require every story to be grand or every powerful character to be invulnerable.

Each reader should identify the strongest possibility to protect and the most consequential risk, including the strongest evidence that the outline could be delivering a different story from the one requested. A favorable conclusion still needs scene evidence; inventing an objection is unnecessary. Preserve short, attributed first findings in `notes.md` before sharing them. Then relay each reader's actual comparison, objection, and evidence to the writer and the other reader. Discuss prompt fulfillment before refining the plot's plausibility. Let participants question inferred promises and test the writer's proposed direction against the user's words. Mechanical repairs can follow once the direction is supported; they cannot settle a mismatch in dramatic center or payoff.

Before drafting, the coordinator records a concise, dated comparison in `notes.md`: the request being served, the outline's actual dramatic center, the concrete scene and ending evidence for fulfillment, and how material mismatches were resolved. This requires the coordinator's own judgment even when both readers approve. Generic praise, absence of objections, authorial intent, and the presence of requested nouns are insufficient. If the evidence shows displacement, revise the relationship, agency, conflict, or payoff and have both existing outline readers test the changed beats against their request readings. Defer drafting until that comparison supports the direction. Interpretations remain open to reasoned challenge; an explicit user requirement cannot be waived. No extra reader panel, scorecard, outline PASS, fixed story shape, or fixed round count is required. These readers and Claude sessions have seen the plan and cannot be cold prose readers later. A substantial edit of an older package may discuss a new direction without adding `outline.md` or changing that package's layout.

Once the promise comparison supports the direction, both outline readers test the rest of the outline as editors, using the third part of brief B: whether the chosen take is the strongest, whether the scene briefs fit the length and give one scene real room, whether the cast is small enough to know and its voices distinguishable, whether each reference character study describes the person in the supplied original in temperament as well as appearance and plans its details into action, whether the setting is a chosen, particular place that will act on the story, and whether the ending is loaded and of the right scale. Each names the one thing most worth protecting and the one change that would most improve the story before a word is drafted. The writer answers and revises the outline; the coordinator records the outcome. This belongs to the same discussion. It does not reopen a settled promise question, and it cannot substitute for one.

The keeper continues through the draft and revisions, comparing the current dramatic center, agency, main tension, and ending with the request and the evidence used to support the outline. Check whether the premise's distinctive elements drive events and revelations change the story's possibilities or choices. Literal inclusion is insufficient. When grandeur is requested, powerful characters should exercise consequential power; a brief spectacle followed by an unrelated modest resolution may still miss the promise. Raise material failures at the outline or structural level before polishing, including drift from an initially sound direction. Better dialogue, clearer mechanics, or household-task symbolism cannot supply a missing experience. Keep the remit focused on the request rather than turning the keeper into another general craft reviewer. Its informed continuity cannot substitute for cold prose readers.

Use the collection-variation safeguard when choosing and revising the direction: flag recurring defaults, especially extraordinary premises reduced to humble work, therapeutic conversations, or maintenance. Ask whether that choice serves this request or displaces it. These approaches remain available; do not force every story toward spectacle or artificial novelty, or read the collection without a useful comparison in mind.

Give a Codex writer agent ownership of the outline and prose and room to make substantive choices. The coordinator owns editorial judgment, not the sentences or a replacement for the user's request. The writer works to [the craft standard](references/craft-standard.md) throughout and may use `creative-writing-craft` for a specific craft need and `dialogue`, `story-sense`, or `prose-style` to diagnose a problem. Use those diagnostic references only for the problem at hand; they do not add stages or separate reports. Before handoff the writer reads the complete story aloud or skeptically in mind, watching fulfillment of the promised experience, reader knowledge, scene consequences, dialogue uptake, and whether the ending earns its effect, and then makes the craft standard's own check on a page from the middle: more than one speed of sentence, speakers who could not trade lines, a room with an hour and a smell, the most important scene given the most space, and each reference character as much themselves at the end as at their entrance. A draft that fails that check is revised before anyone else reads it. Commit the first complete draft before review so later changes can be understood.

The outline round-robin is required for a new `[WP]`: after independent first reads, the writer, Codex reader, and Claude must receive one another's actual concerns and respond before drafting. Do not substitute a bundle of separate reviews or a coordinator summary for their exchange.

## Check prompt fidelity with Decisions

For new story generation, use the installed `$decisions` skill at two required gates: after the outline discussion and before drafting, then after the final whole-story discussion and resulting revisions, before art, rating, or handoff. Send the complete user request, all later directions, the complete outline or current prose, and every supplied original reference image. Never send only a synopsis, an outline's statement of intent, or a textual substitute for the images. Check the saved originals against the prompt's reference inventory first. Keep earlier checkpoint answers and editorial framing out of the evaluation, and keep checkpoint results away from cold readers until their first findings are recorded.

Follow [the checkpoint procedure and helper](references/fidelity-checkpoints.md). It asks one boolean question: does the outline or story deliver the story the [WP] asks for, as refined by explicit later user story directions and applicable image guidance? The defining premise and narrative promise must govern the actual story. “Yes” allows creative elaboration, unspecified choices and minor nonmaterial gaps; “No” means a material contradiction, omission or displacement of that promise. Do not invent motives, emotional arcs, morals or endings the user leaves open. Do not equate workflow metadata or art-edit directions with prose obligations, or incidental image details with binding features. Preserve the native confidence and Yes/No probabilities. A reliable **No** with confidence at least **0.80** triggers a fresh outline or story automatically; a reliable **Yes** at that confidence may proceed. Lower confidence, unresolved interpretation, conflicting evidence, refusal, missing inputs, or an incomplete or unavailable result means **halt production and ask the user one focused question**. Uncertainty takes precedence over restarting. After **two unsuccessful fresh starts at a stage**, halt and ask for direction rather than looping. Preserve failed work in Git, preserve the prompt and original images, and record the answer, confidence, action and restart count concisely in `notes.md`. Do not lower the confidence threshold, resample unchanged work, or add superficial prompt mentions to obtain a Yes.

This is a required fidelity gate alongside the existing independent readings and round-robin discussion. It does not certify literary quality, waive an explicit requirement, unlock canon, or authorize a whole-story replacement outside the active generation task. A substantial revision invalidates the affected checkpoint; a mechanical correction does not. While an uncertainty question is pending, do not continue drafting, revising, making art, or authorizing the next stage.

## Try the voice, then draft in scenes

After the outline gate proceeds, the writer drafts the story's opening two different ways, 250 to 400 words each. The two must differ in something real: the teller, the distance, the tense, the temper. They are not two polishings of one voice. The coordinator labels them 1 and 2 in an order of its choosing and gives both to the two outline readers with brief C, without saying which the writer prefers. Each reader says which they would keep reading and why, quoting the line that decided it. The writer chooses, having heard them, and may ask for a third attempt if neither has a pulse. Record the choice and the reason in `notes.md` and keep both openings in Git. This is one short exchange in sessions that already exist, and it is the cheapest point in the process at which to find the story's voice.

The writer then drafts **one scene at a time**, each from its brief, in the chosen voice. Drafting a whole story in a single pass is what compresses every scene to its plot function. Before each scene, reread its brief, the place sheet, and the notes on how its speakers talk. Note what each speaker knows going in and what each wants from the other, and write every line from that speaker's knowledge, not from the outline's. Give the long scene its planned share and let it run. When all scenes exist, write the summary passages that join them and read the whole.

Before anyone else reads it, the writer:

- Reads the complete story aloud or skeptically in mind, as described above.
- Goes through every exchange once from each listener's side. Could this person, having heard only what was said aloud, give that reply? Rewrite any line that opens on a pronoun or bare question the listener could not place, any “I know” or silence that closes a conversation the scene needed, and any account cut too short to retell. Confirm that the conversation named in the outline takes place aloud.
- Runs `python .agents/skills/story-create/scripts/prose_profile.py stories/<slug>/story.md --baseline stories` and reads its observations. The profile counts what is hard to hear in one's own draft: sentence and paragraph rhythm, the length of spoken turns, runs of bare exchange, bare acknowledgements, narrated non-answers, cut-off lines, short lines that point at something unnamed, the senses, stock mannerisms. It is a diagnostic. Do not write to its numbers; use it to find the pages to reread.
- Revises wherever the read-through or the profile shows the house manner, then commits the first complete draft.

Save the profile under `tmp/` for the line editor. Record only a sentence about it in `notes.md`.

## Read independently, then talk

For a new complete story or a substantial rewrite of a non-canon story, obtain a cold Codex reader subagent and a cold Claude reading. If Claude cannot be used, recruit another independent reader and say what changed. Use new readers who did not join the outline talk. Start the Codex reader fresh without the coordinator's prior conversation; paste the complete prompt and current prose into its assignment, or direct it to read only those two named files in the absolute worktree. Keep that same reader available for discussion. Give each reader only those inputs, without the outline, `notes.md`, prior reviews, the writer's explanation, or the other reader's response. If a supplied original image carries a binding detail or is needed to judge the story, provide that original to both readers as well.

For each independent Claude first read (outline, draft, or final), create a separate temporary directory under the active worktree's `tmp/` and start a new session of the normal authenticated CLI there. Use `--safe-mode` to disable automatically loaded `CLAUDE.md`, skills, plugins, and other customizations so repository or parent instructions do not contaminate the cold read; pin `--model claude-opus-5-5`, use `--tools ""`, `--strict-mcp-config`, and `--output-format json`. At the outline stage, send only the complete request and relevant originals for the initial understanding, then resume that session with the complete request and outline for its independent comparison. At draft and final stages, pipe the complete prompt with only the target whole prose through stdin. Use stdin rather than a Windows command argument for all review inputs. Set `TMPDIR`, `TMP`, and `TEMP` to the worktree's absolute `tmp/` path in the subprocess environment. When an original image is needed, copy it into that session's temporary directory and grant only the Read tool for that input. Keep each session ID and resume that session from the same directory with the same isolation flags for its discussion. Do not expose other files or feedback until each independent first reading is delivered. After both readings in a pair arrive, preserve short, attributed excerpts of their observations in their own words in `notes.md` before discussion; keep the coordinator's interpretation separate and do not rewrite those observations when opinions change.

Use the `claude.ai` subscription for these readings. Before the first call, clear inherited `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` from the Claude subprocess environment and check `claude auth status` there for `authMethod: claude.ai` and an active `subscriptionType`. If a call reports low API credit, correct the authentication and retry through the subscription; do not treat that billing error as Claude being unavailable.

Ask each reader what held attention or feeling, where the story lost them, what they believed happened, whether they experienced the prompt's distinctive promise, and which passages created those effects. Ask for the most consequential weakness even when the story is broadly successful. Have them distinguish explicit requirements, inferred promises, demonstrated problems, and taste preferences, and identify what is already working and should survive revision. A checklist, number, or PASS is not a substitute for a reader's account.

Each cold reader then gives a **craft reading** as a distinct second part of the same first report: prose, dialogue, reference characters, setting, shape, and the largest gap between this draft and one a good magazine editor would take. Give the assignment as brief D in [Reader and editor briefs](references/reader-briefs.md), pasted whole with the prompt and prose. Give the reader the supplied originals whenever a character or place in them appears in the story, since the reference question cannot be answered without them. The reader reports the experience first and the craft reading second, so that analysis does not replace the reaction. The continuing keeper also reads the current prose against the complete request; keep its feedback separate until the cold first readings are delivered.

Alongside the cold readings, obtain a **plain reading**. Start one more isolated Claude session with the same flags and give it brief G and the whole prose, and nothing else: no request, no outline, no originals. It numbers every spoken line, restates each in plain words with the earlier text that makes it clear, and lists the lines that have no such support, the replies a listener could not have given, the conversations cut off, and any account it cannot retell. Every other reader has seen the request, and a model resolves a missing reference without noticing that it has; this is the one reading placed to catch talk that depends on the plan. Its findings are demonstrated problems, not taste. The writer answers each by rewriting the line or restaging the moment, and may not answer by explaining what the line means, by adding narration that explains it, or by giving someone a new line that states the missing fact. Send the rewritten passages back to the same session in context. Record the number of findings and two or three of them, quoted, in `notes.md`.

Then convene an actual exchange. Relay each participant's own words, including the keeper's focused findings, to the others instead of replacing them with the coordinator's summary. Resume Claude's same session for follow-up; if that session is lost, record the break honestly. Let the writer explain intent, challenge a reading, and propose or reject repairs. Ask the readers and keeper to clarify their evidence, respond to each other, and test proposed changes. The coordinator must answer evidence-based objections against the complete request and the text. Do not settle disagreements by vote: majority approval cannot override an explicit user requirement, and the keeper's inferences do not become requirements merely by being recorded. An explanation of authorial intent can clarify a deliberate effect; it does not erase an effect a cold reader experienced. Keep reasoned dissent visible when the choice is to preserve the passage.

Address material failures to deliver the requested premise or experience before surface repairs; resolve them in the underlying scenes or structure. Then focus on the issues with the greatest effect on the story: attachment and desire, causal movement, scene purpose, point of view, dialogue, revelation, and ending. Use specific passages.

Craft findings are material, not polish to be traded away. When the craft readings show the collection's habits across the draft (one speed of sentence, interchangeable speakers, a reference character reduced to wardrobe or missing their temperament, a setting that is a category, more scenes than the length can hold), the writer revises at the scale of the cause: cutting or merging scenes to give the central one room, recasting a character's speech throughout, or moving the story to a place that acts on it. Patching in a description or lengthening a few sentences does not answer such a finding. Equally, do not let the discussion flatten the prose: when a reader reports confusion, restage the moment or cut what needed explaining instead of adding a clarifying clause or a spoken line that states the missing fact, and after each round of repairs the writer rereads the whole story for voice. Widen a revision when the cause is structural and the authorized scope permits; protect strong scenes and lines that a proposed fix would damage. If a repair repeatedly fails, reconsider the scene's job or the story's premise instead of polishing the same surface.

## Revise and remember

The writer revises `story.md` directly. Commit each substantial editorial round with a plain description of the change. Reassess material choices against the complete request with the keeper during revision, including later user directions, and continue the conversation with the same readers to find whether the reported effect has changed and what the revision may have broken. After a structural rewrite that changes the premise, scene order, point of view, or ending, obtain a fresh cold reading; returning readers already know the intended repair. After the last substantial revision, require a returning independent reader to read the entire current prose from beginning to end, not just the diff. Use contextual checks for local repairs; minor wording changes do not restart the outline, draft, and final stages or need a separate keeper review. Repeat discussion and revision while a material issue warrants action; an unmet explicit requirement cannot be waived as editorial taste. There is no required round count.

## Line edit

When the discussion above has settled the story's structure (its scenes, their order, the cast, the ending), and before the fresh final readings, one editor works through the prose. Do not line-edit scenes that may still be cut.

Start a new, isolated Claude session with the same isolation flags as a cold read, since a model other than the writer's hears the writer's habits best. If Claude is unavailable, use the Codex `story_editor` agent and record the substitution. The editor has seen no outline and no discussion. Give it brief E from [Reader and editor briefs](references/reader-briefs.md) with the whole current prose, the complete request, the supplied originals, the craft standard from “What this collection does when nobody decides otherwise” through “Setting”, and a fresh prose profile of this draft.

The editor returns an overall note on the story's voice at its best, passage-by-passage notes in story order, and a keep list. The writer owns the prose: work through the notes, rewrite in the story's own voice instead of pasting the editor's examples, and leave the keep list alone. Decline a note with a reason when it would cost the story something. If the editor reports that the draft cannot reach the standard without a structural change, return to the discussion above instead of polishing around it.

Record in `notes.md` the editor's overall note, the kinds of change made, two or three before-and-after excerpts, and anything declined. Commit the line-edited draft. The line edit is one pass. It is not a new round-robin and the editor does not become a final reader.

## Read the finished story with fresh eyes

When the coordinator, keeper, and returning readers have examined material promise objections and the text appears to deliver the request, recruit a new Codex reader and a new Claude session that saw neither the outline nor the draft conversation. Give each only the complete prompt, including later user directions, and current whole prose, independently, with the same cold-read isolation as above. Do not include the keeper's interpretation or findings. Ask each to describe the story they actually experienced before advising on it: what they cared about, where attention or belief broke, whether the prompt's distinctive promise was fulfilled, and the most consequential reason they would hesitate to publish. Invite scrutiny of choice, dialogue uptake, physical and temporal logic, and the ending, but do not turn that into a box-ticking report. Give the assignment as brief F in [Reader and editor briefs](references/reader-briefs.md): it asks each for the same quoted craft reading the cold readers gave, with the supplied originals in hand where a reference character or place appears, and for a plain answer on whether they would publish it. The aim is to expose assumptions the revision room has normalized, including a house manner the room has stopped hearing.

Save their first effects in `notes.md` before sharing opinions. Relay each fresh reader's actual finding to the writer and the other fresh reader; include the keeper's final comparison with the request, then bring in earlier readers where useful. Ask them to test one another's evidence, the writer's intent, and proposed repairs rather than vote on readiness. The writer repairs at the scale the cause needs. After a substantial change, both fresh final readers reread the complete story and discuss whether the reported effect changed or another problem appeared; after a local change, the reader whose material concern prompted it checks the passage in whole-story context and the coordinator judges it. If distinct concerns prompted the edit, each reporting reader checks their effect. A structural reworking also needs another genuinely fresh reading, because the discussing readers know the intended fix. Continue while a material concern warrants work, not for a preset number of turns or unanimous agreement. Record the reason for accepting or declining a well-supported objection; do not decline an explicit user requirement because the room prefers another story.

Use `notes.md` as a compact, dated memory for the next writer or editor. Keep both outline readers' short request-only understandings, their comparisons with the proposed story, their findings on the craft plan and what changed in answer, the voice trial's result, the coordinator's evidence for beginning the draft, prose readers' original effects and passages, the substance of their craft readings with the quoted sentences, the line edit's overall note and what it changed, each plain reading's findings and how they were answered, fresh final findings, the writer's responses, the coordinator's reasons for resolving objections, what changed, what rereading showed, and important unresolved disagreement. Include the outline and final Decisions checkpoint Yes/No answers, native confidence, action, useful evidence, restart counts, and any resulting user rulings. Keep raw checkpoint inputs and responses under `tmp/`. Include short before/after excerpts where Git's eventual merge style might otherwise hide the actual repair. Record decisions and lessons, not a full transcript, separate scorecard, or process report. A later round can correct an earlier diagnosis without erasing it.

The final whole-story round-robin is required for a new `[WP]`: the writer, fresh Codex reader, and fresh Claude session must respond to one another's actual first effects before the coordinator declares readiness. Recheck revisions at the scale described above; independent final reads alone do not complete the discussion.

Before handoff, confirm that prompt fulfillment has held through the final revisions; it is an ongoing test, not a late check after deciding the prose works. After the last change to the prose, run the plain reading once more, with a new session and brief G on the current whole text, and repair what it finds; repairs made late in the discussion are where lines that depend on the discussion get in. A repair confined to spoken lines and their staging does not reopen the round-robin. Then complete the final Decisions fidelity gate on the current whole prose after the final discussion and any resulting revisions; obey its restart or halt-and-question outcome before proceeding. Check relevant shared facts, names that might confuse readers, and internal time, space, knowledge, and object continuity. Resolve a canon conflict with the user; do not distort the story to satisfy a routine check. The coordinator states why the current version delivers the request and is ready, including the checkpoint outcome and any deliberate dissent, in `notes.md` and the pull request. That statement also answers the fresh readers' craft readings in their own terms: what they quoted as strongest and weakest, how the largest reported gap was closed or why it stands, and whether each reference character and the setting were recognized. Readiness needs both halves. The fidelity decision does not certify literary quality; that judgment still belongs to the editorial discussion. No SHA-256 pin or test suite certifies the story.

## Art and handoff

After the prose is ready, make the character and location references first for a new `[WP]` story unless the user asks for prose only. Identify the principal characters and the distinct locations important to the finished story before choosing a cover scene. Make reference sheets for those characters and location paintings for those places, including important settings that will not appear on the cover. A story with multiple important settings needs references for at least two distinct locations; different views or corrections of one place do not count as different locations. If the story takes place entirely in one setting, reference that setting without inventing another. The reference art should help a later artist recognize the story's characters and places without adding unsupported facts.

Make a separate character sheet for each principal character on a plain white background, as a landscape 1536×1024 WebP image by default, labeled with that character's name and the story title. Exactly one character belongs on each sheet; group or ensemble sheets do not satisfy this requirement. Multiple views, poses, and expressions of that same character belong together on its sheet. A multi-headed creature still counts as one character. Show the same identity and clothing across multiple full-body angles or poses and at least one face or expression study. Keep hands empty and omit props and separate object studies. Adapt the views to a nonhuman character if needed; a single scene portrait does not count as the sheet. Location paintings should establish each place's story-relevant geography, architecture, layout, and light from the prose and supplied references. Select existing collection sheets or paintings afresh for their relevance to this story, browse beyond familiar examples, and inspect their pixels before reuse. Record the chosen references' purpose briefly in notes.md and identify each input's role in the image prompt. The user's supplied scene references govern its light, atmosphere, and appearance; a brushwork example supplies brushwork. Default to the collection's Group of Seven inspired oil style unless the prompt asks otherwise; follow the [collection art direction](../../../universe/art.md).

Complete and inspect the full character and location reference set before generating the cover. Use the relevant accepted references as visual inputs for `title-image.jpg`, a 9:16 portrait JPEG at 864×1536, with the exact story title rendered once as part of the image rather than added afterward. Compose a novel cover that invites the right story. The cover follows the references; do not derive the reference set from the cover or limit it to the cover's subjects. Use the built-in image-generation skill for all art, attach relevant inspected originals from this story's `art/references/` and accepted references, and inspect the saved pixels for story fidelity, coherent objects and anatomy, consistent character identity across sheet panels and the cover, readable sheet labels and cover title, and consistent locations.

Put accepted character art in `art/characters/` and location paintings in `art/landscapes/` or `art/interiors/`, using descriptive names for the actual characters and places. Keep only accepted final images in the package, along with supplied originals in `art/references/`. Do not create paired “Original” and “Selected” location images or a new `art/selection-notes.json`. Correct a candidate when needed and keep drafts, superseded candidates, and rejected images under the worktree's `tmp/`; Git history preserves revisions to previously committed art. Further art is optional. Before the rating and draft pull request, confirm that the principal characters and important distinct locations have inspected references and the cover agrees with them. No gallery capture or site build is part of `[WP]`.

**Required AO3 rating gate:** After the final whole-story discussion and all resulting prose revisions are complete, and after any selected cover is finished and inspected, rate the finished prose and cover together under the shared General, Teen, Mature, or Explicit definitions. If the user requested prose only, rate the finished prose. Record the classification, a brief reason, and specific content notes in `ratings.md` before handoff or a draft pull request; do not assign General automatically. Reassess the rating if either the prose or cover changes afterward. For a substantial authorized revision, review the existing rating after the revised work is final and update it if needed.

Commit the story, notes, and selected art on the story branch. Push and open a draft pull request for the user to review. Do not merge it automatically. A localized prose edit that changes meaning, action, or voice needs one independent contextual read of the changed passage and a brief note, rather than a full discussion round. The coordinator rereads a purely mechanical correction. A substantive rewrite deserves fresh whole-story reading. Preserve legacy package layouts and prior reviews as history.
