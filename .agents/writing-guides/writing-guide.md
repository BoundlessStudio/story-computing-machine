# Short-fiction writing guide

## What the prose must accomplish

A short story should create a particular experience, not merely deliver a
premise and explain its ethical implications. Concentrate attention. Select a
small number of characters, places, objects, pressures, and images that can
gather meaning through use.

The speculative idea is not the story by itself. The story begins when that
idea meets a person with a specific desire, obligation, blind spot, or fear and
forces a choice that changes what follows.

## Scene movement

Enter a scene when a live pressure is already present. A viewpoint character
should want something concrete in the immediate situation, even if it is only
to finish dinner without being questioned, keep a machine running until dawn,
or avoid admitting why they came.

During the scene:

- introduce friction from another person's aim, the environment, a cost, a
  missing fact, or the character's own divided motives;
- let tactics change when the first approach fails;
- allow information to arrive through use, conflict, mistake, or consequence;
- end after something changes: leverage, knowledge, commitment, danger,
  relationship, permission, or the available choices.

Not every scene needs an argument or reversal. Quiet scenes still need movement
in attention, understanding, intimacy, or resolve.

## Viewpoint and interiority

Filter the page through the viewpoint character's attention. They do not notice
everything equally, and what they miss can matter.

- Render thoughts in the character's own mental vocabulary rather than in the
  neutral language of a case summary.
- Let thought associate, recoil, rationalize, fixate, and contradict itself.
- Do not translate every action into its psychological meaning.
- Avoid naming an emotion when the more revealing material is what the
  character does because of it.
- Let the character be wrong without the narration immediately correcting
  them.

Use free indirect style when close third person benefits from it: narration may
temporarily take on the character's diction and judgment without `she thought`
or `he realized`.

## Description

Description is selection under pressure, not inventory.

- Choose details that establish place, affect action, reveal the observer, or
  acquire meaning later.
- Prefer specific nouns and active verbs to stacks of adjectives.
- Include multiple senses when the viewpoint would notice them, not as a fixed
  checklist in every setting.
- Describe speculative objects through interaction and consequence before
  supplying taxonomy.
- Allow places to contain inconvenience, maintenance, clutter, wear, local
  adaptation, and evidence of lives beyond the plot.
- Do not give every paragraph a metaphor. Literal precision often provides the
  strongest image.

Worldbuilding should feel larger than the visible explanation. One used object,
routine compromise, or disputed term can imply more than a paragraph of lore.

## Exposition

Give the reader what is needed to understand the present pressure, at the point
when it becomes useful. Canon notes are private constraints, not a list of facts
to place on the page.

- Characters do not think encyclopedia entries about familiar things.
- Proper nouns do not substitute for lived detail.
- Explain a mechanism only as far as the scene's action, choice, or consequence
  requires.
- If history matters, present it through evidence, ritual, disagreement,
  damaged records, inherited habit, or material aftermath where possible.
- Summary and direct explanation are legitimate. Use them decisively when
  dramatization would be slower or less clear; do not apologize for telling.
- Do not repeat information in narration, dialogue, and thought.

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

Rhythm should follow attention and action.

- Mix short, medium, and long sentences for meaning, not manufactured variety.
- Use short sentences for actual turns, shocks, refusals, or emphasis; too many
  produce a mechanical dramatic beat.
- Use longer syntax to hold simultaneous perception, qualification, memory, or
  accumulating pressure.
- Vary paragraph length. A one-line paragraph spends emphasis and should not be
  the default container for every striking sentence.
- Read punctuation as timing. Commas, periods, colons, semicolons, parentheses,
  and dashes create different relationships; do not let the em dash do all the
  work.
- Repeat words or structures deliberately for echo, ritual, obsession, comedy,
  or escalation—not because the model has found a reusable cadence.

## Figurative language

Build images from the viewpoint character's body, work, culture, surroundings,
and private history. Avoid comparisons that could be transferred unchanged to
any story.

Do not explain an image after it lands. Do not stack two images where one is
enough. Recurring imagery can become motif without being revealed as a shared
cosmic mechanism.

Ration familiar synthetic patterns such as objects “looking like an
accusation,” rooms “contracting” around news, silence becoming a physical
substance, or feelings moving as creatures inside the body. None is forbidden;
frequency and predictability are the problem.

## Pacing and compression

Spend words in proportion to consequence, novelty, and emotional difficulty.

- Expand irreversible choices, discoveries the reader must interpret, and
  moments when relationships change.
- Compress repeated attempts, travel, routine procedure, and background history
  unless their accumulation is the experience.
- In action, preserve spatial and causal clarity. Alternate intention, motion,
  obstruction, and result rather than cataloguing moves.
- After a major beat, allow only the amount of reflection the viewpoint would
  genuinely have before the next demand arrives.

## Openings

Begin with a specific disturbance, desire, image, voice, or contradiction. The
opening should create orientation and curiosity without explaining the whole
premise.

Avoid default openings that announce weather, awaken a character into
confusion, summarize the world, or present a cryptic line whose only function
is to conceal information. Any of these can work when made necessary and
specific.

## Endings

End on the story's final meaningful change, image, action, or decision—not on a
summary of everything the reader should conclude.

- Resolve the central movement; secondary facts may remain alive and unsettled.
- Do not grant forgiveness, romance, institutional reform, or certainty merely
  to make the ending complete.
- Avoid an epigram that restates the theme in polished abstract language.
- A callback earns force only if its meaning has changed.
- Aftermath may be compressed, but avoid turning the final page into a ledger
  of outcomes unless that documentary voice belongs to the story.

## Restraint

The prose should not visibly perform intelligence in every line. Leave some
sentences plain. Leave some jokes unmade. Leave some implications for the
reader. Precision is not the same as constant cleverness, and clarity is not
the same as saying everything.
