# Shared-universe voice guide

<!-- Production guidance for prose. This file does not establish in-world facts. -->

## The shared voice

The universe does not have one fixed narrator. A comic romance, domestic ghost
story, mythic fantasy, and far-future tragedy should not sound interchangeable.
What they share is a way of looking:

- The strange is material and particular. Magic, technology, ritual, and
  anomaly are experienced through weight, temperature, labor, cost, habit, and
  consequence—not generic awe.
- Large ideas remain attached to ordinary lives. Cosmic scale may widen the
  frame, but the prose returns to what a person notices, wants, avoids, risks,
  misunderstands, or must do next.
- People are more complicated than the story's thesis. No character exists
  only to embody the correct moral position, explain a system, or receive a
  lesson.
- Warmth is earned through attention, not sentimentality. Let affection appear
  in memory, accommodation, irritation, work, private jokes, and specific acts
  of care.
- Moral seriousness comes from choices and consequences. Do not make every
  character speak in the language of consent policy, therapy, governance, or
  ethical analysis merely so the story's values remain unmistakable.
- The reader is trusted. Important meaning may remain unstated, disputed, or
  visible only in behavior.

## Voice is story-specific

Derive the narrator's voice from the prompt, genre, viewpoint character, and
social world. Establish a small number of dominant qualities and let them
shape syntax, attention, figurative language, and narrative distance.

A voice is not a list of adjectives pasted over neutral prose. If the viewpoint
belongs to a tired paramedic, the narration may sort danger quickly and notice
bad lifting technique. If it belongs to a court archivist, it may notice who is
named, omitted, or permitted to touch a record. These differences should alter
what enters the prose, not merely decorate the same observations.

Do not use a flamboyant narrator by default. Transparent prose can still be
specific because the viewpoint controls selection and emphasis.

## Narrative distance

Keep the reader at a deliberate distance from the viewpoint character.

- Close narration may borrow the character's vocabulary, biases, omissions,
  sentence fragments, and private associations without adding thought tags.
- More distant narration may summarize, contextualize, or move across time,
  but it should retain a definite attitude and rhythm.
- Move closer at moments of decision, bodily danger, shame, desire, or
  recognition. Move farther away to compress repetition, travel, routine, or
  history.
- Do not drift into another character's unspoken thoughts inside a limited
  viewpoint.
- Do not explain an emotion after action, dialogue, or sensory detail has
  already made it legible.

## Emotional register

Prefer emotional accuracy to emotional emphasis.

- Let characters misname, minimize, postpone, intellectualize, or redirect
  their feelings when that fits them.
- Use bodily response selectively. A tight throat, racing heart, held breath,
  clenched jaw, and twisting stomach are not interchangeable shorthand for
  every strong emotion.
- A concrete choice often carries more force than a declared feeling.
- Do not convert every painful beat into a polished insight. Understanding may
  arrive late, partially, or not at all.
- Permit mixed responses. Relief can coexist with resentment; love with
  boredom; grief with administrative nuisance; courage with vanity.

## Wit and lyricism

Wit belongs to characters and situations, not to every available sentence.
Some people are funny deliberately, some accidentally, some only with friends,
and some not at all. Under pressure, one character may sharpen while another
becomes literal, repetitive, silent, or clumsy.

Use lyrical language where attention genuinely intensifies. One exact image is
stronger than a chain of clever comparisons. Do not make every object seem to
judge the character, every room contract around a revelation, or every feeling
behave like a physical creature.

## Tonal range

The following can all belong in the same universe:

- playful adventure with real risk;
- domestic comedy with unresolved friction;
- quiet realism without a speculative event;
- horror grounded in agency and aftermath;
- mythic or epic fantasy that remains sensorially concrete;
- procedural or technical fiction that remains centered on people;
- tragedy without nihilism and hope without guaranteed repair.

Do not force darkness into light stories to prove depth. Do not neutralize dark
stories with compulsory reassurance. Match the ending's emotional temperature
to what the story has actually earned.
