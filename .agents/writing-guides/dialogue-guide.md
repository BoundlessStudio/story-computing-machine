# Dialogue guide

<!-- Production guidance for fictional dialogue. This file does not establish in-world facts. -->

## Core principle

Dialogue is action between people. Each speaker is trying to obtain, prevent,
test, conceal, repair, delay, or redefine something. The spoken subject and the
actual pressure do not need to match.

Every line has two possible justifications: what the author needs the scene to
accomplish and what makes this character speak now. A line supported only by
the first justification is usually exposition, interpretation, or instruction
wearing a character's voice.

Conversation should create the impression of life without transcribing all of
real speech. Remove routine throat-clearing unless it carries relationship,
status, mood, or misdirection. Preserve the evasions, dropped threads,
interruptions, nonanswers, and unequal attention that make speech human.

## Distinguish speakers from the inside out

Do not differentiate characters mainly through catchphrases, phonetic accents,
or different quantities of slang. Give each speaker a distinct conversational
logic shaped by:

- what they want from this exchange;
- what they refuse to say directly;
- what they assume everyone already knows;
- education, work, region, generation, and social group;
- relative status and perceived safety;
- typical tactic: joke, challenge, appease, lecture, bargain, deflect, confess,
  ask, withdraw, or change the subject;
- sentence habits: terse or expansive, concrete or abstract, exact or
  approximate, formal or casual;
- tolerance for silence, conflict, uncertainty, and embarrassment.

These traits are pressures, not gimmicks. A character's speech changes with
audience and circumstance. Someone may sound fluent at work and inarticulate
with a parent; charming when safe and painfully literal when afraid.

## Subtext and indirectness

Characters rarely state the complete emotional and moral meaning of a scene.
Let them protect themselves.

- Answer the dangerous part of a question with the safe part.
- Let an argument about an object, chore, route, meal, or technical detail
  carry a relationship conflict.
- Allow silence, a physical task, or a change of subject to function as a reply.
- Let characters hear only the portion they are ready to answer.
- Use direct speech when directness is itself a choice with a cost.

Subtext should be inferable, not merely obscure. The scene must provide enough
context—action, history, selection of detail, or later consequence—for the
reader to understand the pressure.

## Conflict without compulsory hostility

Dialogue gains energy from incompatible aims, not constant aggression. People
can love one another and still compete over pace, interpretation, privacy,
responsibility, or what happens next.

Track tactics, not just topics. Resistance should make a speaker press harder,
retreat, bargain, joke, lie, become precise, become vague, or stop speaking.

Do not make every exchange a smooth relay in which one speaker asks exactly the
question the other needs in order to explain the story. Speakers may:

- refuse the premise;
- answer too literally or too generally;
- interrupt at the wrong moment;
- repeat because they were not heard;
- concede a small point to protect a larger one;
- misunderstand sincerely;
- lie badly, tell a partial truth, or tell the truth for the wrong reason;
- leave before the conversation reaches its cleanest conclusion.

## Moral and technical language

The universe values consent, agency, personhood, and accountable power. Those
values should usually be visible in who asks, who is allowed to refuse, what a
choice costs, and what happens when a boundary is crossed.

Do not make characters recite the project's ethical framework. Explicit terms,
conditions, and boundaries belong in dialogue when the character, culture,
profession, ritual, contract, or immediate danger would naturally require
them. Elsewhere, dramatize the principle.

Likewise, characters do not explain familiar customs or systems to one another
for the reader's convenience. Exposition inside dialogue should be contested,
incomplete, strategically framed, or needed by someone present.

## Banter and humor

Ration polished banter. A run of perfectly timed replies makes characters sound
authored and alike, especially during fear, grief, attraction, or conflict.

- Give the best line to the person most likely to produce it, not automatically
  to the viewpoint character.
- Let jokes fail, arrive late, go unanswered, or expose the speaker.
- Do not use a quip to seal every emotional beat.
- Do not make all characters admire the same kind of cleverness.
- A repeated private joke can reveal relationship; generic snark cannot.

## Tags, beats, and bodies

Use `said` and `asked` freely. They are quieter than a parade of descriptive
speech verbs. Omit a tag when speaker order is unmistakable.

Action beats should do real work: alter distance, handle an object, delay an
answer, contradict the line, reveal attention, or change the power in the room.
Avoid stage business inserted only to break up quotation marks.

Do not attach an explanatory sentence to every line. If the words and action
already reveal that a character is angry, frightened, or lying, trust them.

## Speech texture

- Use contractions where the character naturally would.
- Prefer speakable phrasing to formally complete sentences.
- Keep fragments when they carry voice or pressure.
- Do not overuse names in direct address.
- Avoid eye dialect and dense phonetic spelling. Convey dialect through syntax,
  vocabulary, idiom, and rhythm with restraint.
- Profanity, formality, metaphor, and specialist vocabulary should vary by
  speaker rather than by the story's desired intensity.

## Example: policy-shaped dialogue versus lived dialogue

Policy-shaped:

> “You may decline my invitation, and I understand that accepting dinner does
> not imply any future romantic obligation.”

Lived, for a guarded but hopeful speaker:

> “Dinner. Just dinner.” He worried the loose thread on his cuff. “And if you
> hate it, I’ll develop a sudden respect for early trains.”

The second version does not weaken consent. It makes the boundary emerge from a
particular person, relationship, and moment. Another character might be blunt,
formal, ceremonial, or unable to joke; write that version instead.

## Dialogue density

Let important conversations breathe, but do not turn every disagreement into a
complete hearing. A scene may end on a withheld answer, an irreversible action,
or a change in what can be said. Summary is useful for repeated negotiations,
routine talk, or conversations whose exact wording is not the point.

After a reconciliation or moral decision, do not automatically let every
participant state the lesson, confirm the terms, and certify the meaning. End
when the relationship or available action has changed; the reader can carry the
remaining interpretation.
