# Dialogue review checklist

<!-- Operational review guidance. This file does not establish canon. -->

Use this after a complete draft and again when reviewing final prose. It is a
diagnostic pass, not a scoring rubric. Raise a formal finding only when a
pattern is repeated or materially weakens character, scene movement, clarity,
emotional force, or prompt fulfillment.

## 1. Map the conversation beneath the subject

For each important dialogue scene, identify:

- what the author needs the scene to accomplish;
- what each speaker wants from the other person now;
- what each speaker believes the conversation is about;
- what each refuses, fears, or fails to say;
- where resistance forces a change of tactic;
- what is different when the exchange ends.

If every participant wants the same orderly exchange, introduce a credible
difference in pace, premise, attention, risk, interpretation, or desired next
step. Do not manufacture hostility when quieter incompatibility is enough.

## 2. Test speaker distinction

Select six to twelve consecutive lines from each important exchange and hide
the tags. Ask whether sentence shape, diction, assumptions, tactics, and
relationship behavior provide evidence of who is speaking.

Failure of the tag-stripping exercise is not automatically a prose failure;
neutral lines sometimes need tags. Revise when substantial lines could be
swapped between characters without changing meaning, strategy, or emotional
risk.

Check especially whether every character:

- makes the same kind of joke;
- praises, refuses, apologizes, threatens, or curses in the same way;
- possesses the same degree of emotional and ethical vocabulary;
- becomes equally concise, insightful, and composed under pressure.

## 3. Separate character intention from author intention

Mark lines that exist mainly to explain canon, summarize the theme, certify the
story's values, or tell the reader how to judge the preceding action. For each,
ask why this speaker would say it now, to this listener, in these words.

Cut, move, contest, or embody a line when it has an authorial job but no
character motive. Exposition in speech should be needed, disputed,
strategically incomplete, or altered by the speaker's interests.

## 4. Audit moral and therapeutic abstraction

Search important exchanges for abstract terms such as consent, agency,
permission, boundaries, accountability, choice, harm, safety, closure, and
healing. Keep the exact term when profession, law, ritual, danger, culture, or
deliberate character directness requires it.

Elsewhere, ask whether the value can become legible through:

- who asks and who assumes;
- whether refusal changes what happens;
- a delayed answer or partial truth;
- an object returned, retained, repaired, or destroyed;
- a concrete cost accepted or displaced;
- a boundary crossed and a consequence that cannot be argued away.

Do not weaken a story's ethics. Move them from project vocabulary into
character behavior when explicit analysis is not natural to the scene.

## 5. Check subtext and imperfect listening

Look for exchanges that operate as clean question-and-answer relays. Where the
relationship supports it, consider whether a speaker would:

- answer the safe portion of a dangerous question;
- reject the premise instead of the conclusion;
- hear accusation where reassurance was intended;
- continue a task rather than reply;
- interrupt late, after the triggering words have already landed;
- return to an earlier point because they were not heard;
- leave the best answer unsaid.

Do not add filler, fragments, interruptions, or ellipses by quota. Naturalness
comes from unequal knowledge, attention, power, and desire—not random verbal
damage.

## 6. Ration banter and polished insight

Identify consecutive lines that all land as jokes, aphorisms, or elegant
rebuttals. Decide who actually owns that fluency and how the other characters
respond to it.

Let some jokes fail, arrive too late, conceal panic, irritate the listener, or
receive no acknowledgment. Under pressure, preserve character-specific failure:
one person may sharpen while another repeats, lectures, becomes literal, talks
around the point, or cannot finish.

## 7. Keep bodies and setting in the exchange

Action beats should alter distance, attention, timing, leverage, or the meaning
of a line. Remove generic gestures that merely separate quotation marks. Add a
beat when an object, task, silence, or environmental interruption can carry
meaning the dialogue is currently explaining.

Read the scene aloud. Listen for identical cadence, excessive direct address,
formal completeness, implausibly prompt replies, and emotional turns that have
no breathing room.

## 8. Stop at the earned ending

After the decisive choice, apology, refusal, or revelation, mark every line
that restates the lesson, confirms mutual understanding, negotiates complete
terms, or supplies reassurance. Keep only what this relationship and situation
still require.

A conversation may end because someone acts, leaves, withholds an answer,
changes the available future, or can finally say one smaller thing. It does not
need to close every interpretive loop.

## Finding threshold

When recording a dialogue finding, name the pattern, cite representative
locations, explain its effect on this story, and require an outcome rather than
a mechanical edit count. Preserve intentional ritual, legal, technical,
nonverbal, or highly stylized dialogue when the prompt and plan support it.
